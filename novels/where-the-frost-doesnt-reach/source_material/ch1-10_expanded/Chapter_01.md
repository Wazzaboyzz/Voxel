**Chapter One**

The funeral home in Amity Falls still smelled like carnations and floor
wax, the same as it had eight years ago, and Mara Voss stood in the
doorway wondering if that was a real memory or something she'd built
out of a hundred other funerals since. She had a structural engineer's
distrust of memory the way some people had a distrust of adjustable-rate
mortgages, on principle, from long professional experience with things
that looked stable until you actually put weight on them. Smell was the
worst offender. Smell didn't ask permission. It just arrived, fully
formed, and told you where you were before your eyes had caught up.

She stood there a beat too long, long enough that a man in an
ill-fitting black suit held the door for her with the patient, slightly
concerned expression reserved for people who might be about to faint or
about to bolt, and Mara made herself walk in before he had to decide
which.

Her grandmother had picked this dress out for her, once, for a cousin's
wedding when Mara was nineteen. Black, high collar, sleeves that ran a
little long, the fabric a heavier weave than the season really wanted.
She hadn't planned to wear it today. She'd packed for a weekend, three
days at most, a will to sign and a house to walk through and a flight
back to Chicago booked for Sunday evening before she'd even left
O'Hare, and then the days had kept multiplying the way days did in
places like this, until laundry became a decision instead of an
afterthought, and this was what came out of the drawer clean. She'd
stood in her grandmother's guest room that morning holding the dress up
against herself in a mirror gone spotted with age, and thought,
distantly, that Eleanor Voss would have had an opinion about the sleeves
being a decade out of fashion, and that she would never get to hear it,
and that the specific shape of that particular absence hadn't finished
arriving yet.

"You look like her," said a voice behind her, and Mara turned to find
Odette Reyes holding two paper cups of coffee like she'd been standing
there deciding whether to interrupt, or perhaps deciding how long she
could watch a grieving woman stand frozen in a doorway before it became
rude not to.

"Like who?"

"Your grandmother. When she was young." Odette handed her one of the
cups, the paper already gone soft and warm from her grip. Black, no
sugar, which meant Odette remembered exactly how Mara took her coffee
even after eight years, which was either kindness or the kind of
small-town memory that never let anyone leave cleanly, filed away along
with everyone's shoe size and their mother's maiden name and,
apparently, whatever else this town had decided not to volunteer. "I'm
sorry, Mara. She was a hard woman to like and an easy one to love.
That's rare."

"That's a very specific thing to say about someone at their own
funeral."

"I've had time to think about the wording." Odette's mouth did
something that wasn't quite a smile, more the ghost of the shape a
smile would make if the occasion allowed one. "People do, around
here."

Mara let that sit, turning the coffee cup slowly between her palms,
feeling the heat of it against skin that had gone cold somewhere between
the parking lot and the door. She'd forgotten this particular Amity
Falls habit, the way people said things a half-step sideways of what
they meant, so that you left every conversation feeling like you'd
missed a footnote printed in ink too pale to read. She used to think it
was just a small-town thing, everyone knowing everyone's business and
needing a code for it so the knowing didn't have to be said out loud in
the cereal aisle. Standing here now, eight years and a whole life
removed, she was starting to remember it felt like more than that. It
felt, she thought, like a town that had practiced keeping things vague
long enough that vagueness had become load-bearing, the way a wall stops
being decorative once enough of the ceiling starts depending on it.

She was halfway through the coffee, letting the room's low murmur wash
past her, half-recognized faces nodding condolences she didn't have the
energy to fully receive, when she saw him.

He was standing near the back, in a gray suit that fit him better than
anything he'd owned in his twenties, talking to a man Mara didn't
recognize, someone with the sun-weathered look of another orchard owner,
the kind of face this valley produced by the dozen. Same jaw. More
weather in the face than she remembered, the kind that came from years
outdoors rather than years indoors worrying, and a scar through one
eyebrow that hadn't been there before, thin and pale, the sort of thing
that had healed clean a long time ago. Caleb Reyes had always carried
himself like a man who trusted his own hands more than his own mouth,
shoulders set, weight balanced, the physical certainty of someone who'd
spent his whole life doing work that required his body to know things
before his brain finished catching up, and that hadn't changed. What
had changed was harder to name, something underneath the posture rather
than in it. A kind of stillness that used to read as ease, unhurried and
comfortable in his own skin, and now looked more like caution, a man
standing carefully inside a body he wasn't entirely sure he trusted.

Her own body reacted before her mind caught up, that specific,
humiliating lurch under the ribs that she'd spent eight years and one
entire relationship in Chicago trying to unlearn, the way you might try
to unlearn flinching from a specific sound. She'd told herself, driving
up from the city two days ago, that this exact moment wouldn't happen,
that eight years and several thousand hours of very expensive therapy
had surely done something to the reflex by now. Apparently not. She set
the coffee down on the nearest table so he wouldn't see her hand, and
pressed that same hand flat against her thigh, an old habit from job
sites, steady the shake before anyone official notices it.

He looked up. Their eyes met across the room the way they had a hundred
times, in the orchard rows at dusk, in the cab of his truck with the
radio turned low, in the kitchen of the house that used to be half his
and half a plan they'd never finished making, a mortgage they'd priced
out on the back of a receipt and never signed. For one full second,
maybe less, Mara let herself believe the old current was still there on
both sides, that whatever had gone wrong eight years ago might unwind
itself in this one look, the way a hard math problem sometimes resolves
the instant you stop staring directly at it.

Then she understood what she was actually seeing.

He looked at her the way you look at someone you're trying to place at
a grocery store. Not hostile. Not guarded, even, which might almost have
been easier to absorb, since guarded would have meant there was still
something underneath being protected. Just blank, the polite blank of a
stranger deciding whether it was worth the mild social cost of saying
excuse me, do I know you, and Mara stood there in her grandmother's
funeral in her cousin's old wedding dress and felt the floor tilt a
half degree under her, the particular vertigo of being unrecognized by
the one person whose recognition she'd built half her adult life around
avoiding. She had spent eight years arranging herself so that his
opinion of her couldn't reach her anymore. She had not, it turned out,
prepared for the possibility that there would be no opinion to arrange
herself against at all.

He excused himself from the man he'd been talking to, unhurried, and
crossed the room toward her, and for one absurd second Mara thought,
he's coming to say something cruel, he's finally going to say the
thing he never said when he left, eight years of imagined confrontations
rising up all at once, fully formed, ready to be delivered. Her chin
came up on instinct, the posture she used in client meetings when
someone was about to tell her a beam calculation was wrong and she
needed her face to say prove it before her mouth had to.

"I'm sorry," Caleb said, and his voice was exactly the same, low and
unhurried, the specific cadence of a man who'd never in his life felt
the need to fill a silence just because it existed, "I know I should
know you. Everyone keeps looking at me like I should know you."

The room did not tilt further because it had nowhere left to go. Mara
heard herself say, in a voice that came out steadier than she had any
right to expect, the engineer's voice, the one that reported load
calculations to nervous contractors, "Mara. Mara Voss. Thomas Voss's
granddaughter."

"Caleb Reyes." He held out his hand like they'd never touched it
before, which, evidently, as far as he was concerned, they hadn't. His
grip was the same, firm and unshowy, calloused across the palm in the
particular pattern of a man who worked with his hands every day of his
life and always had. That was the part that nearly undid her, standing
there in a room that smelled like carnations and floor wax, that his
hand still knew how to shake hers with exactly the same pressure even
though the rest of him apparently didn't know it had ever done so
before. "I run the orchard next to your grandfather's land. I'm sorry
for your loss. Eleanor was, everybody around here has a story about
her."

"You didn't leave," Mara said, before she could stop herself, the
sentence out of her mouth before her better judgment could intervene.

Something crossed his face, fast, gone before she could read it fully, a
flicker like a shadow passing over water. "Leave where?"

"Amity Falls. I thought." She stopped. Recalibrated, the way she would
on a job site when a measurement came back wrong twice in a row and the
problem wasn't the tape, it was the assumption underneath it. "I'm
sorry. It's been a long day."

"They usually are, these." He glanced past her toward the closed
casket at the front of the room, and something in his jaw tightened, the
first thing she recognized without effort, the way he'd always braced
before saying something that cost him, an old tell she'd have known
blindfolded. "Can I get you anything? Water, another coffee, that
one's gone cold."

"I'm fine."

"You don't look fine." He said it without judgment, a simple
observation, the kind of blunt kindness she remembered now in the shape
of it even if the man delivering it was, functionally, a stranger
wearing Caleb's face and Caleb's hands and none of Caleb's memory of
the eleven years before either of those things had stopped belonging to
her. "But that's none of my business. Sorry. Small towns make
everybody's business everybody's business, it's a bad habit."

He excused himself before she could answer, unhurried again, and Mara
stood by the cooling coffee and watched him walk back across the room to
the man he'd been talking to, apologizing to him too for the
interruption, easy and ordinary, and thought, with the kind of clarity
that only arrives after the worst possible moment to arrive in,
something is wrong here. Not sad. Funerals were sad, that was the whole
design of them, grief was the expected weather. Not strange in the
small-town way either, the way everyone here talked in half-sentences
and knew things about each other they had no earthly right to know.
Wrong. A specific, structural kind of wrong, the sensation she got
standing under a beam that looked fine to every eye in the room except
hers, the one trained specifically to notice the load wasn't being
carried the way it should be.

She'd spent eight years telling herself a story about the night she
left, about a man who'd looked her in the eye across this same kitchen
table she could still picture with total precision and told her he
needed time, and time had apparently turned into never calling, never
writing, into her packing a car in the dark because staying felt worse
than any version of leaving she could invent. She had built her whole
adult competence on top of that story, brick by careful brick.
Structural engineering, she'd told her therapist once, half joking, in
a session she still thought about more often than she'd admit, was just
a way to spend her life making sure things didn't fall down without
warning. She had wanted, very badly, to be the kind of person things
didn't fall down on again.

And the man who'd let her whole life fall down without warning, eight
years ago, in one unremarkable evening she'd replayed a thousand times
looking for the crack she must have missed, had just shaken her hand
like it was the first time either of them had ever touched it.

Priya found her twenty minutes later, by the coat rack, staring at
nothing, the coffee gone cold and untouched in her hand.

"You saw him," Priya said. Not a question.

"He didn't know me."

Priya's face did something complicated, grief and guilt and old loyalty
all arriving at once, visible for the length of a breath, and in the
eight years since Mara had last seen her, Deputy Priya Nair had
apparently gotten very good at controlling her face, because it smoothed
out again fast, back into the neutral, professional expression Mara
imagined she used on traffic stops. "Mara."

"Priya. What happened to him."

"It's complicated."

"I used to live here. I know what complicated means in this town."
Mara heard her own voice sharpen and didn't try to soften it, the way
she wouldn't have softened it on a job site either, not when a straight
answer mattered more than someone's comfort. "What happened to
Caleb."

Priya glanced toward the front of the room, toward the closed casket,
toward anywhere that wasn't Mara's face, the particular evasive
geography of someone deciding, in real time, exactly how much of the
truth a person could be handed on the worst day of their year. "Not
today. Please. Let your grandmother have today."

It was, Mara would think later, replaying the moment on nights when
sleep wouldn't come, the single most effective thing anyone could have
said to her, because it was true, and because it meant Priya knew
something worth withholding, something large enough that even a deputy
sworn to plain answers had decided a lie of omission was kinder than the
truth on this particular day, and because for the length of one funeral,
Mara Voss let herself be the kind of person who didn't push.

She had the rest of her life, it turned out, to regret how long she let
that promise hold.
