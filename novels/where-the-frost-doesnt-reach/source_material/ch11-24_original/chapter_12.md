# CHAPTER TWELVE

*Seven weeks after the funeral*

By the second week after the confrontation at Kell's house, the story going around Amity Falls was that Mara Voss had come back from the city with a chip on her shoulder and a grudge against a good man, and that grief over her grandmother had apparently curdled into something that made her drag Caleb Reyes, poor Caleb, still not himself, into wild accusations against Ambrose Kell of all people. Mara could trace the story's spread almost the way she'd trace a load path through a building, one small point of contact at a time, a comment here, a raised eyebrow there, until the whole structure of it stood upright without anyone ever quite admitting who'd poured the foundation. It was, she thought, a remarkably efficient piece of engineering in its own right, built entirely out of plausible deniability and the town's well-practiced fondness for tidy explanations.

Mara heard the shape of it first from the woman at the pharmacy counter, who went quiet and overly polite the second she recognized her, ringing up a bottle of aspirin with the studied concentration of someone determined not to make eye contact for even a second longer than the transaction required, and then more directly from her own cousin Denise, who called to ask, with careful concern, whether Mara was doing okay, really okay, because people were saying things.

"What things," Mara said, though she already knew, the phone pressed to her ear while she stood in her grandmother's kitchen doing dishes she didn't strictly need to do, the repetitive motion of it the only thing keeping her hands from shaking with something closer to anger than hurt.

"That you're not, you know. Handling grandma's death well. That you're seeing conspiracies." Denise's voice dropped lower, apologetic, the particular tone of someone relaying gossip she felt guilty for having heard at all. "I'm not saying I believe it. I just wanted you to hear it from me instead of the diner."

Odette, when Mara stopped by the store that afternoon, confirmed the rest of it without being asked, her hands moving through the motions of restocking a shelf with a steadiness that felt deliberate, a woman who had clearly decided somewhere in the last two weeks that usefulness was the only apology she had left to offer. "He's good," she said, restocking a shelf with more force than the cans strictly required, the metal clinking together with a sharpness that punctuated her sentence. "I watched him do this exact thing to a county inspector fifteen years ago who asked one too many questions about a drainage permit. Within a week the whole town believed the inspector had a drinking problem. He never did."

"So what do we do? We can't fight a story with just the truth if nobody wants to hear it." Mara felt the particular frustration of a person accustomed to solving problems that responded to evidence, standing now in front of a problem that clearly didn't care about evidence at all.

"You don't fight the story." Odette set the last can down and looked at Mara directly, the store's fluorescent lights catching the tired lines around her eyes in a way the softer light of the porch never had. "You get proof he can't spin. Something physical. Something that isn't a nineteen year old girl's shaky account or your grandfather's forty year old suspicions." Her eyes were steady, and something in them had shifted since the porch confession, a woman done apologizing and ready to be useful instead. "You're an engineer. You understand load and pressure and things that fail in predictable ways. That weather station out past the mill, the one that's supposedly reading conditions for the county extension office. Nobody's serviced it in years except Kell himself. He insisted on it personally, said it was a hobby."

Mara turned the thought over, feeling the particular satisfaction of a piece finally sliding into a puzzle she'd been assembling for weeks without a picture on the box to guide her.

Mara felt something click into place, the particular satisfaction of finding the actual crack instead of guessing at where one might be, a feeling she recognized from a hundred inspections where a stray detail suddenly explained everything else that hadn't been sitting right. "You think he's tampering with the readings."

"I think a man who's been faking weather emergencies for decades needs a way to make sure the official record backs up whatever story he's telling. I think if you got inside that station and found evidence it's been rigged to report frost warnings that don't match the actual temperature on the ground, you'd have something even Ambrose Kell can't talk his way around."

---

She told Caleb that evening, out at the fence line where they'd first really talked, the crooked apple tree throwing long shadows in the last of the light, its branches heavier now with fruit than they'd been the last time she'd stood here, the ordinary business of the orchard continuing underneath everything else that had happened. There was something almost absurd, she thought, in how normal the world insisted on looking even now, apples ripening on schedule, the same birds calling from the same trees, as if the valley itself hadn't noticed anything was wrong.

"It's breaking and entering," he said, not arguing so much as making sure she'd thought it through, his eyes steady on hers in a way that felt like a test she wanted to pass. "Or close enough to it that a friendly judge could call it that if Kell decides to press it."

"I know."

"And if you're wrong, if it's just an old station and nothing's been tampered with, we've handed him exactly the story he's already telling about us. Reckless. Paranoid. Willing to break the law to chase a theory."

"I know that too." Mara looked at him, the sun catching the scar through his eyebrow, and thought that a week ago this man hadn't known her name and now he was the person she trusted most to tell her honestly when a plan was bad. "I'm not asking you to come. This is mine to risk."

"No." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said most things that mattered. "You're not doing this alone. Not because I don't think you can handle it. Because I don't want you to have to."

Something in Mara's chest gave way, quietly, the specific give of a wall she hadn't realized she'd still been holding up, a small structural surrender that felt, for once, like relief instead of failure.

"Okay," she said. "Tonight. Before he thinks to check it himself."

---

The weather station sat on a low rise past the old mill, a modest metal box on a post, unremarkable enough that Mara understood immediately why nobody had thought twice about it in years, the kind of unassuming municipal fixture that blended so completely into the landscape it might as well have been invisible. Caleb held the flashlight low and cupped while Mara worked the housing open with a screwdriver from his truck, her hands steady despite the hammering in her chest, years of fine motor work under pressure paying off in exactly the way she'd hoped they would.

Inside, nestled among the ordinary sensors, was something that didn't belong, a small secondary unit wired directly into the reporting line, unmarked, clearly not county issue, its housing a slightly different shade of gray than everything surrounding it, the kind of mismatch that would mean nothing to a casual observer and everything to someone trained to notice when a system had been altered after the fact.

"There," Mara breathed, photographing it from every angle her phone allowed, her pulse loud in her own ears. "That's not standard equipment. That's an override."

"Can you tell what it does?"

"Not without taking it apart, and I'm not doing that here." She closed the housing carefully, making sure it looked untouched, easing the screws back into place with the same care she'd use resetting an access panel on an active site. "But I know someone who can. Priya still has evidence handling training, and more importantly, she has a brother who owes this town the truth after what Kell did to his reputation this week."

They were halfway back to the truck when headlights swept across the field, slow and deliberate, and Mara felt Caleb's hand close around her wrist, pulling her down into the tall grass beside the fence line before she'd consciously registered the danger, the cool, dew-damp grass closing over them both.

The car idled at the top of the rise for a long moment, engine running, lights sweeping once across the weather station and once, almost lazily, across the field where they crouched, and then it pulled away into the dark, unhurried, the way a man moves when he already knows exactly where to look and simply hasn't decided yet whether tonight is the night he acts on it. Mara held her breath the entire time, acutely aware of Caleb's shoulder pressed against hers, the two of them still and silent in a way that felt, absurdly, almost intimate given the circumstances. She counted the seconds the way she'd count them waiting for a load test to finish, each one stretching longer than it should, her whole body braced for a failure that never quite arrived.

"That was him," Caleb whispered, once the taillights had vanished around the bend.

"Or someone he sent." Mara's heart was still hammering, her hand still caught in his, and she found she didn't want to let go even once the danger had passed. "Either way. He knows somebody was out here tonight."

"Then we move fast." Caleb's voice had gone low and certain in the dark. "Tomorrow. We get this to Priya and Dev before Kell decides waiting to see what we do is more of a risk than doing something about it himself."
