# CHAPTER NINETEEN

The unease Mara had carried out of Odette's back office refused to settle, and by the following afternoon it had hardened into something she could no longer dismiss as simple exhaustion talking. She found herself parked across from the store on a pretext of picking up supplies, watching through the windshield as Odette locked the front door at an hour well before closing, a hand-lettered sign propped in the window reading back in twenty minutes in handwriting too careful to have been dashed off in a hurry.

Twenty minutes for what, on a Tuesday afternoon, with no obvious errand pulling her away.

Mara sat with the engine off, feeling faintly absurd, a grown woman conducting surveillance on a store owner who had done nothing this summer but bring her coffee and hold her hand through hard conversations. She almost started the truck twice, ready to abandon the whole exercise as an unworthy suspicion, and both times something held her in place, the same instinct that had caught hairline cracks in load-bearing walls before, refusing to let her simply drive away from a detail that didn't sit right.

She waited, telling herself she was being paranoid, that a woman who had spent forty years managing a store alone was entitled to step away without accounting for her time to anyone, and then watched Odette emerge from the alley behind the building fifteen minutes later, walking briskly toward the old feed store lot where a car Mara didn't recognize sat idling, windows tinted just enough to obscure whoever waited inside.

The conversation, from this distance, lasted less than three minutes. Odette leaned toward the driver's window, said something Mara couldn't begin to guess at, and straightened again with the particular stiffness of someone who had just agreed to something she didn't want to agree to. The car pulled away before Mara could get close enough to note so much as a partial plate, and Odette walked back into the store without once glancing toward the lot where Mara sat watching, her posture carefully, deliberately ordinary in a way that felt, to Mara's practiced eye, like its own kind of tell.

She told Caleb and Priya that evening, gathered again at the orchard house, and watched the same unease she'd been carrying settle visibly onto both their faces.

"Could be nothing," Caleb said, though his tone suggested he didn't believe it any more than Mara did. "Could be a supplier, a family matter, a dozen ordinary things."

"Could be," Priya agreed. "Except ordinary things don't usually require locking the front door and hand-lettering a sign instead of just stepping out back for five minutes like she does every other day."

Dev, sitting up now in the guest room bed with more color in his face than the day before, listened to the account with a growing frown. "I never worked with her directly, but I remember hearing things. Rumors that never quite solidified into anything specific. That Odette knew more about the old arrangements than she let on. That her family had some connection to the founding bargain that went back further than anyone talked about openly."

"She's never mentioned anything like that," Mara said, though even as she said it she found herself running back through every conversation she'd had with Odette this summer, searching for a detail she might have missed.

"People don't usually volunteer the parts of their family history that implicate them," Priya said grimly.

"I want to be careful here," Caleb said, and Mara heard the effort in his voice, a man trying to hold suspicion and fairness in the same hand without letting either one collapse. "Odette's been decent to all of us. More than decent. If we start treating every private conversation of hers like evidence, we're not that different from what this town's been doing to me for two years, deciding what's true about a person based on fear instead of proof."

"That's fair," Mara said. "I don't want to accuse her of anything. I want to understand what I actually saw, and right now I don't have an explanation that isn't at least a little bit alarming."

Dev shifted against his pillows, something thoughtful crossing his tired face. "There's a way to test it without confronting her directly. Something small enough that an innocent explanation would satisfy it easily, but something specific enough that a guilty one would be harder to talk around cleanly."

They decided, after considerable debate, to test the theory carefully rather than confront it directly, and it was Wren, of everyone, who offered the plan that felt least likely to blow up in their faces.

"Let me ask her something," Wren said. "Something small, something that would only matter if she actually has a connection to Kell or the old bargain. If she deflects too smoothly, or knows more than she should about something she has no ordinary reason to know, that tells us something without anyone having to accuse her outright."

"What would you ask," Mara said.

"The ledger. Dev said it's hidden somewhere Kell's never thought to look. If Odette's connected to any of this, even peripherally, she might know things about the old founding families' records that an ordinary shopkeeper wouldn't. I could ask her, casually, if she's ever heard of anything like a ledger from the original bargain. See how she reacts."

It felt thin, and dangerous, and none of them had a better idea, so the next morning Wren went to the store alone, under the guise of an ordinary shift, and reported back that evening with an account that only deepened the unease rather than resolving it.

"She went very quiet," Wren said. "Not surprised quiet. Careful quiet. Like she was deciding how much to say instead of trying to remember whether she knew anything at all. She said her grandmother used to mention old records the founding families kept, something about accounting for debts too large for any one family to carry alone, and then she changed the subject so fast it felt rehearsed."

"That's not proof of anything," Caleb said, though his voice carried little conviction.

"No," Mara agreed. "But it's not nothing either. And I don't like how much it's starting to look like something."

"There's more," Wren said, twisting her hands together in her lap. "She asked me, right after, whether any of you had mentioned a ledger specifically. Not records in general. The exact word. I told her no, that I'd just heard the term somewhere and gotten curious, and she looked at me for a long moment before she let it go. It felt like she was checking whether I actually knew something or was just fishing."

Mara felt the small hairs rise on her arms at that detail, the specific unease of watching a theory gain a piece of supporting evidence she hadn't wanted to find. "That's not an innocent reaction. An innocent person doesn't check whether you know a specific word. They just answer the question you actually asked."

Priya, quiet through most of the discussion, finally spoke, her voice careful, the deputy weighing evidence rather than letting suspicion run ahead of what they actually knew. "I want to be fair to her. She's been kind to all of us this summer, kinder than this town usually manages to be to outsiders. If we're wrong about her, I don't want to have treated her like a suspect for weeks over nothing more than a locked door and an old family rumor."

"I don't want that either," Mara said, meaning it, feeling the particular discomfort of holding two truths at once, gratitude for real kindness and suspicion about what that kindness might be covering. "But I also don't think we can afford to give anyone the benefit of the doubt right now, not with what's already happened to Caleb and Dev both. We watch. Carefully. We don't accuse her of anything until we actually know something, but we also don't stop watching just because watching feels unkind."

"What does watching actually look like," Wren asked. "I'm the one who has to be in that store with her most days. I need to know what I'm supposed to be noticing without turning every conversation into an audition for evidence."

"Small things," Mara said. "Who she talks to when she thinks no one's paying attention. Whether she volunteers information before you ask for it, or only answers exactly what's asked and nothing more. Whether her explanations for odd behavior come too quickly, too smoothly, like she's had time to prepare them in advance." She thought of every deposition she'd sat through as an expert witness, every contractor who'd over-explained a shortcut before anyone had accused them of taking one. "Guilty people over-explain. Innocent people usually just look confused about why you're asking."

"That's oddly comforting," Wren said. "Having something concrete to watch for instead of just a vague, awful feeling."

"Concrete is usually better than vague," Mara agreed. "Even when what it eventually confirms isn't something any of us want to be true."

Caleb reached for Mara's hand, some of the day's tension finally easing slightly now that a plan, however uncomfortable, had settled into place. "Careful is the only speed we've got left to move at right now. I'd rather be careful and wrong about Odette than careless and right too late about something worse."

Outside, the evening had gone still and heavy, the particular stillness that Mara had learned, this summer, to read as something other than peace, and she found herself hoping, without much confidence, that whatever Odette was hiding turned out to be smaller and more forgivable than the shape their fear had already begun building it into.

She thought, lying awake that night in the orchard house's quiet, of every kindness Odette had shown her this summer, the coffee, the steady counsel, the hand reaching across the store counter when Mara had needed to believe someone in this valley was simply, uncomplicatedly good. She didn't want the woman who'd offered those things to also be the woman quietly feeding Kell information, or worse, actively protecting whatever machinery he'd built to keep this valley compliant. But wanting a thing untrue had never once made it so, not in her professional life and not, she suspected, in this one either.

Caleb found her still awake near midnight, sitting on the porch step with a cup of tea gone cold beside her, and settled next to her without needing to ask what kept her up.

"You're thinking about Odette," he said.

"I keep hoping I'm wrong. I keep running the whole summer back through my head looking for a version where I misread all of it." Mara wrapped both hands around the cold mug, more for something to hold than any remaining warmth. "She held my hand the day I told her about leaving eight years ago. She's the one who first suggested the weather station might be rigged. It doesn't fit, and I hate that it doesn't fit, because a person who does one is supposed to be too decent to also do the other."

"Maybe she's decent and compromised both," Caleb said quietly. "People aren't always only one thing, even when we badly want them to be for our own convenience. Dev proved that already. Maybe Odette's proving it too, in whatever way we haven't fully understood yet."

Mara leaned into his shoulder, grateful for the steadiness of him, and let the question sit unresolved a little longer, understanding that some answers only revealed themselves on their own schedule, however much she wanted to force one into being tonight.
