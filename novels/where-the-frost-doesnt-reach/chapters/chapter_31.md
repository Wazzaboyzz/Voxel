# CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE

The photographs still existed, at least, backed up on three separate phones and one external drive Dev had insisted on the night they'd found the amplifier, and it was that stubborn redundancy that kept the whole effort from collapsing entirely in the days that followed the clerk's office break-in, a small mercy that Mara found herself grateful for more than once as the days wore on. She'd spent the first morning after the break-in half convinced they were starting from nothing again, standing in Priya's kitchen at six in the morning with a cup of coffee going cold in her hand, running through every worst version of what came next, and it was Dev, of all people, who'd talked her back off that ledge, reminding her flatly that a man who could destroy a filing cabinet couldn't touch a cloud backup he didn't know existed.

"You're doing the thing where you assume the worst has already happened," Dev told her, not unkindly, setting his own coffee down across from her. "I did that for two years straight after Kell first got his hooks into me. It's exhausting, and most of the time it isn't even accurate. He's good, but he's not omniscient. He didn't know about the drive. That matters."

Mara had appreciated the observation more than she'd let on at the time, filing it away alongside the small, growing list of things Dev had taught her about surviving a fight like this one, patience chief among them, the discipline of refusing to let a single bad morning collapse three weeks of careful work.

"He can destroy the physical unit," Dev said, spreading printouts across Priya's kitchen table for what felt like the tenth time that week, his voice rough with exhaustion, the bruise on his wrist finally fading to a dull yellow-green, "and he already has, the station's been swapped out for a clean one, I checked yesterday. But he can't un-take these photos, and he can't erase the serial number we already traced to a supplier out of state."

"A supplier who's not going to want his name anywhere near a manipulation scandal," Yusuf added, arms crossed by the window, watching the street with the wariness of a man who'd started checking his mirrors more often since his name went on that complaint, a habit Mara suspected he hadn't fully admitted to himself yet. "I called them yesterday. They confirmed the unit type, confirmed the shipping records show a purchase to a P.O. box registered under a name that traces back, after some digging, to a shell account Kell's used before for personal expenses he didn't want on the town books."

"So we still have a case," Mara said, though even as she said it she could hear how much thinner the ground under that sentence had gotten since the break-in, the words sounding more like a hope she was trying to talk herself into than a fact she was certain of.

"We still have a case. A slower one, and an uglier fight to get it heard now that the physical evidence is gone, but yes." Yusuf's eyes moved to her, something almost gentle in them despite the exhaustion carved into his face. "The county board doesn't like being told a filed complaint just vanished from their own archive. That story, on its own, without any of the stranger parts, is already making some of them nervous. Bureaucracies hate looking incompetent nearly as much as they hate looking corrupt."

"How long before they'll actually open something formal," Mara asked, already anticipating an answer she suspected she wouldn't love.

"Weeks, if we're lucky. Longer, if Kell's got anyone on the board he can lean on quietly." Yusuf rubbed a hand over his jaw, tired in a way that had settled into him permanently over the last month, worn down the way a man gets fighting a fight he'd expected to take years and was now being asked to win in a matter of days. "I'd rather tell you that honestly now than let you find out the hard way later. This isn't going to be fast, even with everything we've got."

Mara looked around Priya's kitchen, at the printouts and the phones and the small, stubborn machinery of people refusing to let a theft of paper become a theft of the truth itself, and felt something steady settle into her chest that had been missing since the break-in, the calm of watching a plan reassemble itself out of pieces that had briefly scattered.

"What do you need from me," she asked. "Beyond what I've already given you."

"Patience, mostly," Yusuf said. "And your grandfather's original file, if you're willing to let it be entered as supporting material. I know that's not a small thing to ask."

Mara thought of Thomas Voss's careful, cramped handwriting, the years he'd spent quietly documenting a wrong he'd never found the leverage to fix alone, and understood that he would have wanted exactly this, his private suspicion finally doing the public work he'd never been able to make it do while he was alive.

"Take it," she said. "He wrote it so somebody eventually would."

It took nine more days of careful, unglamorous work, phone calls, notarized statements, a second technician flown in at Dev's own expense to independently verify the supplier records, before the county board finally agreed to open a formal inquiry into both the vanished complaint and the underlying allegations against Ambrose Kell. Mara spent most of those nine days at her grandfather's desk, coordinating between Yusuf's careful procedural language and Dev's technical documentation, translating between the two the way she once translated between an architect's vision and a contractor's practical limits, learning as she went that building a case against a man like Kell required exactly the same patience as certifying a building safe, one verified detail at a time, no shortcuts, no assumptions left unchecked.

Priya handled the county board itself, sitting through two preliminary meetings with a patience Mara privately admired more than she said out loud, absorbing polite skepticism from men and women who'd known Kell for decades and clearly did not relish the idea of believing anything Priya was telling them. She came home from the second meeting quieter than usual, and when Mara asked how it had gone, she was honest about it in a way that made the whole fight feel more real than any of the paperwork had.

"One of them asked me, straight out, whether I thought my judgment might be compromised, given everything with Dev," Priya said, sitting heavily at the kitchen table, still in her uniform, her hat set down beside her with more care than the object required. "I understand why he asked it. I'd probably ask it too, in his position. It still felt like being hit."

"What did you say," Mara asked.

"That my judgment was exactly what led me to my own brother tied up in a silo, and that I'd trust it again in a heartbeat, because it was right." Priya managed something close to a smile, tired but real. "He didn't have much to say back to that."

Caleb, for his part, spent those same nine days doing what he did best, keeping the orchard running, keeping Toll fed and walked, keeping the ordinary machinery of daily life turning so the rest of them had somewhere steady to return to at the end of each long day of phone calls and paperwork, a contribution none of them named out loud but all of them quietly relied on.

"You could be doing more of this," Mara told him one evening, finding him in the kitchen making dinner for all of them without being asked, the smell of something simmering on the stove filling the whole downstairs. "The board meetings, the statements. You have as much standing as any of us."

"I know." He didn't look up from the pot he was stirring, his voice easy, unbothered. "But somebody has to make sure the rest of you eat something besides gas station coffee, and I'm better at this part than I am at sitting still through a county board meeting pretending I'm not furious the whole time. We each do the part we're actually good at. That's not less important just because it's quieter."

It was, Mara thought, exactly the kind of thing her grandfather would have said, and she filed the observation away without saying it out loud, not trusting her voice not to catch on it.

The formal inquiry, when it finally opened on the ninth day, felt smaller than Mara had expected, a single paragraph in the county board's public minutes, dry and procedural, giving no hint of the machinery of fear and theft it was quietly setting out to examine. She read it three times over at her grandfather's desk that evening, the paper trembling slightly in her hands, not from doubt this time but from something closer to relief, hard-won and unglamorous, watching a slow process finally produce something that couldn't simply be photographed off a doorstep and made to vanish.

"It's not much," Priya admitted, calling to tell her the news before it hit the public record. "One paragraph. No mention of Whitlock, no mention of anything we haven't already made airtight."

"It's enough," Mara said, and meant it. "It's a paragraph he can't make disappear a second time. That's more than we had a week ago."

She thought, setting the phone down, of every job site inspection she'd ever signed off on, that satisfaction of watching a structure finally pass, not because the danger was gone, but because the flaw had finally been named where everyone could see it, documented in a place no single person could quietly erase. It wasn't victory. It was something steadier than that, and for the first time in nine exhausting days, she let herself believe it might actually be enough to build the rest of the fight on.

She called Caleb before she called anyone else, the phone ringing only once before he picked up, as though he'd been waiting for exactly this news all evening without saying so.

"It opened," she said, no preamble, the way she'd learned to deliver both good news and bad these past two months. "Formal inquiry. It's real now, Caleb. On the record, where he can't quietly make it disappear."

There was a pause on the line, brief, and when he spoke again his voice had gone thick in a way she recognized, the sound of a man who had spent two years being spent by other people's decisions finally hearing that somebody official, somewhere, had agreed to look at the ledger honestly. "Nine days," he said. "Feels like it should feel bigger than it does."

"It will. Once it's actually moving instead of just opened." She leaned back in her grandfather's chair, letting herself, for a moment, simply breathe. "But tonight, I think we're allowed to feel like it's enough."

"Come home," he said, simple and certain, the word landing somewhere warm in her chest that had nothing to do with the inquiry at all. "I'll keep dinner warm. We can worry about the next nine days starting tomorrow."

She found, gathering her grandfather's file back into its folder, that she wanted exactly that more than she wanted to keep working tonight, and understood, closing the folder carefully, that learning to let a good day simply be a good day was its own kind of victory too, one nobody would ever enter into the county's official record, but one she intended to hold onto all the same.
