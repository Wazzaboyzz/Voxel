# CHAPTER FORTY-FIVE

They were married in the orchard on the first Saturday in April, under the crooked tree, third from the fence post, because Caleb had asked, quietly, one evening in February, whether it would be too strange to marry her in the exact place he'd apparently asked her twice before without remembering either time, and Mara had told him there was no place on this earth that felt less strange to say yes in.

The trees were just beginning to blossom, early and pale against a sky scrubbed clean by a spring that had, for the first time in decades, arrived on its own terms rather than one Kell had quietly engineered behind the scenes, and Mara stood at the end of a short aisle of folding chairs borrowed from the church basement, her grandmother's ring on her hand, a new one on the other, and watched Caleb watching her walk toward him with an expression that held nothing back at all.

Thomas had not lived to see it. He'd died in November, four days after the harvest festival, easy, in his sleep, the way Mara had stopped believing anyone in this town was allowed to go, and she had grieved him fully and completely through a long winter that had also, somehow, held space for everything else this valley was slowly learning how to become. She thought of him today anyway, standing at the fence line in the pale spring light, and found the grief sat easier now than it had in November, worn smooth by months of handling, a weight she'd learned to carry instead of one that still threatened to buckle her.

Odette gave a reading, something plain and unsentimental that suited her exactly, and Yusuf, sworn in permanently as council chair after a countywide vote that had surprised absolutely no one who'd watched him hold this valley together through its worst month in living memory, stood as Caleb's best man with a gruff, visible pride he made no effort to hide. Priya stood beside Mara instead, in a blue dress rather than her uniform, Dev seated in the second row beside their mother, out on the specific, closely supervised terms the valley's council had eventually settled on for a coerced violation weighed heavily in his favor, a debt of service to the community rather than exile from it, the first time in living memory that particular kind of mercy had been extended to anyone.

Wren sat near the front, nineteen turned twenty now, enrolled formally in the valley's Finder apprenticeship program under Mara's own reluctant, proud supervision, her nosebleeds fewer these days, her control steadier with every month that passed, and she caught Mara's eye during the vows with a fierce pride, understanding exactly how much this ordinary happiness had cost to arrive at.

"I don't have the whole record," Caleb said, when it came time for his vows, his voice steady despite the crowd, despite the sun, despite everything this town had put both of them through to reach this exact ordinary Saturday. "I used to think that meant something was permanently broken between us, some gap I owed you a full accounting for before I had any right to ask you to stay. I don't think that anymore. I think love was never really about the record. I think it's about choosing, over and over, with whatever you've actually got in front of you, and I've got you. I've got the parts I remember and the parts I don't, and every single one of them, remembered or not, points to the same true thing. I choose you. I'll keep choosing you, memory or none, for as long as you'll let me."

Mara's own vows came out steadier than she'd expected, given how much of the last eight months had asked of her, given how much of the last eight years had taught her to expect leaving instead of staying. "I spent eight years believing I was the kind of person people leave," she said, holding his hands, holding his gaze, holding, finally, without reservation, the whole shape of what she'd come back to this valley half afraid to want. "I built a whole life around never needing anyone to prove that belief wrong, because it felt safer than hoping. You proved it wrong anyway. Not by remembering everything. By choosing me twice, without full evidence either time, which taught me something I don't think I could have learned any other way. That staying was never about the proof. It was always just about the choosing."

They danced that evening under string lights strung between the orchard rows, the whole valley turned out for the reception in a way that would have felt, eight months ago, like exactly the kind of careful, watchful crowd Mara had spent her whole childhood learning to read for danger. It didn't feel that way tonight. Tonight it felt like a town that had finally, painfully, learned the cost of its own careful silences, and had decided, in the aftermath, to try being honest with itself instead.

Denise pressed a slice of pie into Mara's hands somewhere near sunset, insisting she hadn't actually eaten anything all day, and Yusuf gave a toast that ran twice as long as anyone expected and left half the reception laughing and the other half quietly wiping their eyes, stories about a man he'd argued with across a council table for eleven years and never once regretted the argument. Caleb's aunt danced with Priya's mother, the two of them trading careful, healing conversation about sons and nieces and everything this town had asked its families to carry alone for far too long. Toll wound her way between tables all evening, indulged shamelessly by every guest with a scrap to spare, until she finally settled, sated and content, at Wren's feet beneath the dessert table, keeping watch over the last of the wedding cake with proprietary devotion.

Mara found herself, more than once, simply standing still in the middle of it all, letting the noise and warmth of it wash over her, trying to memorize the exact shape of an evening she'd spent eight years telling herself she'd never get to have.

Late in the evening, after most of the guests had drifted toward their cars, headlights sweeping slow down the dirt track toward town, Wren found Mara near the dessert table, an old, water-stained envelope held carefully in both hands.

"I wanted to wait until tonight to show you this," Wren said, something careful and unsettled in her voice that cut through the last of the wedding's warm haze. "I finished going through the rest of the Frey attic boxes last week. Found this tucked inside an old ledger, way at the bottom, older than anything else in there."

Mara took the envelope, the paper brittle and yellowed under her fingers, and opened it carefully, a single folded letter inside, the handwriting old-fashioned but legible, addressed to no one Mara recognized, dated in a hand that named a year nearly a century past.

*If you are reading this, the bargain has been renewed again, and I am sorry for whatever it has already cost you to learn that much. I made the first agreement in good faith, believing it would end with my own life, the way any honest bargain should. I did not understand, until it was nearly too late, that something willing to trade a town's safety for a person's memory does not particularly care whose life it borrows next, so long as someone keeps agreeing to pay. I have tried, in the years since, to find a way to end it properly, to give the valley back what it took without costing anyone else what it has already cost me. I have not succeeded. I am leaving this letter in the hope that whoever finds it has more courage, or more time, or simply better luck than I ever did. My name was Adelaide, before he took even that from how this valley remembers me. If anyone still living reads this and wonders whether the woman who disappeared ever mattered enough to be looked for, I hope this proves, finally, that she was looking too, the whole time, for exactly the same ending you're chasing now.*

Mara read it twice, the warm noise of the reception fading around her, Caleb's hand finding the small of her back as he came to stand beside her, reading over her shoulder in silence.

"She wrote this before she disappeared," Mara said slowly, understanding arriving in careful, structural pieces, the way it always did for her, load by load. "She knew what she was risking. She left this anyway, hoping someone would eventually find it."

"There's something else," Wren said, her voice tight now, urgent in a way that cut through the last of the evening's softness. "I checked the county's old vital records this afternoon, before I brought this to you. There's a name in the 1980s drought file, the same crisis your grandfather investigated and never solved. A name that shouldn't still be walking around this valley today, not if the person attached to it aged the way ordinary people do." She met Mara's eyes, steady despite the fear underneath it. "I think Kell wasn't the only one who ever renewed something he shouldn't have. I think whoever did this to Ambrose Whitlock a hundred years ago, whoever taught him how to trade a town's safety for someone else's memory in the first place, might still be out there. Might still be renewing whatever they started, generation after generation, while the rest of us keep thinking each ending is finally the last one."

Mara looked out across the orchard, at the string lights swaying gently in the evening breeze, at Caleb's steady presence beside her, at Wren's fierce, frightened resolve, and felt the old, familiar hum of unfinished business settle back into her chest, less frightening this time, mostly because she understood now, with total certainty, exactly who she'd be facing whatever came next beside.

"Then I suppose," she said, folding the letter carefully back into its envelope, "we're not quite as finished here as we thought we were."

She tucked the letter into her dress pocket, and turned back toward the reception, toward her husband and her wedding and one more ordinary hour of a night that had earned, at least until morning, the right to simply be happy before the next thing came looking for them.

Caleb met her halfway across the grass, his jacket off now, sleeves rolled against the warm evening, and he didn't ask what Wren had shown her, not yet, reading correctly in her face that whatever it was could wait until tomorrow's ordinary daylight instead of tonight's.

"Dance with me," he said instead, holding out his hand, the string lights catching gold in his eyes the way they had every night since April, since the fence line, since two years and eight years and a whole valley's worth of careful, cowardly silence had finally, painfully given way to something honest. "Just this. Just tonight. Whatever's coming next can start tomorrow morning, same as everything else this valley's ever thrown at us."

Mara took his hand, and let him pull her close under the swaying lights, and thought, resting her head against his steady, familiar chest, that this, finally, was the thing her grandfather had been trying to tell her all along, in every careful, unfinished sentence he'd never quite managed to complete. Love was never the proof. It was only ever the choosing, over and over, for as long as two people were willing to keep doing it.

She intended to keep choosing this, letter or no letter, for as long as the valley would let her.
