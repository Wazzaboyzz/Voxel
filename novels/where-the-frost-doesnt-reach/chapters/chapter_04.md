# CHAPTER FOUR

The general store hadn't changed the arrangement of its shelves in eight years, which Mara found both comforting and slightly unnerving, the way it was comforting to find a beam exactly where the original drawings said it would be and unnerving to realize nobody had thought to update anything since. Canned goods on the left, dry goods center aisle, a cooler in back that hummed at a pitch just wrong enough to notice if you stood near it too long. The floor still dipped half an inch by the register, a settling flaw she used to step over without looking, and her feet found the adjustment automatically now, an old habit surfacing before she'd consciously registered the floor was even there to compensate for. Odette Reyes was behind the register, same as she'd been eight years ago, same as she'd probably been every day in between, ringing up a bag of flour for an elderly man Mara didn't recognize and greeting him by name without glancing at anything but his face.

"Mara Voss." Odette said it like she was trying the shape of it out loud, testing whether it still fit. "Heard you were back. Sorry about Eleanor. She was a hard woman to like and an easy one to love. That's rare."

"That's a very specific thing to say."

"I've had time to think about the wording." Odette rang up a can of coffee Mara hadn't quite decided to buy yet, sliding it across the counter with the unhurried efficiency of someone who'd been doing this exact job long enough to stop thinking about it. "You look tired. Staying at the house?"

"For now."

"Whole thing to sort through, a life like that. Sixty acres and a lifetime of things nobody's thrown away." Odette's eyes flicked toward the window, toward the orchard rows visible in the distance past the parking lot, the boundary line where Voss land gave way to Reyes land, and something in her expression shifted, careful, deliberate, exactly the kind of carefulness Mara would come to recognize over the following weeks as the entire town's default setting whenever a certain subject came anywhere near the surface. "You'll be seeing Caleb, I expect. Being neighbors and all."

"I saw him yesterday. At the funeral."

"Right." Odette's hands went still on the register for just a half second too long. "Right, of course you did." She resumed bagging the coffee with more attention than the task required, folding the top of the paper bag down twice when once would have done. "He's a good man, Caleb. Works harder than anybody I know. Keeps to himself more than he used to, but grief does that to people, and he's had his share."

Mara felt the sentence land wrong, a beam bearing weight it hadn't been rated for. "Grief over what?"

Odette's smile didn't waver, but something behind it did, a door closing so smoothly Mara almost missed the sound of the latch. "His father, of course. Passed a while back now. Left him the whole operation younger than he should have had to take it on." She pushed the bag across the counter. "You take care out there, Mara. This town's smaller than it looks, and slower to let go of things than people expect when they've been away a while."

An older woman in line behind Mara made a small, impatient sound, and Odette's attention shifted with practiced ease, the conversation folding itself closed the way Mara suspected most conversations in this store had been folding closed for years, a skill so well worn it barely registered as evasion anymore, just weather, just how things were done here.

It was, Mara would think later, walking back to her car with a bag of coffee she hadn't meant to buy, the first of what would become a very long list of sentences in this town that sounded like ordinary small talk and were not, in fact, ordinary at all.

---

She spent the afternoon in the orchard house doing the kind of quiet, physical work that required just enough attention to keep her from thinking too hard about anything else, clearing out a hall closet full of coats nobody would ever wear again, most of them her grandmother's, one or two that must have belonged to her grandfather from a decade before his health made coats and cold weather the same kind of risk. The closet smelled the way old houses did after enough decades of the same lives being lived inside them, cedar and dust and something fainter underneath that she couldn't name but that felt, unmistakably, like her grandmother, a smell she hadn't thought about once in eight years and now couldn't seem to stop noticing. She found, halfway through a box of scarves that smelled like cedar and mothballs, a photograph she didn't remember existing.

It was old, the color gone slightly orange the way film photos did after enough decades, and it showed her grandmother, young, maybe thirty, standing in front of this same house with a man Mara didn't immediately recognize, both of them laughing at something outside the frame. Her grandmother's hair was different then, longer, dark instead of the silver Mara had only ever known, and there was an ease in her posture that felt almost like looking at a stranger wearing a familiar face. On the back, in her grandmother's handwriting, faded but legible: T. and me, the year everything went wrong, and somehow also right.

Mara sat on the closet floor with the photograph for a long time, turning her grandfather's words from the day before over in her mind. You look like your grandmother did the year everything went wrong. She'd assumed grief, an old man's mind wandering toward whatever decade felt most present to him in his final weeks. Looking at the photograph now, at that same phrase written in her grandmother's own hand, she found the assumption harder to hold onto. Two people, independently, reaching for the exact same sentence to describe the exact same year, was not the kind of coincidence her professional instincts let her file away without comment.

She put the photograph in her bag instead of the donation box, and told herself it was sentiment, and mostly believed it.

The light was going gold and long by the time she stepped back outside for air, and she found herself walking, without quite deciding to, toward the fence line at the edge of the property, the one that marked the boundary between Voss land and Reyes land, the one she used to cross without thinking twice.

She didn't cross it today. She stood on her own side and looked at the orchard rows stretching away into the gold light, at a truck moving slowly between them, too far off to make out who was driving, and felt something she hadn't expected to feel this soon, a low, persistent hum of wrongness she couldn't yet put a name to, the unease of a person who spends her professional life reading structures for the hairline signs of a problem nobody else has noticed yet, standing in a town that was, she was beginning to suspect, held together by exactly that kind of invisible strain.

---

Her phone rang while she was still standing at the fence line, her firm's number lighting up the screen, and Mara answered it mostly out of reflex, the old muscle memory of a woman who had never once in six years let a call from the office go to voicemail during business hours.

"You're supposed to be on bereavement leave," said Renata, her project lead, without preamble, which was as close as Renata ever came to expressing concern. "Why does the Halvorsen site keep emailing me instead of you?"

"Because I forwarded everything before I left and apparently forgot to tell them I did." Mara pressed the phone between her shoulder and her ear, rubbing at her eyes with her free hand. "Tell them the load calculations on the west retaining wall are fine as submitted. I already flagged the soil report inconsistency in my notes, they just need to actually read past page one."

"You sound tired."

"I buried my grandmother two days ago, Renata."

"Right. Sorry." A pause, papers shuffling somewhere on the other end, the familiar sound of a life Mara had built specifically because it required no explanations, no history, no town remembering things about her that she'd rather forget. "How long are you thinking you'll be out there?"

Mara looked out at the orchard rows, at the fence posts leaning at angles she used to know without counting, and found she didn't actually have an answer that felt true. "I don't know yet. There's more to sort out than I expected."

"Take the time you need. The Halvorsen thing isn't going anywhere, and frankly neither is anything else on my desk this week." Renata's voice softened, just slightly, the closest thing to real warmth she allowed herself during work hours. "You okay, Mara? Really?"

It was such an ordinary question, and Mara found she had no ordinary answer for it, standing at a fence line in a town that had started feeling less like the place she'd grown up and more like a structure she hadn't been given the full blueprints for.

"I will be," she said, which wasn't quite a lie, and ended the call before Renata could ask anything else that required a truer answer.

She stood there a while longer after hanging up, watching the light fade gold to gray over trees that seemed, the longer she looked at them, to be holding something in their silence, and thought that for the first time in eight years, the city felt like the unreal place and this valley, whatever it actually was underneath its ordinary face, felt uncomfortably, undeniably real.

---

She didn't go straight back to the house. She drove the long way instead, out past the edge of town where the road curved along the river before looping back toward the Voss property, a route she used to take without thinking, back when driving nowhere in particular had been one of the things she and Caleb did together on slow evenings, radio low, windows down, no destination required.

The river looked the same. That was the strange part, the part she kept running into all day, how much of this place had simply continued existing exactly as she'd left it, waiting, patient as a held breath, for her to come back and notice all the ways it hadn't changed while she had. The water moved the same lazy brown-green it always had this time of year, low from the summer, the rocks along the bank exposed in the same pattern she remembered climbing across as a teenager, daring herself a little further each time until her mother found out and put an end to it. She used to know the name of every rock family, private, unserious names she and a handful of other kids had invented one long summer, and she found, driving past, that she could still recall two or three of them without trying, small useless artifacts of a childhood she'd assumed she'd filed away completely.

She parked at the old boat launch, empty this time of evening, and sat for a while with the engine off, listening to the river do what rivers did, indifferent to towns and bargains and whatever her grandfather was too afraid to say out loud in a room with a locked door and an oxygen tank. It occurred to her, sitting there, that she'd spent eight years building a life specifically designed to never require this kind of stillness, always another site visit, another deadline, another reason to keep moving so the questions she didn't want to answer never got enough silence around them to actually be heard.

The valley wasn't going to let her have that luxury. She could feel it already, some slow gravity reasserting itself, pulling her back toward stillness whether she wanted it or not, and she wasn't sure yet whether to resent it or be grateful for it, the same way she wasn't sure yet whether coming home had been an accident or the only honest decision she'd made in eight years.

She started the car again before the thought could go any further, and drove the rest of the way back to the house in the deepening dark, her headlights carving a narrow, familiar path through a town that had never stopped, she was beginning to understand, quietly watching her.
