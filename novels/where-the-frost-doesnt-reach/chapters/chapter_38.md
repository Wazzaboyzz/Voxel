# CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT

The call came at eleven at night, four days after the hearing, and Mara knew before she'd even fully answered it that whatever Del was about to say had been building toward this for longer than either of them had let themselves say out loud.

"He's had a bad turn," Del said, her voice carrying the same worn calm of someone who had delivered this exact kind of news too many times to let panic show in it anymore, though Mara could hear, underneath the calm, something rawer than the professional register usually allowed. "They've taken him to County General. I think you should come, Mara. I think you should come tonight, not tomorrow."

She was in the car within four minutes, Caleb answering on the second ring when she called him from the driveway, and he didn't ask a single unnecessary question, just said he'd meet her there, and the fact that he didn't need to be asked twice settled something in her chest even in the middle of the fear.

The hospital corridor outside her grandfather's room smelled like every hospital corridor she'd ever stood in, antiseptic and old coffee and something underneath both that she'd never been able to name, and Mara found Del sitting in the row of chairs outside, her hands wrapped around a paper cup she wasn't drinking from.

"They've stabilized him for now," Del said, standing as Mara approached. "His heart, mostly. It's been working harder than it should for longer than any of us wanted to admit. The doctor's talking in terms of days now instead of weeks. I'm sorry. I know that's not the way anybody wants to hear it, standing in a hallway at midnight."

Mara felt the words land somewhere she'd been carefully not looking, a part of her that had known, distantly, that this was coming, and had built an elaborate structure of denial around it anyway, the way she might have once ignored a hairline crack in a wall she didn't want to be told was load-bearing.

"Can I see him?"

"He's been asking for you since they brought him in. Kept trying to sit up and pull the oxygen mask off to say something, until they got him settled enough to stop fighting it." Del's eyes were tired, kind, carrying the plain weight of someone who did this work because she was good at holding other people's fear so they didn't have to hold all of it alone. "He said your name. And another one I didn't recognize. Adelaide. Does that mean anything to you?"

Mara felt something cold move through her, a name she'd never heard in all her grandfather's careful, decades-long circling of what he knew and wouldn't yet say. "No. It doesn't."

Caleb arrived as she was still standing with that name settling into her, and he didn't ask what was wrong, just put his hand at the small of her back, steady, present, the same gesture he'd made a hundred times this summer without either of them needing to name what it meant anymore.

Thomas was awake when they came in, propped up against pillows that seemed to swallow what was left of him, the oxygen mask pushed down around his neck against doctor's orders, his eyes finding Mara's the moment she crossed the threshold with a clarity that cut straight through the frailty of everything else.

"You came," he said, his voice thinner than it had been even a week ago, each word costing him something visible.

"Of course I came." Mara pulled the chair close, taking his hand, the skin thin and cool under hers. "Grandpa, Del said you asked for someone. Adelaide."

Something moved across her grandfather's face, old grief surfacing fast and total, and for a moment Mara thought he might not answer at all, might simply let the name dissolve back into whatever private history he'd carried it in for so long.

"Adelaide Whitlock," he said finally, and the room went very still, Caleb's hand tightening slightly on Mara's shoulder. "Ambrose's wife. Or the closest thing to a wife a man like that ever really had, back before he started wearing other names the way other men wear coats." Thomas's breath rattled, and he pressed on anyway, urgency overriding whatever caution had held him back for decades. "She figured it out. Long before I did. Long before anyone else in this valley even suspected what he was. And when she tried to tell people, tried to warn the town the way I eventually tried and failed to warn it myself, she disappeared. Everyone assumed she'd simply left him. Left the valley. A woman running from an unhappy marriage, that was the story, and it was such an ordinary story that nobody thought to question it."

"You don't think that's what happened," Mara said, though it wasn't really a question.

"I think a man who's spent over a century learning exactly how to make an inconvenient truth disappear had a great deal of practice, by the time his own wife started asking questions he couldn't afford answered." Thomas's grip on her hand tightened, weak but insistent. "I never had proof. I chased it for a decade and never found her, not a grave, not a record of her leaving on any train or bus this county ever ran, nothing. But I know the ground here better than anyone left alive, Mara, and I know there are places on that mill property nobody's ever thought to look closely at, places a man might put something he needed permanently out of sight."

Caleb had gone very still beside her, and Mara understood, without needing to look at him, that he was thinking the same thing she was, the silo, the grain elevator, all the places this town had already learned to associate with things it needed hidden.

"Why tell me this now," Mara asked, gently, though the question felt almost cruel given the answer both of them already understood.

"Because I'm not going to get another chance to, and because Wren's the only other person in this valley with a gift sharp enough to find what I never could, and I need you to protect her better than this town ever protected me." Thomas's eyes, exhausted but fiercely clear, held hers. "Kell already knows how close you're getting. A man who's buried one inconvenient woman before won't hesitate long over a second, if he decides the investigation is finally cornering him enough to need it."

The machines beside his bed beeped their steady, indifferent rhythm, and somewhere down the hall a nurse's cart rattled past, ordinary hospital sounds continuing exactly as they would have on any other night, entirely unaware of the shape of what had just been handed across this bed.

"I'll protect her," Mara said, her voice steadier than she felt. "I promise you that."

"I know you will." Thomas's hand relaxed slightly in hers, some visible weight lifting now that the words were finally out of him after however many years he'd carried them alone. "I'm sorry I waited this long to tell you. I told myself I was protecting you, the way I told myself the same thing about Caleb, all those years ago, and I was wrong both times, and I'd like to not die having made that same mistake a third time."

"You're not going to die tonight," Mara said, though even as she said it she heard how much she wanted it to be true rather than how certain she actually was.

"No," Thomas agreed, something almost like his old dry humor surfacing faintly through the exhaustion. "Probably not tonight. But soon enough that I'd rather spend what's left telling you the truth than managing it the way I've managed everything else in this valley for sixty years." His eyes moved to Caleb, holding there a moment. "Take care of her. Not because she needs taking care of. Because she's spent her whole life being the one who holds everyone else up, and she deserves someone who notices when the weight gets too heavy for her to carry alone."

"I will," Caleb said, quiet, certain, and Mara felt the promise land in the room like something solid finally set down after being carried too long.

Del appeared in the doorway a few minutes later, gentle but firm, the kind of firmness of someone whose job required interrupting even the most important conversations when a patient's body demanded rest, and Mara kissed her grandfather's forehead before she let herself be ushered out, the name Adelaide Whitlock settling into her chest alongside grief and fear and a fierce, urgent need to warn Wren before Kell decided the investigation had finally left him with nothing left to lose.

---

They didn't speak much on the drive back, the dark highway unspooling ahead of them in a way that felt, after the hospital's fluorescent hush, almost too ordinary to hold everything that had just happened inside it. Caleb drove with one hand loose on the wheel, the other resting on the console between them, close enough that Mara could have taken it if she'd wanted to, and after a few miles she did, lacing her fingers through his without either of them saying anything about it.

"Adelaide Whitlock," Caleb said eventually, testing the shape of the name out loud, the way he might test a board for rot before trusting his full weight to it. "A hundred years, and nobody ever thought to ask what happened to his wife."

"People don't ask questions about things they've already decided have an ordinary answer." Mara watched the dark fields slide past the window, the same fields she'd grown up watching from this same stretch of road a thousand times before, and found she couldn't look at any of it now without wondering what else this valley had spent a century deciding not to notice. "A woman leaves her husband. Nobody investigates that. It's the most ordinary story there is, right up until you learn the man she left was capable of doing considerably worse than being left."

"You think she's really out there. At the mill."

"I think my grandfather spent forty years of his life chasing a hunch he never had proof for, and I've learned this summer that his hunches have an uncomfortable habit of turning out to be true." Mara's grip on Caleb's hand tightened slightly. "I think we need to talk to Wren tomorrow, carefully, about whether she'd be willing to try reading something of the mill's, or near it, the same controlled way she read the knife. Not tonight. Tonight she should get one more night of not knowing how much danger she might actually be standing in."

"And Kell? If he's watching the investigation the way we think he is, watching to see how close it's actually getting."

"Then we move faster than he expects us to, and more carefully than he thinks we're capable of." Mara let out a long breath, the exhaustion of the night finally catching up to her all at once, grief and fear and the strange, aching tenderness of the promise she'd made at her grandfather's bedside all sitting heavy in her chest at the same time. "I keep thinking about what he said. About me being the one who holds everyone else up. I don't know if that's a compliment or an accusation."

"Maybe it's just true," Caleb said quietly. "Doesn't have to be either one on its own. It can just be a fact about you, the way it's a fact that apples need a certain number of cold nights before they'll set fruit properly. Doesn't make the cold a punishment. It's just what the tree needs to do the thing it's built to do."

Mara looked over at him, at the tired, steady line of his profile in the dashboard light, and felt something in her chest ease slightly despite everything the night had handed her. "That's a strange metaphor for eleven-thirty at night."

"I've had a lot of time this summer to think about what things need to hold weight properly." He glanced at her, brief, warm, before returning his eyes to the road. "Turns out I learned it from you, mostly, without either of us noticing when it happened."

They drove the rest of the way in a quiet that felt less like exhaustion now and more like two people bracing together for whatever the valley still had left to ask of them, and Mara thought, watching the dark orchard rows finally rise up along the roadside as they neared home, that her grandfather had given her more than a name and a warning tonight. He'd given her, without quite meaning to, one more reason to believe that whatever came next, she wouldn't be facing it as the only person left holding up the weight.

Priya called before either of them had managed more than a few hours of sleep, her voice carrying the same clipped briskness of someone who had already decided how the morning needed to go before making the call.

"Del phoned me an hour ago," she said. "Told me what Thomas said. Mara, I need you to let me loop in the independent investigator on this, carefully, before we do anything with Wren. If there's any chance Adelaide Whitlock is actually out at that mill property, that's not just our fight anymore. That's evidence in an active investigation, and if we go poking around it ourselves first, we risk contaminating exactly the kind of proof that could finally end this properly."

"I hear you," Mara said, rubbing at her eyes, the hospital's fluorescent hush still sitting heavy behind them. "But Wren still needs to know what she might be walking toward, investigator or not. I'm not going to let her find out by accident the way she almost did with your brother."

"Agreed. Tell her today. Just tell her carefully, and tell her we're bringing in official channels before anyone goes near that property in person." Priya's voice softened slightly. "How's Thomas?"

"Stable. For now." Mara felt the word catch in her throat, the quiet ache of describing someone she loved in the clinical, temporary language hospitals required. "Del says days, not weeks."

"I'm sorry, Mara." A pause, the sound of Priya deciding whether to say the next thing. "For what it's worth, whatever he just handed you, he chose to hand it to you instead of taking it to his grave the way this town usually does. That's not nothing. That's the opposite of everything Kell's spent a century doing."

Mara held onto that thought after she'd hung up, sitting on the edge of Caleb's bed in the grey early light, watching him move around the room getting ready for a day neither of them had expected to start this way. Her grandfather had spent decades being careful, the same careful this whole valley specialized in, and last night, with days instead of weeks left to spend it any way he chose, he'd decided honesty was worth more than caution after all.

She intended to spend whatever was left of this fight making sure that decision had actually meant something.
