# CHAPTER EIGHT

The county records office closed at five, which meant Mara and Caleb had forty minutes to find a boundary dispute filed under water rights from nineteen ninety-one, in a building that smelled like dust and toner and hadn't updated its filing system since roughly the era in question. The fluorescent lights overhead buzzed at a pitch just below hearing, the kind of sound Mara had learned to tune out in a hundred municipal offices over the years, load calculations and permit reviews conducted under the same institutional hum no matter which county she was standing in, and there was something almost comforting in the familiarity of it now, a small, ordinary anchor in a day that had stopped being ordinary somewhere around the second sentence out of her grandfather's mouth.

"Your grandmother," Caleb said, pulling a drawer that stuck halfway open, the metal shrieking in protest loud enough that the clerk at the front desk glanced up without much interest and went back to her crossword, "sounds like she'd have made a decent smuggler."

"She once hid a bottle of my grandfather's good whiskey inside a hollowed-out family Bible for two years so he'd quit before his doctor made him. He found out at her funeral. Well." Mara stopped, correcting herself with the same reflexive precision she'd bring to a mislabeled blueprint, a habit so deeply ingrained she barely noticed herself doing it anymore. "Not her funeral. His toast at somebody else's wedding, when he told the story like it was funny."

"Is it funny?"

"It's funnier now than it was when he found the Bible." She found the drawer marked 1991 WATER and started walking her fingers through the folders, each one labeled in a court clerk's efficient, bored handwriting, decades of ordinary disputes about irrigation easements and fence lines nobody remembered arguing about anymore, the kind of paperwork that accumulated in every small town the way sediment accumulated in a riverbed, layer on uneventful layer. "Here. Voss-Reyes boundary, water rights amendment."

The folder was thin, thinner than a real dispute would generate, which told her before she'd even opened it that her grandmother had padded a real filing with something that didn't belong there. A genuine boundary dispute, she knew from professional experience, generated surveys and counter-surveys, letters from lawyers, at least one aggrieved handwritten note from a neighbor convinced the other side had moved a fence post six inches in the night out of spite. This folder had none of that heft. It had exactly enough paper to look plausible to anyone who wasn't looking closely, three or four pages of genuine surveyor's language, a signature, a stamped date, and Mara had spent her whole career being paid specifically to look closely at things other people assumed were fine.

Behind the actual survey pages, in a plain manila sleeve gone soft and yellow at the corners, was a second, older document, typewritten, the kind of typewriter font with the crooked lowercase e that dated it decades back, the letter always sitting a hair lower than it should, a mechanical flaw some machine had carried through thousands of pages until somebody finally retired it and the flaw along with it. Mara's hands had gone very still, the particular stillness of someone about to read something they suspected would change the shape of everything after it.

Caleb read over her shoulder, close enough that she felt the words land as much as saw them, his breath slowing beside her ear, the warmth of him at her back a strange, grounding presence against the cold precision of what they were about to find.

**Case notes, private. T. Voss. Re: E. Frey, missing time, 1982-1986.**

Four pages. A timeline, dense with dates and small, precise annotations in her grandfather's younger handwriting, steadier than the trembling script she'd grown used to at Rosewood, the hand of a man still confident enough in his own body to write in a straight line without effort. A list of names, most crossed out with a single decisive line, one circled twice, the pen pressed hard enough into the paper to leave a faint indent she could feel under her fingertip even now, forty years later, like the paper itself remembered the force of whatever certainty had driven the pen down that hard.

**A. Kell? Weather anomalies match his rotation schedule. No proof. Council will not authorize further inquiry.**

"Kell," Caleb said, his voice gone flat and careful, the particular flatness of a man deliberately keeping something enormous from cracking his voice open. "Ambrose Kell. He's council chair now."

"He was on the council in 1986 too, apparently." Mara ran her finger down the list of crossed-out names above the circled one, the ordinary weight of a small town's suspicion recorded in ink that had gone brown with age, most of the names unfamiliar to her, family names she didn't recognize or half recognized from the general store's bulletin board. Then Caleb went very still beside her, and she stopped moving her finger and looked up.

"That's my grandfather's name," he said quietly, pointing to a line three names above Kell's, crossed out with the same decisive stroke as all the others. "Silas Reyes. My grandfather was on that list."

"Crossed out," Mara said carefully, watching his face for what this information was doing to him. "Which means my grandfather looked at him and ruled him out. It doesn't mean anything happened to him."

"Or it means my grandfather was close enough to whatever was going on that Thomas thought to check him at all." Caleb's jaw had tightened, the old bracing tell she'd learned to recognize. "Nobody in my family's ever mentioned this. Not once. My father never said a word about his own father being investigated for something like this, and my father wasn't a man who kept secrets easily. If he didn't know, that means whatever happened here, it stayed buried even from the people it touched."

"Or your grandfather knew, and decided the same thing Odette's been deciding for you. That not knowing was safer." Mara closed the folder gently over the list, feeling the weight of exactly how many families in this valley might be quietly sitting on some version of this same silence, layered generation over generation like sediment nobody ever bothered to dig back through. "This town doesn't just have one secret, Caleb. It has a whole architecture of them, load-bearing walls built out of things nobody agreed to say out loud, and I think we've only just found the first crack in it."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Outside the window, the town's one traffic light cycled through its colors for nobody, the street empty at this hour, the kind of empty that in a normal town would just mean small and quiet and in this one, Mara was starting to understand, might mean something was being kept out of sight on purpose. She found herself scanning the parking lot through the window without quite deciding to, an old habit from job sites where you learned to notice who was watching a building and from where, cataloguing exits and sightlines the way she'd once catalogued them for insurance inspections, and saw nothing, which didn't make the feeling of being watched go away so much as relocate it somewhere she couldn't point to, a low hum of unease sitting just under her ribs.

"He's had forty years to get better at hiding it," Caleb said finally, setting the folder down gently on the table between them, as though it might bruise if handled roughly, his fingers lingering on the edge of it a beat longer than necessary.

"Or he's had forty years to convince himself he's not doing anything wrong." Mara closed the folder, careful with the old paper, the particular care of someone handling evidence she already suspected mattered more than either of them yet understood. "My grandfather said the alternative to admitting the bargain could be abused was worse, to the town. That's not a monster's logic, Caleb. That's the logic of somebody who thinks the ends justify it. Those people are harder to catch because they don't act guilty. They act certain." She'd seen the type before, in her own field, engineers who'd cut a corner once and gotten away with it and then spent the rest of their careers quietly, confidently cutting the same corner, certain their judgment had simply been better than the code required.

"You sound like you've met a few."

"I build things for a living. You'd be amazed how many structural failures start with somebody being absolutely certain they were right." She slid the folder into her bag, glancing at the clock mounted above the clerk's window, its second hand ticking with the particular deliberate slowness of institutional time, each second stretched thin by the weight of what they'd just read. Twelve minutes to closing. "We need to know what Kell's been doing that would run up a debt this size. My grandfather said weather anomalies matched his rotation schedule in eighty-six. If it's happening again, there'll be a pattern. Rain where there shouldn't be. Frost that missed a field it should've hit."

She watched something shift in Caleb's face as the sentence landed, a slow-dawning recognition that had nothing to do with memory and everything to do with a man doing his own version of the math she'd just described, running his own life back through a lens he hadn't had access to an hour ago, his jaw working slightly as though physically chewing on the implication before he could bring himself to say it out loud.

"There's a pattern," he said slowly, the words coming out careful, deliberate, a man testing the weight of a bridge before committing his full weight to crossing it. "I know there's a pattern. I've been standing inside it for two years and calling it good luck."
