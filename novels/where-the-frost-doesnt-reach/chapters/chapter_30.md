# CHAPTER THIRTY

The harvest crew had gone home for the evening, and the orchard had that particular hush that came after a full day of ladders and crates and shouted instructions across the rows, the kind of quiet that felt earned rather than empty, the air still faintly sweet with the smell of bruised windfall apples nobody had gotten around to clearing. Mara had stayed later than she meant to, walking the rows alone in the last of the light, still turning over everything Odette had said in the kitchen the night before, the word Whitlock sitting in her chest like a stone she hadn't found anywhere to set down.

She found Caleb in the equipment barn, oiling a hinge on a gate that had been sticking for years, apparently, a small ordinary task he'd taken up because his hands needed something to do while his mind worked through the county board's timeline for reviewing Yusuf's complaint. The single work lamp threw long shadows across the barn's dirt floor, and Toll lay curled in a patch of straw near the door, one ear twitching at every sound from outside. The barn smelled the way it always did, machine oil and old hay and the particular dust that settled into wood this old, and Mara stood in the doorway a moment just watching him work, the unhurried competence of his hands a small, steadying thing after a week that had offered her very little to hold onto.

She thought, watching him, of how much had changed since the last time she'd stood in this exact doorway, eight years ago, seventeen and certain of very little except that she wanted to leave this valley and never look back. She hadn't imagined, then, that she'd end up back here watching a man oil a gate hinge with the same careful attention he'd once used to fix her grandmother's porch step, or that the sight of it would undo her more thoroughly than anything Kell had said or done all summer.

"They're saying two more weeks before it goes to a full hearing," she said, leaning against the doorframe, watching him work with the particular pleasure of watching someone competent at something ordinary. "Dev's technician confirmed the amplifier's not standard county issue. It's something."

"It's something." Caleb set the oil can down, wiping his hands on a rag that had seen better decades, and turned to face her fully, leaning back against the workbench. "Kell's still walking around like none of it touches him."

"He's good at that. We knew that going in." She stepped further into the barn, the smell of hay and machine oil and dust settling over her like something familiar despite everything that had changed since the last time she'd stood in a place exactly like it, eight years ago, laughing about nothing in particular. "I keep thinking about what my grandfather said. About watching myself. I don't think he meant it as a metaphor."

"You scared?"

"Some." She admitted it plainly, no performance in it, the honesty coming easier now than it had at the start of the summer. "Mostly I'm tired of being scared and doing the thing anyway. I don't know if that's brave or just what's left when you run out of other options."

Caleb crossed the space between them, slow, unhurried, the way he did most things that mattered, and stopped close enough that she had to tip her head back slightly to hold his gaze, close enough that she could smell the machine oil on his hands and, underneath it, something warmer, something that had started to feel, over these past weeks, distinctly like him. Toll lifted her head briefly at the shift in the room, then settled back down, entirely unbothered, as though she'd long ago decided this particular development was overdue.

"I think," he said, "that's the most honest definition of brave I've ever heard."

"You're supposed to disagree with me. Tell me I'm being too hard on myself."

"I don't remember how to lie to you convincingly. Apparently I never did." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "Mara. I've been trying to figure out the right time to do this for about three weeks now, and I don't think there's ever going to be a right time in this particular life, so I'm just going to."

He kissed her before she could ask what he meant, gentle at first, careful, like a question he was still waiting to see her answer, his hand coming up to rest against the side of her face with a tenderness that made her chest ache, and when she answered it, hand curling into his shirt, pulling him closer instead of pulling away, something in him seemed to finally, fully exhale, eight weeks of caution dissolving into something that had been building since a funeral home doorway. The work lamp buzzed faintly overhead, the only sound in the barn besides their own breathing and, somewhere outside, an owl calling once and going quiet.

It wasn't the kiss of two people meeting for the first time, and it wasn't quite the kiss of two people picking back up where they'd left off either. It was something new, built out of both, careful and certain at once, and when they finally broke apart, foreheads still touching, both of them breathing a little unevenly, Mara found herself laughing, quiet and disbelieving, the sound catching in her throat somewhere between joy and disbelief.

"What," Caleb said, low, close, his thumb still tracing slow, absent circles against her cheekbone.

"Nothing. Just. Eight years I spent telling myself a story about you deciding I wasn't worth staying for. I don't think I ever once imagined getting to do that again and having it feel exactly like coming home."

"I don't have the old memory to compare it to." His hand found the side of her face, thumb brushing along her cheekbone. "But if this is what coming home felt like the first time, I understand why you spent eight years missing it."

She kissed him again, softer this time, unhurried, and for a few long minutes the county board and Ambrose Kell and the whole tangled weight of the valley's secrets receded to something distant and manageable, two people finally allowing themselves the thing they'd both wanted since the moment their eyes met across a room full of carnations and floor wax. His hand found her waist, steady, and hers found the collar of his shirt, and for a little while the barn held nothing more complicated in it than the two of them and a dog too old and too settled to care.

"I keep waiting for this to feel complicated," she admitted, her forehead still against his, her voice barely above a whisper. "Given everything else this summer's been. It doesn't. It's the one uncomplicated thing in the whole mess."

"Maybe that's how you know it's real," he said. "Everything else about this summer has had a catch to it somewhere. This is the first thing that hasn't."

She wanted to stay in that thought a while longer, wanted to let herself believe, just for the length of one quiet evening in a barn that smelled like hay and machine oil, that they'd earned a single uncomplicated thing after everything else the valley had cost them both. She was still deciding whether to say any of that out loud when the moment ended for her, decided by someone else's urgency instead of her own readiness to let it go.

Priya's voice, calling Caleb's name from somewhere near the house, arrived like cold water, sharp and sudden enough that they both startled, though neither of them fully stepped back.

"In here," he called back, not moving away from Mara, though his hand slid down to find hers, steady, present, grounding them both before whatever was coming had a chance to.

Priya appeared in the barn doorway a moment later, out of breath, her flashlight beam bobbing wildly across the dirt floor, her expression telling them both, instantly, that whatever she'd come to say was not going to let this moment last much longer. Toll lifted her head again at the sudden noise, ears pricking forward, some old instinct reading the room faster than any of the humans in it.

"County clerk's office called," she said, chest heaving. "Somebody broke in tonight. Filing cabinets tossed, records missing. They think it happened sometime in the last two hours." Her eyes moved between them, grim, the flashlight beam finally steadying on the ground between all three of them. "Guess which complaint just vanished from the system along with half the water rights archive it was filed next to."

Mara felt the warmth of the last ten minutes drain out of her all at once, replaced by the familiar, cold clarity of a structure she'd trusted suddenly showing a crack she hadn't accounted for, the specific vertigo of watching solid ground reveal itself to be far less solid than she'd believed. She was aware, distantly, of Caleb's hand still in hers, tightening slightly, the only thing keeping the floor from feeling like it had actually moved under her.

"He destroyed the evidence," she said.

"Worse." Priya's jaw was tight, her voice shaking slightly with something that might have been fury or exhaustion or both at once. "He destroyed it in a way that makes it look like it never existed, which means if this goes to a hearing now, it's Yusuf's word and some photographs against a council chair with forty years of goodwill, and Kell's already got half the town believing you two are the ones who can't be trusted."

"How much time do we have," Caleb asked, his voice gone flat and level, the particular steadiness Mara had come to recognize as the sound of him setting aside everything he'd rather be feeling in order to deal with what was actually in front of him.

"I don't know. Depends how fast he moves now that he knows we're getting close." Priya rubbed a hand over her face, the exhaustion of the last two months finally showing plainly through the cracks in her own composure. "I'm sorry. I know I just walked in on something. I wouldn't have if it could wait."

"You didn't walk in on anything that isn't still here in the morning," Mara said, and found, to her own surprise, that she meant it, the barn's ordinary dark settling back around the three of them, Toll's steady breathing the only sound for a moment before the night's real business reasserted itself. She looked at Caleb, at the same face that had just been close enough to kiss, now set into the particular grimness of a man readying himself to fight for something he'd only just let himself want out loud.

They walked back toward the house together, the three of them, Priya's flashlight beam cutting a narrow path through the dark orchard rows, apples hanging heavy and unbothered on either side of them, indifferent as ever to the human business unfolding underneath the branches. Mara felt Caleb's hand find hers again somewhere along the way, wordless, steadying, the two of them falling into step without either one having to ask.

"Who do we call first," she asked, once they'd reached the porch light, her mind already sorting the night's damage into something she could actually work with, the same instinct that had once made her a good engineer now turned toward an entirely different kind of structural failure. "Yusuf, or Dev, or the clerk's office itself, see if there's any record of who signed in tonight."

"All three," Priya said, already dialing. "In that order. And Mara." She paused at the porch steps, her flashlight beam dropping to the ground between them, something almost gentle slipping through the exhaustion in her voice. "For what it's worth, I am sorry about the timing. You two looked happy in there. First time I've seen either of you look like that all summer."

"We'll take the rest of it up again once this is handled," Caleb said, his hand tightening briefly around Mara's before letting go, already turning toward the work in front of them. "It'll keep. Kell's been waiting forty years to be caught. He can wait one more night to watch us come after him again."
