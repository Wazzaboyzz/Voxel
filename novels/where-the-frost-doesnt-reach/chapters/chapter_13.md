# CHAPTER THIRTEEN

"I need to show you something," Wren said, before Caleb had even gotten the door fully open, her voice pitched too high, too fast, the words tumbling over each other in a way that made Toll's low whine climb into something closer to a bark. "I know it's late. I know I should have called first. I couldn't call first, I didn't trust myself to explain it right on the phone, and I needed you to actually see it instead of just hearing me try to describe it badly."

"Wren." Mara came up behind Caleb, taking in the girl's pale face, the way her hands wouldn't stay still in her jacket pockets, the brittleness of someone holding herself together through willpower alone. "Breathe. Tell us what happened."

"I was biking home past your orchard, the back way, past the old equipment barn." Wren's eyes flicked to Caleb, uncertain, apologetic. "I didn't mean to go near it. I wasn't even thinking about it, I was just riding, and then I was off my bike and standing at the door before I'd decided to stop, and I don't know how to explain what happened next except that it felt like touching a live wire that had been buried for years, still humming underneath the dirt."

Caleb went very still. "The old barn. The one my dad used for equipment storage before I converted half of it."

"That one." Wren nodded, fast, jerky. "I put my hand on the door. Just to steady myself, I think, I was dizzy, everything felt too loud all at once. And then I saw something. Not clearly. Like a photograph left in water too long, all the edges bleeding into each other. But I saw you." Her eyes found Caleb's, and something in her face carried genuine fear now, not for herself, Mara realized, but for him. "I saw you standing in that barn, except you looked different, thinner in the face, your hair longer than it is now. And there was someone else there. I couldn't see who. Just a shape, a person standing close to you, and then everything went white and hot and it felt like something being taken, physically, out of the air between the two of you."

Mara's stomach dropped, the cold clarity of a structural fault finally showing itself after weeks of quiet, hairline warning signs. "You think you touched the edge of a toll. His toll."

"I don't know what else it could have been." Wren's voice cracked. "I've felt small things before, an object with an old memory attached, nothing this big, nothing that felt like it was still happening instead of already finished. It didn't feel like the past, Mara. It felt like something still open. Like a wound that never actually closed all the way."

Caleb was already moving toward his boots by the door, his jaw set in the hard line Mara had learned meant he'd stopped being afraid long enough to get angry instead, a shift she'd come to recognize as his version of courage. "Show me."

"Caleb, it's nine thirty at night." Mara caught his arm, not to stop him, only to steady the pace of it. "If there's something real in that barn, we go carefully. All three of us, together, and Priya knows where we are before we walk in."

He held her gaze for a moment, something fierce and grateful passing between them, the shorthand two people build when one of them keeps remembering, out loud, to be careful on the other's behalf. "Priya first. Then the barn."

While they waited for her to arrive, Mara made Wren sit at the kitchen table with a glass of water she didn't drink, watching the girl's hands shake against the glass with a fine, persistent tremor that hadn't been there when Mara first opened the door. "How long have you had your gift," she asked, gentler than the question probably needed to be, mostly to give Wren something steady to hold onto besides her own fear.

"Two years, officially. Longer than that, unofficially, if you count the years before anyone had a name for what kept happening to me." Wren's laugh came out thin, humorless. "I used to think I was just unusually good at finding things people lost. Car keys. A neighbor's missing cat. Nobody thought much of it until I found something I shouldn't have been able to find, a ring my aunt swore she'd never owned, buried in a box she swore she'd never packed. That's when my mother finally sat me down and told me what I actually was."

"That's a hard thing to learn about yourself all at once."

"It's a hard thing to learn you're the kind of person this valley watches carefully instead of just, I don't know, likes." Wren wrapped both hands around the glass, steadying it. "Finders redirect tolls. That's the taboo everyone whispers about but never explains properly to a fifteen-year-old who just wants to know why finding her aunt's ring made her whole family go quiet at dinner for a week." She looked up at Mara, something raw and searching in her face. "Is that what I did tonight? Redirect something?"

"No." Mara said it firmly, certain of at least this much. "You touched the edge of something someone else did. That's not the same thing at all, and I don't want you carrying guilt for a crime that isn't yours."

Priya arrived twenty minutes later, off duty but dressed like she wasn't, a flashlight already in hand, and the four of them, Toll included, crossed the dark orchard rows together, the September night gone cool and still around them, no wind at all, which somehow made the silence feel heavier rather than more peaceful.

The barn sat at the far edge of the property, a squat, weathered structure Mara had walked past a dozen times this summer without once feeling anything unusual about it, ordinary corrugated siding, a sliding door on a track that had needed oiling for years judging by the way it groaned when Caleb finally hauled it open. Inside, old equipment sat under tarps gone grey with dust, the smell of motor oil and hay and time settled into every surface, nothing about it visually different from any equipment barn on any orchard in the valley.

"Here," Wren said, stopping just inside the threshold, her hand hovering near the doorframe without quite touching it, her whole body braced like she expected the wood itself to bite her. "Right here. This is where I felt it."

Mara crouched to examine the frame, running her fingers along the old wood the way she might inspect a suspect beam, looking for anything a normal inspection would catch, water damage, rot, anything ordinary that might explain a structural anomaly without requiring the supernatural at all. She found nothing unusual, no marks, no discoloration, nothing but decades of ordinary wear.

"There's nothing here I can see," she said. "Nothing physical."

"It's not physical the way you're looking for it." Wren's voice had steadied slightly, some of her Finder instinct apparently overriding the fear now that she'd committed to being here. "Can I try again? Carefully this time, with all of you actually here instead of me alone on a dark road."

Priya's hand found Wren's shoulder, gentle but firm. "You don't have to. Nobody's making you touch that door twice tonight."

"I want to. If it's connected to what's happening to Caleb, I want to know it properly instead of leaving you all with half a description I panicked my way through." Wren took a slow breath, steadying herself the way Mara had seen her do once before, at the funeral reception, a girl deliberately gathering herself before doing something that frightened her. "Just be ready. I don't know what it'll look like from the outside."

She reached out and pressed her palm flat against the doorframe, deliberate this time instead of accidental, and Mara watched her whole body go rigid almost immediately, her breath catching, her eyes fluttering closed.

"I see it again," Wren said, her voice gone distant, threaded through with a strange, underwater quality that made the hair on Mara's arms rise. "Caleb. Standing right here. And the other person, closer now, I can almost see. Dark hair. Young. They're saying something to him, I can't hear the words, but I can feel what it costs. It's costing him something enormous, right here, right in this doorway, and he doesn't even know it's happening."

"Dev," Priya breathed, the name arriving before she seemed to fully decide to say it out loud, dread coloring every syllable.

"I don't know who it is." Wren's brow furrowed, straining. "It's too fragmented. But whoever it is, they're not doing this out of malice. I can feel that much clearly. There's so much guilt in it, so much reluctance, like watching someone forced to do something they hate every single second they're doing it."

Caleb's hand found the doorframe beside Wren's, not touching her, just bracing himself against the same wood, his face pale in the lantern light Priya had set on an overturned crate. "How many times. Can you tell how many times this happened, right here."

Wren was quiet for a long moment, her whole body trembling now with visible effort. "Many," she said finally. "This spot. This exact spot, over and over, like a wound reopened in the same place every time instead of healing between visits." Her eyes snapped open suddenly, and she staggered back from the door, catching herself against a support post, blood beginning to track from her nose in a thin dark line. "I'm sorry. I can't hold it any longer. It's too much for one try."

Priya caught her before she went all the way down, easing her onto an overturned crate, checking her pulse with the same steady competence Mara had watched her bring to every crisis this summer. "You did enough. More than enough. You didn't need to push that far."

"I needed you to believe me," Wren said, her voice thin, exhausted. "I needed you to see it wasn't just me panicking about nothing."

"We believe you," Mara said, kneeling beside her, taking her free hand. "This barn. This exact spot. Something happened here, repeatedly, and it's connected to what's been done to Caleb." She looked up at him, at the stillness that had settled over his face, a man standing in a building he owned, discovering it had been the site of his own quiet theft for who knew how long. "We need to search this whole barn properly. In daylight, carefully, for anything that might explain why here, specifically, out of every place on this property."

"Tomorrow," Caleb said, his voice rough. "I want daylight for this. I want to see every corner of this barn without a single shadow left to hide anything else in."

They walked Wren back to the house first, settling her on the porch with a glass of water and Toll's warm, comforting weight pressed against her side, and it was there, in the quiet aftermath, that Mara finally asked the question that had been sitting cold in her chest since the moment Wren described the dark-haired shape standing close enough to Caleb to steal something from him.

"Priya. You said a name back there, before you meant to. Dev."

Priya's face went carefully blank, the exact blankness Mara had learned to recognize as a woman calculating exactly how much of an oath to the town council she was about to break. "My brother," she said finally, quiet, tight. "He left four years ago. Nobody's heard from him since. I've spent four years telling myself he just needed distance." She looked toward the dark orchard rows, toward the barn still standing silent at the far edge of the property. "I don't know why his name came out of my mouth just now. I don't want to know why it came out of my mouth just now. But I don't think I get to keep not knowing much longer."
