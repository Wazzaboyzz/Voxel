# CHAPTER FORTY

Caleb was through the tree line before Mara had finished raising her hand to signal him, some old instinct overriding every promise about staying put the moment he saw Wren standing whole and steady in the grain office doorway, and Mara let him come without arguing, because whatever fragile honesty Kell had rationed out tonight had already used up its welcome.

"She knows where Adelaide is," Mara said, before Caleb could ask, watching the words land on him the way she imagined a diagnosis landed on a patient who'd spent years half suspecting it anyway.

Priya arrived a moment later, her dark car pulled up close to the office without headlights, her hand already resting on the radio clipped to her shoulder though she hadn't keyed it yet. "Say the word and I call this in properly. County, state, whoever it takes."

"Not yet." Kell's voice, from the doorway, had gone quiet in a way that carried more weight than his earlier practiced warmth ever had. "If you call this in as a crime scene tonight, with sirens and strangers and a county evidence team that has no idea what this valley actually is, you will lose whatever is left of her before anyone qualified to understand it gets anywhere close." He looked, for the first time all night, genuinely old, the mask of forty years finally slipping all the way off. "There are things on this property that do not behave the way physical evidence behaves anywhere else. I am asking you to let someone who actually understands that go first."

"You don't get to ask us anything," Caleb said, low and hard, stepping between Kell and Wren with the flat stillness of a man who had spent two years being handled gently by people he no longer trusted for it.

"No," Kell agreed. "I don't. I forfeited that a long time ago, and considerably more so tonight." He didn't move to stop them, didn't move at all, standing in the doorway of the office like a man who had already accepted whatever came next as something he'd earned rather than something being done to him. "But I would ask you to consider that I have walked this mill property for forty years, and I know precisely which parts of it are safe to cross and which parts of it are not, information that will cost you considerable time and possibly considerable safety to learn the hard way instead."

Wren's hand found Mara's sleeve, small and steady despite everything the evening had already asked of her. "He's telling the truth about that part," she said quietly. "I felt it the second we crossed onto the property. It's not like the knife, or the amplifier. This is bigger. It's like standing near a fault line instead of a single crack." She looked past Mara toward the dark shape of the mill's main structure, its silos rising black against a sky that had gone strange in the last few minutes, clouds building fast and wrong at the edges in a way no forecast Mara had checked that morning had predicted. "It's pulling at me the way the toll pulls at a debt. I think whatever's out there has been sitting unpaid for a very long time."

"That's not weather," Priya said sharply, following Mara's gaze upward, her hand tightening on the radio. "That's not natural at all."

"No," Kell said. "It's me. Or what's left of my control over the thing I've been managing badly for two years now." He rubbed a hand over his face, and for a moment the exhaustion underneath the performance was total, undisguised. "The valley's protection has been failing since spring, the same slow failure it went through in the eighties, and I have spent every week since patching it with borrowed weather I can no longer fully account for or contain. Tonight, with everything unraveling at once, I am afraid I no longer have the composure required to keep it steady. I would suggest you move quickly, before it decides to demonstrate exactly how unsteady I actually am."

Thunder rolled somewhere out past the ridge, too fast and too close for the clear evening it had been an hour ago, and Mara felt the first cold gust cut across the gravel lot, carrying the mineral smell of a storm that had no business existing yet.

"Wren stays with Priya," Mara said, decision arriving fast and absolute. "In the car, doors locked, radio on, and if anything happens to either of us in there, you call this in properly and you don't wait for permission from anyone, including me."

"Absolutely not," Wren said, straightening, nineteen years old and immovable in a way that reminded Mara sharply of her own grandfather. "I'm the one who can feel where she is. You'll be walking blind without me, and you don't have time to walk blind tonight."

"Wren." Mara crouched slightly to meet her eyes properly, urgency and tenderness both fighting for space in her chest. "He kidnapped you two hours ago. I am not walking you into a live crime scene with a storm that isn't natural building over our heads."

"I know exactly what he did." Wren's voice didn't waver. "And I also know that if I sit in that car while you two walk in without me, and something happens to Adelaide's proof, or to either of you, because I let fear win when it mattered most, I don't think I ever forgive myself for it. I've spent my whole life being the person this town protects instead of the person who protects something. I'm done being that tonight."

Mara looked at Priya, a silent question passing between two women who had known each other since before either of them understood what the valley actually cost the people who loved it. Priya nodded, once, reluctant but resolved. "I'll stay close behind. Radio contact every five minutes, no exceptions, and the second any of you says the word out loud, I am calling every agency this county has, valley secret or not."

Kell led them across the property with the careful, practiced steps of a man who had walked this exact route a hundred times in the dark, past the main mill structure and its rusted, silent machinery, toward a low concrete outbuilding half swallowed by weeds that Mara had never once noticed from the road despite driving past this property her entire life.

"The original grain testing lab," Kell said, his voice nearly lost under the rising wind now, lightning flickering somewhere behind the ridge with a frequency that had no business belonging to a storm barely ten minutes old. "Sealed since the mill closed in the seventies. I believe it is the only structure on this property old enough, and undisturbed enough, to have held anything this long without someone noticing."

Wren stopped several feet from the outbuilding's door, her whole body going rigid, her breath catching in a way that made Caleb's hand find her shoulder instinctively, steadying. "It's here," she said, her voice thin and strained, the same strain Mara had learned meant her gift was pulling harder than her small frame was built to carry. "Underneath. Not inside the building itself. Below it."

"There's a cellar," Kell confirmed. "Used for climate-controlled seed storage, before the mill modernized and abandoned the whole wing." He produced an old iron key from his jacket pocket, his hand visibly unsteady now, whether from the cold wind or the weight of what he was about to unlock, Mara couldn't tell. "I want it understood, before we go any further, that I did not do this myself. I have never once, in forty years, been able to bring myself to come down here and confirm what I have always suspected. That cowardice, I suppose, is the one thing about tonight I am not ashamed to admit."

The lock gave with a groan that sounded, in the wind, almost like something exhaling after a very long held breath, and the door swung open onto a set of concrete stairs descending into total darkness, cold air rising up to meet them, carrying the cold, mineral stillness of a space that had not been disturbed by anything living in a very long time.

Caleb went first, his flashlight cutting a narrow path down the stairs, Mara close behind him, Wren gripping the back of Mara's jacket with a fierceness that betrayed exactly how frightened she actually was beneath the composure she'd fought so hard to keep. The cellar opened into a low, wide room lined with rusted shelving, old canvas seed sacks long since rotted to dust, and at the far end, half concealed behind a collapsed section of shelving that looked, on closer inspection, deliberately arranged rather than accidentally fallen, a disturbance in the packed earth floor that no honest structural eye could mistake for anything natural.

"There," Wren whispered, pointing, her whole arm shaking now. "Right there. I can feel it so clearly it's like standing next to a live wire."

Mara knelt at the edge of the disturbed earth, running her fingers along the seam where the floor had clearly been broken and reset decades ago, the deliberate unevenness of ground that had been dug into and packed back down by someone who understood exactly what they were burying and precisely how carefully it needed to stay hidden. Her structural training gave her the shape of it before her heart fully caught up to what the shape meant, a rectangular depression, roughly the size and proportion of a grave.

Behind them, at the top of the stairs, a voice that did not belong to Kell called down into the dark, young, strained, and terribly familiar to Priya, whose sharp intake of breath told Mara everything before the name arrived.

"Ambrose. You need to come up here. Right now." A pause, footsteps shifting overhead. "She's not alone anymore, and I don't think I can hold what's left of this together for much longer either way."

Priya's radio crackled to life in her hand, static and urgency both, and Mara looked up from the disturbed earth to see her friend's face go pale in the flashlight's narrow beam, recognition and dread arriving in the same instant.

"Dev," Priya said, barely above a whisper, the name landing in the cold cellar like something breaking loose after being held too tightly for far too long. "That's my brother."

Thunder cracked directly overhead, close enough now to rattle loose dust from the cellar's ancient rafters, and Mara understood, kneeling beside what might well be Adelaide Whitlock's grave with a storm no forecast had predicted building into something dangerous above them, that whatever ending Kell had claimed to feel relieved about arriving was no longer a slow unraveling anyone could manage carefully. It had already started, faster and more violent than any of them had prepared for, and the only way through it now was up those stairs and directly into the middle of it.

"Stay with her," Mara told Caleb, nodding toward Wren, who had gone very still, one hand pressed flat against the disturbed earth as though she could somehow steady whatever she was sensing there by touch alone. "Priya and I go up first."

"Like hell you do." Caleb's grip on her arm was gentle but immovable. "We go up together, or none of us goes up at all. I already spent two years finding out what it costs when the people who love you decide protecting you means leaving you out of the hardest part. I'm not doing that to you tonight, and I'm not doing it to her either."

Mara looked at him for one long moment in the flashlight's narrow beam, at the steady, unshakeable certainty that had replaced every trace of the careful stranger she'd met at the fence line two months ago, and felt something settle into place inside her that no amount of evidence or memory could have manufactured on its own.

"Together, then," she said, and reached for Wren's hand, the four of them climbing the narrow stairs back toward whatever the storm and Dev Nair were both about to demand of them, out of the cold, undisturbed dark and into a night that had stopped waiting for anyone's permission to finally break open.
