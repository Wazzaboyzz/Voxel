# CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR

Caleb held the flashlight low and cupped while Mara worked the housing open with a screwdriver from his truck, her hands steady despite the hammering in her chest, years of fine motor work under pressure paying off exactly the way she'd hoped it would. The screws came loose one at a time, each one dropped carefully into her palm rather than the grass, the same discipline she'd bring to any active job site where a lost fastener could mean an hour lost searching for it later.

The mill loomed off to their left the whole time, a hulking black shape against the deeper black of the treeline, its broken windows catching no light at all, and Mara felt an involuntary chill pass through her every time her eyes strayed toward it, some old instinct recognizing danger in a building she'd never once set foot in. Caleb had noticed her looking on the walk in.

"Bad place," he'd said quietly, not slowing his pace. "Even before any of this, people around here never liked walking past it after dark. My father used to say the wind came off it wrong, colder than it should be for the season."

"You believe that?"

"I believe I don't want to find out tonight."

Now, crouched at the station's base with the mill still looming at the edge of her vision, Mara made herself focus back on the housing in front of her, the ordinary discipline of the work pulling her attention away from a building that had no bearing on tonight's actual task.

Inside, nestled among the ordinary sensors, was something that didn't belong, a small secondary unit wired directly into the reporting line, unmarked, clearly not county issue, its housing a slightly different shade of gray than everything surrounding it, a mismatch that would mean nothing to a casual observer and everything to someone trained to notice when a system had been altered after the fact.

"There," Mara breathed, photographing it from every angle her phone allowed, her pulse loud in her own ears. "That's not standard equipment. That's an override."

"Can you tell what it does?"

"Not without taking it apart, and I'm not doing that here." She closed the housing carefully, making sure it looked untouched, easing the screws back into place with the same care she'd use resetting an access panel on an active site, counting them twice against her palm to be sure none had been left behind in the grass. "But I know someone who can. Priya still has evidence handling training, and more importantly, she has a brother who owes this town the truth after what Kell did to his reputation this week."

She photographed the serial number too, a faint string of digits stamped into the housing's underside, barely legible even with the headlamp angled directly on it, and made Caleb hold the light steady while she took four separate shots at slightly different angles, unwilling to risk a single blurred frame being the difference between proof and nothing. Her hands had gone perfectly steady now, the earlier nerves burned off by the simple, absorbing work of documentation, the same focus she brought to any site inspection where getting the details right mattered more than how fast she got them.

"How long has this been here, do you think," Caleb asked quietly, watching her work.

"Hard to say without testing the wiring, but the corrosion on these leads isn't fresh. Years, maybe. Long enough that whoever installed it wasn't worried about anyone finding it." She sat back on her heels, looking at the little gray box with something like disgust. "Which means he's been doing this a long time, and doing it carefully enough that not one person in this town ever thought to check."

"Not one person who was looking, anyway." Caleb's voice had gone quiet, thoughtful. "My father complained about the frost lines his whole life and never once thought to ask why. I don't think it occurred to him that a man could be doing this on purpose. I don't think it occurred to most people in this valley that it was even possible."

"That's the whole design of it, I think. Not just hiding the evidence. Making the crime itself unimaginable to the people it's happening to." Mara zipped the photos into a folder on her phone, double-checking the file count against her memory of how many shots she'd taken. "Nobody investigates a thing they can't picture in the first place."

They were halfway back to the truck when headlights swept across the field, slow and deliberate, and Mara felt Caleb's hand close around her wrist, pulling her down into the tall grass beside the fence line before she'd consciously registered the danger, the cool, dew-damp grass closing over them both.

The car idled at the top of the rise for a long moment, engine running, lights sweeping once across the weather station and once, almost lazily, across the field where they crouched, and then it pulled away into the dark, unhurried, the way a man moves when he already knows exactly where to look and simply hasn't decided yet whether tonight is the night he acts on it. Mara held her breath the entire time, acutely aware of Caleb's shoulder pressed against hers, the two of them still and silent, an odd, unwanted intimacy forced on them by the circumstances rather than chosen. She counted the seconds the way she'd count them waiting for a load test to finish, each one stretching longer than it should, her whole body braced for a failure that never quite arrived.

She thought, crouched there in the wet grass with her heart pounding, of every safety briefing she'd ever sat through on a job site, the ones that always emphasized the same point: the danger you can see coming is rarely the one that hurts you. It's the one you've stopped watching for because nothing's gone wrong yet. She wondered how long Kell had been driving this exact stretch of road at exactly this hour, confident enough in the valley's silence that he'd stopped expecting anyone to be crouched in the grass watching him do it.

"That was him," Caleb whispered, once the taillights had vanished around the bend.

"Or someone he sent." Mara's heart was still hammering, her hand still caught in his, and she found she didn't want to let go even once the danger had passed. "Either way. He knows somebody was out here tonight."

"Then we move fast." Caleb's voice had gone low and certain in the dark. "Tomorrow. We get this to Priya and Dev before Kell decides waiting to see what we do is more of a risk than doing something about it himself."

Neither of them moved for a moment longer, still low in the grass, the adrenaline of the near-miss slow to release its hold even after the road had gone dark and quiet again. Mara felt Caleb's thumb move once, absently, against the inside of her wrist, a small unconscious gesture that told her more about how frightened he'd actually been than anything he might have said out loud.

"We should go," she said finally, though she made no move to stand.

"In a minute." His voice was rough. "I just need a minute where you're still exactly this close and nothing's driving past us."

She let him have it, the two of them sitting in the damp grass under a sky gone fully dark now, stars starting to show through the last thin haze of evening cloud, and for that one suspended minute the whole valley's danger receded to something distant and manageable, nothing but the sound of his breathing evening out beside her and the steady, grounding pressure of his hand still wrapped around hers.

Eventually he helped her up, brushing a stray blade of grass from her sleeve with a care that felt almost formal given the circumstances, and they made their way back to the truck in silence, checking the road twice before crossing it, the old caution neither of them had needed a week ago now fully instinctive. Caleb didn't start the engine until Mara had her phone's photos backed up to a second, offline folder, an unnecessary precaution maybe, but neither of them was willing to risk losing the one piece of hard evidence they'd finally managed to find.

They sat in the cab a moment before he turned the key, both of them still catching their breath, the adrenaline of the last twenty minutes slow to fully drain out of either of them. Mara laughed quietly, more from released tension than actual humor, and felt Caleb's hand find hers across the seat, warm and steady.

"That's the most illegal thing I've done since I was seventeen," she admitted.

"What'd you do at seventeen?"

"Snuck into the county fairgrounds after hours with Priya to ride the Ferris wheel one last time before they tore it down." She shook her head, half-smiling despite everything. "This felt considerably more dangerous."

"Slightly higher stakes than a condemned Ferris wheel, yeah." He finally turned the key, the engine catching low and familiar. "For what it's worth, you were good in there. Steady hands. I don't think I could've kept mine that still."

"Years of practice not shaking on a scaffold forty feet up." She glanced at him, something warm moving through her despite the lingering nerves. "Turns out it transfers to breaking and entering."

They drove the first mile without much talking, the logging road rough enough under the tires that conversation felt secondary to simply holding on, and Mara thought, watching the dark trees slide past, that tonight had changed something in the shape of what they were actually doing together. Not just gathering evidence anymore. Building trust the hard way, under real pressure, of a kind that couldn't be faked or rushed.

"You okay," Caleb asked, glancing over at a stretch where the road finally smoothed out. "Really. Not just the adrenaline talking."

"I'm okay." She considered the question honestly before answering it further. "Scared, still, a little. But mostly I keep thinking about how much easier that was with somebody beside me who actually wanted to be there instead of somebody I had to convince. I did a lot of hard things alone in Chicago. I forgot what it felt like to not have to."

"You don't have to do any of the rest of this alone either." His voice was quiet, certain. "I meant that back at the fence line. I still mean it."

She let that settle for a moment, watching the dark shapes of fence posts flick past the window in the truck's dim running lights. "It's strange," she said finally. "I spent eight years building a whole life around not needing anyone that badly again. And now I'm sitting in a truck at midnight, still shaking a little from crouching in wet grass while a man who might want to hurt us drove past twice, and it's somehow the safest I've felt all summer."

"Safest how."

"Because you didn't hesitate. Not once tonight. Not when I said I'd do it alone, not when the headlights came over the rise. You were just there, the whole time, exactly when it mattered." She looked over at him, the dashboard light catching the line of his jaw, the scar through his eyebrow. "I don't think I fully understood, until tonight, how much I've been waiting for somebody to just be there instead of needing to be talked into it."

Caleb didn't answer right away, his eyes on the road, and when he finally spoke his voice had gone rougher than before. "I don't know how to be anything else with you. I've tried, a little, these past few weeks, tried to keep some careful distance in case it turned out I was building something on a foundation that wasn't actually mine to build on. Tonight kind of ended that experiment for me."

"Good," Mara said simply, and meant it more than the single word let on.

"First thing tomorrow," she said, as the truck rolled slowly down the logging road, headlights off until they'd cleared the last rise. "Priya, then Dev. We don't sit on this even one extra day."

"Agreed." Caleb's hands were steady on the wheel now, the earlier tension finally easing out of his shoulders. "We finally have something he can't just talk his way around."

Mara looked out at the dark fields passing the window, the photographs sitting heavy and certain on her phone, and allowed herself, for the length of the drive home, to believe they might actually be close to something that would hold.
