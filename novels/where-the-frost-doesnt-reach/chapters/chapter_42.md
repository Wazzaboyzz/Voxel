# CHAPTER FORTY-TWO

The county units arrived within twenty minutes, headlights cutting through the last of the rain in slow, careful sweeps across the mill yard, and Mara stood at the edge of the gravel lot with Wren's weight still braced against her side, watching a night that had felt entirely private and enormous only minutes ago begin, inevitably, to become something official.

An EMT knelt beside Wren almost immediately, checking her pulse, her pupils, the thin dark line of blood still tracking from her nose, and Wren submitted to the examination with the exhausted patience of someone who understood the fuss was necessary even if it wasn't, in her own estimation, warranted.

"Vitals are steady," the EMT said, mostly to Mara, though his eyes kept flicking toward the collapsed section of roofline with the puzzled look of someone trying to reconcile storm damage with a forecast that had shown nothing but clear skies. "We'll want her at the hospital for observation tonight regardless. Blood like that from a fall this minor isn't something to just walk off."

"I didn't fall," Wren said, too tired to argue the point with any real energy, and the EMT, wisely, didn't press it.

Priya stood a short distance away with two uniformed officers Mara didn't recognize, her hand still resting on Dev's shoulder where he sat, wrists zip-tied with a gentleness that told Mara Priya had insisted on doing it herself rather than let a stranger's hands be the last thing her brother felt as a free man for however long the county's justice took to sort out what he actually was, a victim or a criminal or, more honestly, both at once in a way the paperwork would never fully capture.

"I need a statement from you tonight, and one from Miss Voss," one of the officers said to Priya, careful in the same wary way people got careful around a colleague they respected but couldn't fully protect from procedure. "And I'm going to need someone to explain to me what exactly happened to that roofline, because whatever's in my report is going to have to account for storm damage nobody predicted and nobody's radar caught building."

"Structural failure," Mara said, stepping forward, her voice settling into the same flat, competent register she'd used a hundred times on job sites back in Chicago. "Old timber, decades of water damage nobody maintained, a freak microburst nobody could have forecast given how localized these valley weather patterns run. I can write you up a proper structural assessment in the morning if you need documentation for the file."

The officer studied her for a moment, clearly aware there was more to the story than the tidy, plausible version she'd just handed him, and equally aware, in the way small-county law enforcement sometimes was, that some nights in Amity Falls simply didn't survive contact with an honest incident report. "Appreciate that, ma'am. Morning's fine."

Kell had not moved from where he stood near the outbuilding's door, rain-soaked and hollow, watching the whole scene unfold around him with the strange, still passivity of a man observing the end of something he'd spent forty years believing only he could hold up. When the second officer approached him with a slow, deliberate formality, he simply extended his wrists without being asked, no protest, no lawyer's careful language, nothing left of the man who'd argued chain of custody in front of the county board three days earlier.

"I'd like it noted," Kell said quietly, to no one in particular, as the cuffs closed, "that I told them where to look. Whatever else this valley decides I am, I would like that part remembered honestly."

"It'll be in the report," the officer said, not unkindly, and led him toward the waiting car.

Mara found Caleb near the cellar entrance, standing guard over the disturbed earth with a stillness that told her he hadn't moved from that spot since the storm broke, as though leaving Adelaide Whitlock alone even for a moment tonight, after four decades of exactly that kind of abandonment, was a debt he'd quietly decided to pay on her behalf.

"They'll want to excavate properly, in daylight, with an actual forensic team," Mara said, coming to stand beside him. "Tonight isn't for that. Tonight was just for finding her."

"I keep thinking about what Thomas said." Caleb's voice was quiet, rough with everything the night had asked of him. "That he spent ten years chasing this and never found her. And she was forty feet from a road I've driven past my whole life, the entire time." He shook his head slowly. "How does a whole town not notice a woman missing for a hundred years."

"The same way it didn't notice you missing two years of your own life until somebody who loved you refused to accept the ordinary explanation for it." Mara took his hand, cold and rain-slicked in hers. "Towns don't investigate the stories they've already decided are simple. That's the whole mechanism. Ambrose Whitlock built a hundred years of cover on exactly that laziness, and Kell just inherited the same trick and used it smaller."

They stood together a while longer, watching the yard slowly empty of urgency, ambulance lights sweeping steady and unhurried now that the crisis had passed into something merely procedural, and Mara felt, for the first time since Denise's frantic phone call hours ago, the adrenaline finally beginning to recede, leaving behind something rawer and more exhausted than she'd let herself feel while there was still a job to do.

Priya approached them as the last of the county vehicles prepared to pull out, Dev already loaded into the back of one, his face turned toward the window, watching his sister with an expression Mara couldn't fully read from this distance, grief and relief and something like hope all tangled together.

"They're taking him to county holding tonight," Priya said, her voice carefully level in the way Mara recognized as the only thing keeping her upright. "Council will have to decide what happens after that. Whether this gets treated as a straightforward crime, coercion and all, or whether it goes through whatever the valley's own process is for a Finder violation." She let out a long, unsteady breath. "I don't even know which outcome I'm hoping for. I just know I finally get to stop lying to my mother about where he's been."

"You did everything right tonight," Mara said. "All of it. The car in the dark, the radio contact, calling this in the second it was actually safe to. You don't have to carry any guilt about the parts that were never yours to control."

"I know that logically." Priya managed something close to a smile, exhausted and genuine both. "Ask me again in about a week, once I've slept, and I might actually believe it."

Denise Castellano arrived at the edge of the property just as the ambulance doors were swinging open, having refused to wait at home a second longer once Priya's promised call finally came through, and she pushed past the officer trying gently to hold the perimeter with the singular focus of a mother who had spent three hours imagining every possible version of tonight except the one where her daughter walked out under her own power.

"She's fine," Mara called out, moving to intercept her before panic could fully take hold. "Denise, she's fine, she's tired and she's got a bloody nose and she is going to be insufferably proud of herself for the next several weeks, but she is fine."

Denise's whole body sagged with relief, and she gripped Mara's arm hard enough to bruise. "What happened. Somebody tell me what actually happened out here tonight, because the deputy on the phone used the word incident about four times and told me nothing else."

"I'll tell you everything, I promise, but not standing in a gravel lot at one in the morning." Mara guided her toward the ambulance, where Wren had already spotted her mother through the open doors and was calling out something that sounded, even at this distance, distinctly unrepentant. "Go be with her. She wants you to know she did something good tonight, before you hear the parts that'll scare you."

Denise looked at Mara for one long moment, something sharpening behind the fear, an old, practiced instinct for reading exactly how much a person wasn't saying. "This is about the thing Del wouldn't explain to me either, isn't it. The thing this whole town's been carefully not explaining to anyone for as long as I've lived here."

"Yes," Mara said, and found, for the first time in her life, that she meant to actually answer the question instead of deflecting it. "And I'll tell you all of it, properly, once your daughter's had a full night's sleep and a chance to tell you her own version first. She's earned that much."

Something in Denise's face eased, not fully, but enough to let her turn toward her daughter without the last of the dread still holding her rigid, and Mara watched her climb into the ambulance and gather Wren against her, the two of them a single shape in the harsh interior light, before the doors finally swung shut.

"I'll make sure she hears it in the right order," Mara said to herself, watching the ambulance doors close, and found she'd already kept the promise before Wren had even needed to ask for it.

It was nearly two in the morning by the time the last of the county vehicles cleared the mill property, the storm long since spent, the sky overhead gone clear and impossibly full of stars the way it only managed after a hard rain had scrubbed the valley clean. Mara stood in the wrecked, quiet yard with Caleb's arm around her shoulders, both of them too tired to speak, and looked back once at the dark shape of the mill against the stars, at the collapsed roofline, at the cellar door still standing open on whatever daylight and a proper forensic team would finally, formally confirm tomorrow.

"It's not over," she said quietly, echoing what she'd told Kell only an hour ago, though the words felt different now, steadier, less like a warning and more like a simple accounting of what remained. "The investigation. The council decision about Dev. Whatever the town does once this gets out properly. That's all still coming."

"I know." Caleb pressed a tired kiss to the top of her head, his voice rough with exhaustion and something else underneath it, something that had settled and steadied through the whole long night rather than fraying under it. "But we found her. Whatever else this town still has to answer for, we actually found her, after a hundred years of nobody trying hard enough to look. That's not nothing, tonight. I think that's actually the whole thing that mattered most."

They walked back to the truck together in the clean, cold air, past the tree line where Caleb had waited only hours earlier, promising to be careful, and Mara understood, climbing into the passenger seat beside him, that whatever the daylight brought next, tonight had already answered the one question she'd come back to this valley half afraid to ask. The town could still surprise her. It could still cost people everything. But it no longer got to keep its secrets simply because nobody had loved anyone here enough to look past the easy story.

She had. She finally had.
