# CHAPTER THIRTY-NINE

The call from Wren's mother came at six in the evening, three days after the hospital, and Mara knew from the first ragged breath on the line that whatever Thomas had warned them about had stopped being a hypothetical sometime in the last few hours.

"She said she was walking to your place," Denise Castellano said, her voice fraying at the edges in a way Mara had never heard from a woman who ran the diner counter with unshakeable calm through every kind of small-town crisis. "That was two hours ago, Mara. Her phone goes straight to voicemail. I've called everyone. Nobody's seen her since she left the house."

Mara was already reaching for her keys before the call ended, Caleb's hand closing around her wrist to steady the shaking she hadn't noticed starting. "Where would she go if she wasn't coming here."

"She wouldn't deviate," Caleb said, certainty threading through the fear in his voice. "Not with what Thomas told you. She'd have called first if her plans changed. She's careful now, since the mill scare. You taught her to be careful."

Priya arrived within ten minutes of Mara's call, her uniform half on, her face carrying the grim, practiced focus of someone who'd spent years training for exactly this kind of night and had never actually wanted to use it. "I've got a car checking the route between the Castellano place and here. I've got another checking Kell's properties, all of them, the ones on record and the ones I've heard about off the record over the years." She looked at Mara, something apologetic in her eyes. "I have to ask. Does she know about Adelaide?"

"I hadn't told her yet." Mara's stomach dropped through the floor. "I was going to tell her tomorrow morning. Carefully. With coffee and good light, so it wouldn't feel like one more thing dropped on her without warning."

"Then how would Kell know to move now, tonight, if she hasn't said anything to anyone."

The answer arrived in the silence that followed, cold and simple. "He doesn't need her to know anything," Mara said slowly. "He needs her not to find out anything. If he thinks the investigation is finally close enough to Adelaide that someone might ask a Finder to look, he doesn't wait for confirmation. He removes the tool before it gets used."

They found the first real evidence twenty minutes later, in the gravel turnout half a mile from the Castellano house, Wren's bicycle on its side in the ditch, the front wheel still spinning slow and useless when Caleb's flashlight found it. No blood. No sign of a struggle beyond the bike itself, which told Mara something almost worse than violence would have, a controlled, unhurried abduction by someone who hadn't needed to fight for it.

"He didn't do this alone," Priya said, crouching beside the bike, her flashlight tracing tire marks in the soft shoulder that didn't belong to anything Kell himself drove. "Kell doesn't get his own hands dirty. He never has."

Mara's phone rang before anyone could say what that implied, an unfamiliar number, and she answered it with her heart climbing into her throat.

"Miss Voss." Kell's voice, warm and measured, the exact register he'd used at the hearing three days earlier, as though nothing about tonight required him to sound any different than he did presenting a budget report. "I imagine you're somewhere near a ditch and a bicycle right about now, working yourself into quite a state. I'd like to save you the trouble of a search that won't find anything until I want it found."

"Where is she." Mara's voice came out flatter and colder than she felt, every ounce of her structural training holding the panic in a load it could actually bear.

"Safe. For now. Which is considerably more than I can say for how long that stays true, depending on how the next hour goes." A pause, and Mara could hear, underneath the practiced calm, something she hadn't expected from him, a thread of genuine, exhausted strain, a man near the end of a very long performance. "I'd like you to come to the old grain office at the mill, alone. Not the deputy. Not the boyfriend. I have no interest in a standoff, Miss Voss. I have interest in an ending, and I think you're the only person left in this valley capable of understanding the shape of the one I have in mind."

"I'm not coming alone."

"Then Wren stays where she is, and I promise you, wherever that is, it is not a place a nineteen-year-old girl should spend a night, let alone longer." The warmth in his voice hardened, briefly, the mask slipping just enough to show something underneath it that had been holding this town together and strangling it in equal measure for forty years. "One hour, Miss Voss. Alone. I am not a violent man by nature, whatever your grandfather's suspicions have led you to believe about me, but I have spent this town's entire history protecting it from things considerably worse than one difficult conversation, and I will not let a girl's curiosity undo forty years of careful work in a single night."

The line went dead before she could answer.

"Absolutely not," Caleb said, the words out before she'd even lowered the phone, his hand finding her shoulder with a grip that carried real fear underneath the anger. "You are not walking into that mill alone with a man who just admitted to kidnapping a teenager."

"He didn't admit to kidnapping her. He admitted to holding her somewhere I can't find, which is worse, because it means he's controlled enough to still be careful about what he says on a phone line he can't be sure is secure." Mara made herself breathe, made herself think the way she thought through a compromised structure, cataloguing load points instead of panicking at the crack. "But you're right that I'm not going alone. I'm going in a way he doesn't get to see coming."

Priya was already moving toward her car. "I can get a car within two hundred yards of the mill without headlights, park it dark, wait for your signal. It's not backup he'll see. It's backup that's there anyway."

"And me?" Caleb's voice had gone very still, that same stillness she'd learned meant he was holding something enormous in check through sheer force of will.

"You drive me there. You wait at the tree line, close enough to reach me fast, far enough that he doesn't call the whole thing off the second he sees your truck." Mara caught his eyes and held them, needing him to understand this wasn't caution born of doubting him, but the only version of tonight that got Wren out alive. "I need you steady right now, Caleb. Not reckless. I've watched what this town does to men who go in loud when careful was the only thing that would have worked."

He didn't answer for a long moment, his jaw working through something that looked, in the dim light, like the hardest kind of trust a person could offer, the kind that required setting aside every instinct in favor of someone else's judgment. "Okay," he said finally. "Careful. But I'm not staying at any tree line if I hear you scream."

"I know." She touched his face, brief, grounding, for both of them. "Neither would I."

The mill sat black and enormous against the last grey light of the evening, its silhouette the same one Mara had grown up learning to ignore, an ordinary piece of the valley's skyline that she now understood had been quietly holding a woman's disappearance inside its property line for the better part of a century. Kell was waiting outside the old grain office, alone, his suit jacket incongruous against the rust and weeds, a man dressed for a council meeting standing in the wreckage of exactly the kind of thing he'd spent his whole career making sure nobody had to look at directly.

"You came," he said, and there was no triumph in it, only a tired kind of relief that unsettled Mara more than aggression would have.

"Where is she."

"Inside. Unharmed, and considerably more composed than I expected a girl her age to be under the circumstances. She has your grandfather's stubbornness, that one. It's a family trait apparently not limited to blood." He gestured toward the office door, a courtesy that felt obscene given the circumstances. "I'd like five minutes before you see her. I find I need to say some of this out loud to someone who'll actually understand what it cost, and there is nobody left alive in this valley who fits that description except you."

Mara held her ground. "Talk fast."

"Adelaide Whitlock came to me first," Kell said, and something in his careful posture finally, visibly broke, forty years of composure cracking all at once now that the audience for it had finally narrowed down to one. "Before your grandfather ever suspected a thing. She'd found records, old ones, proving Ambrose Whitlock had renewed the bargain more than once, outliving generations of men who should have been his sons by any honest accounting. She wanted to bring it to the whole valley. I told her the truth would end everything this town had built its survival on, that the bargain wasn't a monster to expose, it was the only thing standing between this valley and the drought that had already nearly killed it once."

"And when she wouldn't listen."

"I made a mistake I have spent every day since trying to justify into something smaller than it was." Kell's voice had gone thin, stripped of every practiced warmth it had carried at the hearing. "I did not kill her, Miss Voss. I want that understood clearly, whatever your grandfather believed. But I told the wrong person she intended to talk, and the man I told made the decision I should have prevented, and I let the town believe she'd simply left, because the alternative was admitting I'd handed a woman's life to someone I should never have trusted with the knowledge in the first place. I have carried that for decades. I have tried, every single day since, to be worth the cost she paid so that this valley could keep surviving."

"That's not an explanation. That's a man deciding his guilt makes a better story than his cowardice." Mara felt something cold and precise settle over her, the same hard clarity she'd once reserved for condemning a building nobody else wanted to admit was already failing. "You let a woman disappear, and then you spent forty years running the same calculation every time it threatened to cost you something again. Caleb. Dev. Whoever comes next after Wren, if we let you keep making that math."

"I never wanted this to touch Caleb." For the first time, something that looked almost like genuine grief moved through Kell's face. "That was never supposed to be permanent. A season, maybe two, enough to buy the valley time until the weather pattern stabilized on its own. I did not understand, until it was already done twice over, how much of a man that kind of theft actually takes, or how little control I truly had over the person I'd pressured into taking it."

"Where is Dev." The name left her mouth before she'd fully decided to ask it, an instinct born of everything Thomas had implied and everything the case had circled without quite landing on.

Something shifted behind Kell's eyes, calculation returning fast, the mask sliding back into place with visible effort. "That is a conversation for a different night, Miss Voss, and one I am not yet prepared to have standing in a parking lot with an hour I promised myself would end differently." He gestured again toward the office door, more urgent now, a man aware he'd already said more than he meant to. "Take the girl. Take her home. Whatever comes for me now, formal investigation or something this valley decides on its own terms, I would like it to arrive having done at least this one thing properly before the end."

Mara didn't move yet. "Why tonight. Why take her at all if you were only ever going to hand her back."

"Because I needed to know how close you actually were before I decided what I still had left to lose." Kell's composure had nearly fully returned now, the practiced politician settling back over whatever raw thing had briefly surfaced. "You are closer than I hoped and further than I feared, which tells me the ending is coming regardless of anything further I attempt to control. I find, strangely, that I am relieved by that more than I am afraid of it. Forty years is a very long time to hold something up alone."

He stepped aside from the door, and Mara moved past him fast, every instinct screaming to reach Wren before whatever fragile honesty Kell had rationed out tonight curdled back into something more dangerous. The grain office door was unlocked, and inside, in the grey wash of a single battery lantern, Wren sat on an overturned crate, wrists free, unbound, exactly as composed as Kell had claimed, her eyes finding Mara's the second the door opened with a fierce, relieved clarity that broke something loose in Mara's chest.

"I'm okay," Wren said, before Mara could ask. "He didn't hurt me. He just needed to talk, mostly, and then he needed somewhere to keep me while he decided what talking to you was actually going to cost him." She stood, steady on legs that shook only slightly, nineteen years old and holding herself together through sheer, stubborn will. "Mara. I know where Adelaide is. I've known since about twenty minutes after he brought me here. This whole property has been humming at the edge of my gift since we walked in, and I finally understood why."

Behind them, in the doorway, Kell had gone very still, and Mara understood, watching his face in the lantern light, that whatever ending he'd claimed to be relieved about had just arrived considerably faster than even he had prepared for.

"Show me," Mara said.
