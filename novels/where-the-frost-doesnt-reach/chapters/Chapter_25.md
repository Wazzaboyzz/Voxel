# CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE

Dev met them at Priya's kitchen table at six the next morning, still moving carefully, still bruised along one wrist where the zip ties had bitten deep, but steadier now, the particular steadiness of a man who has decided being useful is the only apology he has left to offer. The kitchen smelled like burnt coffee and toast nobody had gotten around to eating, and Priya's dog, a much older and calmer animal than Toll, lay under the table with its chin resting on someone's foot, entirely unbothered by the tension radiating off every person seated above it. Mara sat with her hands wrapped around a mug she wasn't drinking from, more for the warmth than the coffee, watching the early light climb the kitchen wall in slow increments while Dev worked through his photos.

"It's a redirect amplifier," he said, turning Mara's photos over on the table, zooming in with two fingers on his phone screen, the early light through the kitchen window catching the exhaustion still etched under his eyes. "Crude one, but functional. It's designed to take whatever Kell's gift produces locally and broadcast it as if it's naturally occurring across a wider radius. That's how he's been faking system-wide frost patterns and rain anomalies without anyone questioning why the effect only ever seems to center on his own rotation schedule. The station reports what he wants it to report, and the county extension office just takes it as gospel because why wouldn't they."

"Can you prove it's rigged? Actually prove it, not just theorize." Mara leaned forward over the table, the same posture she'd use reviewing a set of drawings for a stamp-worthy flaw, something concrete enough to put a signature on.

"With this?" Dev tapped the photo. "Yeah. There's a serial number etched into the housing, barely visible, but it's there. If that traces back to a supplier, and if I can get an actual technician, someone outside the valley with no reason to protect Kell, to look at the wiring pattern, that's not spin anymore. That's evidence a county board has to take seriously."

Priya, arms crossed, leaning against the counter with dark circles under her eyes that spoke to a week of very little sleep, finally spoke, her voice rough at the edges in a way Mara had never heard from her before, not even at the funeral. "There's a problem. County board doesn't investigate anything without a formal complaint, and the only people with standing to file one are residents directly affected, which in this case is basically everyone in town, which means the second we file, Kell finds out exactly who's behind it, and he's already shown us tonight he's willing to drive out and check on things himself."

"Then we don't file it alone." Mara had been turning the problem over since the fence line, the shape of an actual plan finally cohering, the satisfaction of a solution assembling itself out of pieces she'd been holding separately for days. "Yusuf's on the council. He's already shown he's willing to push back on Kell's secrecy. If he files alongside us, backed by evidence instead of suspicion, it's not one grieving woman with a theory anymore. It's a sitting council member with a formal complaint and physical proof."

"He'll do it," Dev said immediately. "I've heard him argue with Kell in closed session more than once. He thinks the council's been rotting from a culture of silence for years. He just needed something solid enough to actually act on."

Caleb had been quiet through most of this, watching the exchange with the particular stillness Mara had learned meant he was working something through before he said it out loud, his arms crossed and his gaze fixed somewhere past all of them, on the middle distance of his own thoughts.

"There's a piece we're missing," he said finally. "Dev, you said there was at least one case before Frey. Before the eighties. How far back."

Mara watched him ask it, and understood, somewhere underneath her own racing thoughts, that this question cost him something none of the rest of them fully understood, a man asking how far back a pattern of exploitation went that had, quite possibly, already claimed two years he'd never get an accounting for.

Dev's jaw tightened, and Mara watched him visibly brace before answering, the same bracing she'd learned to recognize in Caleb himself. "I couldn't find a clean record. That's what scared me most, honestly. The trail doesn't just go cold, it gets actively scrubbed the further back you go, like somebody's been maintaining the gaps on purpose for longer than one lifetime should require." He looked up, meeting Caleb's eyes. "I know how that sounds."

"It sounds," Mara said slowly, the pieces rearranging themselves into a shape she hadn't wanted to see clearly until this exact moment, "like my grandfather's last note in that file. If I'm right and I can't prove it, God help the next person he needs to spend. He wasn't just talking about Kell running this once. He suspected it went deeper than one man's career."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, a truck rumbled past on the road, ordinary morning traffic in a town that had no idea how much of its safety had been quietly financed by other people's stolen years, the sound almost obscene in its normalcy.

"One thing at a time," Priya said finally, the deputy in her reasserting itself over the exhausted sister. "We get the amplifier evidence to Yusuf today. We file today, before Kell has more time to prepare a story or make evidence disappear. The older mystery, whoever else has been involved, however far back this goes, that's a fight for after we've actually stopped the bleeding in front of us."

Dev nodded slowly, rubbing at his bruised wrist without seeming to notice he was doing it, and Mara watched Priya's hand find her brother's shoulder briefly, a small, wordless gesture of forgiveness that neither of them acknowledged out loud, the kind of thing siblings communicated in a language older than the crisis currently forcing them to use it.

Before they left, Priya walked Mara out to the porch, out of earshot of the others, her arms crossed against the morning chill. "I need to say something, and I need you not to take it the wrong way," she said. "I've known Dev my whole life. I watched him get in over his head with Kell and I didn't see it happening until it was almost too late. I'm asking you to be careful the same way, both of you, because this town has a way of making people think they're the exception right up until they're not."

"I hear you," Mara said. "I don't have a better answer than that we don't have another option left that isn't this one."

"I know." Priya's jaw tightened. "That's what scares me most. Go. I'll call you the second I hear anything."

---

Yusuf listened to the whole account standing in the back room of his store, arms folded, jaw tight, the smell of feed and fertilizer thick in the small storage space around him, and when Mara finished, he didn't ask a single skeptical question, which told her more clearly than anything else how much weight his earlier warning at the counter had actually been carrying. Stacked feed bags rose around him on every side, and Mara found herself thinking, absurdly, of a man standing at the center of his own small fortress, one he'd apparently been quietly building toward this exact conversation for six patient years.

"I've suspected something for six years," he said quietly. "Never had proof. Never had anyone willing to look hard enough to find it." He took the photos from Dev's phone, studying them with the focus of a man who'd clearly been waiting a long time for exactly this, turning the screen slightly to catch the light better. "I'll file this morning. Formally, on the record, with my name attached, which means Kell will know exactly where it came from within the hour."

"Are you sure," Mara asked. "This puts a target on you too."

"Deputy Nair here nearly lost her brother to that man's version of doing what's necessary." Yusuf's eyes were steady, and something in the set of his shoulders reminded Mara, unexpectedly, of the way her own grandfather used to stand when he'd made up his mind about something he knew would cost him. "I've spent six years being careful. Careful hasn't fixed anything. I'd rather be a target doing the right thing than safe doing nothing." He looked at Caleb directly. "And for what it's worth, son, I'm sorry this town let this happen to you. Some of us should have pushed harder a long time ago."

Caleb didn't answer right away, and when he did, his voice had an edge Mara hadn't heard from him before, something that sounded almost like hope trying to survive contact with reality. "Just help us stop it before it happens to somebody else."

Yusuf nodded once, a gesture that felt more like a handshake than a nod, and turned back to the photos on the counter, already sketching out in his head, Mara suspected, exactly how he intended to word the complaint he was about to put his name to.

"One more thing," he said, as they turned to go. "Once this is filed, don't come back here for a while. Not because I don't want to see you. Because if he's watching either of you, I don't want him watching my store too closely in the process. Call instead. I'll answer."

Mara understood the sense in it even as it stung a little, one more small precaution layered onto an already careful week, one more reminder of exactly how much this town's ordinary trust had already been eaten away by a single man's decades of careful lies. She thanked him, and meant it, and stepped back out into the bright, deceptively ordinary morning with Caleb beside her, the feed store's bell ringing once behind them as the door swung shut.

"He's risking more than he's saying," Caleb said quietly, once they were back at the truck.

"I know." Mara looked back at the store, at Yusuf's silhouette already visible through the window, phone to his ear. "I think that's exactly why I trust him."
