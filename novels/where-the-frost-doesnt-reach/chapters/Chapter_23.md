# CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE

*Seven weeks after the funeral*

By the second week after the confrontation at Kell's house, the story going around Amity Falls was that Mara Voss had come back from the city with a chip on her shoulder and a grudge against a good man, and that grief over her grandmother had apparently curdled into something that made her drag Caleb Reyes, poor Caleb, still not himself, into wild accusations against Ambrose Kell of all people. Mara could trace the story's spread almost the way she'd trace a load path through a building, one small point of contact at a time, a comment here, a raised eyebrow there, until the whole structure of it stood upright without anyone ever quite admitting who'd poured the foundation. It was, she thought, a remarkably efficient piece of engineering in its own right, built entirely out of plausible deniability and the town's well-practiced fondness for tidy explanations.

Mara heard the shape of it first from the woman at the pharmacy counter, who went quiet and overly polite the second she recognized her, ringing up a bottle of aspirin with the studied concentration of someone determined not to make eye contact for even a second longer than the transaction required, and then more directly from her own cousin Denise, who called to ask, with careful concern, whether Mara was doing okay, really okay, because people were saying things.

"What things," Mara said, though she already knew, the phone pressed to her ear while she stood in her grandmother's kitchen doing dishes she didn't strictly need to do, the repetitive motion of it the only thing keeping her hands from shaking with something closer to anger than hurt.

"That you're not, you know. Handling grandma's death well. That you're seeing conspiracies." Denise's voice dropped lower, apologetic, the particular tone of someone relaying gossip she felt guilty for having heard at all. "I'm not saying I believe it. I just wanted you to hear it from me instead of the diner."

Odette, when Mara stopped by the store that afternoon, confirmed the rest of it without being asked, her hands moving through the motions of restocking a shelf with a steadiness that felt deliberate, a woman who had clearly decided somewhere in the last two weeks that usefulness was the only apology she had left to offer, the same instinct Mara now understood a little better after their conversation about Thomas. "He's good," she said, restocking a shelf with more force than the cans strictly required, the metal clinking together with a sharpness that punctuated her sentence. "I watched him do this exact thing to a county inspector fifteen years ago who asked one too many questions about a drainage permit. Within a week the whole town believed the inspector had a drinking problem. He never did."

"So what do we do? We can't fight a story with just the truth if nobody wants to hear it." Mara felt the particular frustration of a person accustomed to solving problems that responded to evidence, standing now in front of a problem that clearly didn't care about evidence at all.

"You don't fight the story." Odette set the last can down and looked at Mara directly, the store's fluorescent lights catching the tired lines around her eyes in a way the softer light of the porch never had. "You get proof he can't spin. Something physical. Something that isn't a nineteen year old girl's shaky account or your grandfather's forty year old suspicions." Her eyes were steady, and something in them had shifted since her own confession the week before, a woman done apologizing and ready to be useful instead. "You're an engineer. You understand load and pressure and things that fail in predictable ways. That weather station out past the mill, the one that's supposedly reading conditions for the county extension office. Nobody's serviced it in years except Kell himself. He insisted on it personally, said it was a hobby."

Mara felt something click into place, the particular satisfaction of finding the actual crack instead of guessing at where one might be, a feeling she recognized from a hundred inspections where a stray detail suddenly explained everything else that hadn't been sitting right. "You think he's tampering with the readings."

"I think a man who's been faking weather emergencies for decades needs a way to make sure the official record backs up whatever story he's telling. I think if you got inside that station and found evidence it's been rigged to report frost warnings that don't match the actual temperature on the ground, you'd have something even Ambrose Kell can't talk his way around."

---

She told Caleb that evening, out at the fence line where they'd first really talked, the crooked apple tree throwing long shadows in the last of the light, its branches heavier now with fruit than they'd been the last time she'd stood here, the ordinary business of the orchard continuing underneath everything else that had happened. There was something almost absurd, she thought, in how normal the world insisted on looking even now, apples ripening on schedule, the same birds calling from the same trees, as if the valley itself hadn't noticed anything was wrong.

"It's breaking and entering," he said, not arguing so much as making sure she'd thought it through, his eyes steady on hers in a way that felt like a test she wanted to pass. "Or close enough to it that a friendly judge could call it that if Kell decides to press it."

"I know."

"And if you're wrong, if it's just an old station and nothing's been tampered with, we've handed him exactly the story he's already telling about us. Reckless. Paranoid. Willing to break the law to chase a theory."

"I know that too." Mara looked at him, the sun catching the scar through his eyebrow, and thought that a week ago this man hadn't known her name and now he was the person she trusted most to tell her honestly when a plan was bad. "I'm not asking you to come. This is mine to risk."

"No." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said most things that mattered. "You're not doing this alone. Not because I don't think you can handle it. Because I don't want you to have to."

Something in Mara's chest gave way, quietly, the specific give of a wall she hadn't realized she'd still been holding up, a small structural surrender that felt, for once, like relief instead of failure.

"Okay," she said. "Tonight. Before he thinks to check it himself."

They spent the last hour of daylight going over what they'd need, sitting on the porch steps while the orchard settled into evening around them, Caleb sketching the mill road from memory onto the back of a feed receipt, marking the rise where the station sat and the treeline that would give them cover on the approach. Mara found herself grateful, watching him work, for the particular kind of competence that came from a man who'd spent his whole life reading land the way she read blueprints, noticing sightlines and cover and the places a truck's headlights would sweep before a driver ever consciously chose to look there.

"There's a gate about here," he said, tapping the paper, "chained but not locked properly, hasn't been in years. We can leave the truck on the old logging turnout and walk the last quarter mile, keeps the engine noise off the ridge."

"You've thought about this before tonight."

"I've thought about a lot of things involving that man's property since the night at his house." His jaw tightened briefly, the memory of Wren's shaking hands still close enough to reach for. "Never had a good enough reason before now."

Mara felt something settle in her at that, the quiet certainty of a plan built by two people who trusted each other's judgment in a crisis, a trust that had been earned in increments over five hard weeks rather than assumed. She packed a small bag while the light faded, a headlamp, a multitool, the same slim screwdriver set she used for inspection work, the tools of her actual profession pressed into service for something considerably less legal than anything she'd signed her name to before.

"Last chance to back out," she said, half joking, zipping the bag closed.

"Not happening." Caleb was already reaching for his truck keys, his voice steady, certain, the particular calm of a man who'd made his decision hours ago and saw no reason to revisit it now. "Let's go find out what he's hiding out there."
