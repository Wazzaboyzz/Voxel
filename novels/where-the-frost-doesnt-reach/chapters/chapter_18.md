# CHAPTER EIGHTEEN

They found Wren at the store the next morning, restocking a shelf of canned goods with Odette a few feet away, and Mara watched the girl's face light up at the sight of them before catching, almost immediately, on a grimness none of them had managed to fully shake off since the silo.

"What happened," Wren asked, setting down the can in her hand. "You all look like you've been up all night."

"We need to talk somewhere private," Priya said, and something in her voice, careful and deliberate, made Odette look up sharply from the register.

"My office," Odette said, already moving to unlock the back room. "Whatever this is, it sounds like it needs a door that closes."

The back room smelled the way it always did, cardboard and old coffee and the faint mustiness of inventory nobody had gotten around to sorting properly in months, and Mara found the ordinariness of it almost jarring against the weight of what they'd come to say. Wren perched on her usual stool, the same one she'd sat on the day she'd first offered to help, and Mara felt a fresh wave of protectiveness looking at her there, so plainly still a teenager despite everything this summer had already asked of her.

They gathered around the small desk, Wren perched on a stool with growing unease as Priya laid out the shape of it, careful and measured, sparing no detail that mattered and sparing Wren nothing about the danger itself, though she softened, slightly, the specifics of Dev's four missing years, leaving that fuller grief for a moment that belonged more properly to Priya alone.

"He's been grooming young Finders," Priya said finally. "Small favors first, building obligation slowly. Dev thinks you might already be on his list."

Wren went very still, the color draining slowly from her face. "He asked me to help him find something two weeks ago. A missing document, he said, nothing important, just misplaced paperwork from a council meeting. I found it in maybe ten minutes. He thanked me so warmly I felt good about it for the rest of the day."

"That's exactly the pattern Dev described," Mara said gently. "Small, harmless-seeming asks, building trust and a sense of obligation before he ever asks for something that actually costs you anything."

"There was another time too," Wren said slowly, the memory surfacing now that she had a framework to hang it on. "About a month before that. He asked me to help locate an old property deed, said the county records office had misfiled it and he needed it for a boundary dispute. I remember feeling so proud of myself for finding it faster than he expected. He gave me twenty dollars I didn't ask for and told me I had a gift the whole valley should be grateful for."

"Two asks, at least," Priya said, jotting a note despite herself, old habits surfacing even in a conversation that had nothing to do with an official report. "Probably more we don't know about yet."

"I thought I was just being helpful." Wren's voice had gone thin with the shame of realizing, after the fact, that kindness had been quietly weaponized against her own good nature. "I thought it made me useful. Important, even. He made it feel like being asked meant something good about me."

"It does mean something good about you," Caleb said firmly. "That's exactly why it worked. He doesn't target people who don't care about being helpful. He targets the ones who do, because that's the lever he knows how to pull."

Odette, who had been listening from the doorway with her arms crossed, finally spoke, something tight and unreadable in her expression. "I should have seen this coming sooner. I've watched that man operate for forty years. I know exactly what the early stages look like, and I still didn't put it together until you all walked in here looking like you'd seen a ghost."

"None of us saw it until Dev told us directly," Mara said, though she noted, filing it away without comment, a defensiveness in Odette's voice sharper than the moment strictly required.

"I've known Ambrose Kell since before any of you were born," Odette went on, something old and tired in her voice now. "I've watched him charm this whole valley for four decades while quietly making sure nobody ever had quite enough proof to challenge him properly. I hate that I still find myself second-guessing what I actually saw versus what he wanted me to see, even now, even knowing what I know."

"That's not a failing," Priya said. "That's what forty years of a skilled manipulator does to everyone around him. None of us should feel foolish for missing what he specifically engineered to be missable."

"What do I do now," Wren asked, looking between all of them. "Do I just, what, avoid him? Refuse every request from now on? That seems like it would just tell him I know something's wrong."

"That's exactly the problem," Priya said. "If you suddenly go cold on him, he'll notice, and noticing might accelerate whatever timeline he's already working on instead of buying us more room to build a real case against him."

"So I keep helping him," Wren said slowly, understanding arriving with visible dread. "I keep letting him think I don't know anything, while all of you figure out how to actually stop him."

"Only small things," Caleb said immediately. "Nothing that costs you anything real. The moment he asks for something bigger, something that feels like it might actually take from you the way it took from Dev, you refuse, and you call one of us before you even finish saying no to him."

"How will I know the difference," Wren asked. "Between a small ask and a dangerous one. Everything he's asked so far has felt small right up until Dev told me what it actually was building toward."

Mara considered the question seriously, understanding it deserved more than easy reassurance. "Trust the feeling in your body more than the feeling in your head. Dev said the asks felt harmless because Kell made sure they felt harmless. But your body might know something your conscious mind hasn't caught up to yet. If something feels wrong even slightly, even if you can't articulate why, that's the moment to stop and call one of us, not after you've talked yourself out of the feeling."

"That's actually good advice," Priya said, surprised.

"I spent a career learning to trust exactly that kind of instinct on job sites," Mara said. "The feeling that something's wrong before you can point to the specific crack. It's saved more lives than waiting for concrete proof ever has."

Wren nodded, though her hands had begun to tremble slightly against the edge of the desk, the fear of a nineteen year old being asked to walk deliberately closer to a danger everyone else in the room understood far better than she did.

"I'm scared," she admitted, quiet enough that Mara had to lean in to catch it fully.

"You're allowed to be," Mara said. "Being scared and doing the careful thing anyway isn't a contradiction. It's just what courage actually looks like most of the time."

"I keep thinking about Dev," Wren said. "Four years in that man's shadow, and it started exactly the way this is starting with me. Small favors. Feeling useful. I don't want to end up wherever he ended up."

"You won't." Caleb's voice carried a certainty Mara understood came from somewhere deeper than simple reassurance, a man who had personally paid the price of this exact pattern and refused to watch it repeat on someone he'd come to care about. "Because this time, we know what it looks like before it finishes happening. Dev didn't have that. You do."

Wren managed a small, unsteady nod, drawing some visible strength from the certainty in his voice, and Mara watched the fear in her settle into something more manageable, not gone, but no longer overwhelming.

Odette moved to Wren's side, resting a hand on her shoulder with a tenderness that seemed, to Mara's eye, almost performed, though she couldn't immediately say why the gesture struck her that way. "I'll keep a closer eye on her here at the store. If Kell comes around asking for her specifically, I'll know within the hour."

"Thank you," Priya said, and Mara watched something pass between the two older women, an old, complicated history neither of them fully explained.

The conversation wound down slowly after that, Wren asking careful, practical questions about what she should say if Kell approached her again that afternoon, Priya walking her through a few plausible, harmless responses that would neither arouse suspicion nor commit her to anything real. Mara found herself watching Odette throughout, unable to fully shake the small, nagging unease that had settled in during the older woman's earlier words, though she couldn't have named exactly what about them had struck her wrong.

"You'll call if anything feels off," Caleb said to Wren, as they finally rose to leave, "even something small. Especially something small."

"I will." Wren managed a shaky smile. "Thank you. For not just telling me to run and hide and let everyone else handle it. I don't think I could stand that right now."

"We know," Mara said. "That's exactly why we didn't."

They left the store a little past noon, the plan settled if not entirely comfortable, and it was only once they were back in the truck that Mara voiced the small, nagging unease she'd been carrying since Odette's hand landed on Wren's shoulder.

"Did that feel strange to either of you? Odette's reaction back there."

Caleb glanced at her, surprised. "Strange how?"

"I don't know exactly. Too quick to volunteer watching Wren. Too certain about knowing Kell's patterns for someone who claims she only just now put the pieces together." Mara turned it over, feeling the shape of a doubt she didn't want to be having about a woman who had, up to this point, been one of the steadiest presences in this entire summer. "I could be wrong. I hope I'm wrong."

"You've been right about worse odds than this before," Priya said grimly from the back seat. "Let's not rule anything out yet. Not even people we want to trust."

The truck rolled on toward the orchard, the day gone gray and heavy overhead in a way that no longer felt like ordinary weather to any of them, and Mara found herself watching the rearview mirror more than the road ahead, some old instinct insisting, quietly but persistently, that they were being watched by more than just the man they already knew to fear.

"We should tell Dev what we decided," Caleb said, breaking the silence as they turned onto the orchard road. "See if he remembers anything else about how Kell approached him at the start. Any detail might tell us how much time we actually have before this escalates past small favors."

"I'll ask him tonight," Priya said. "Gently. He's still fragile, and I don't want to turn every conversation with my own brother into an interrogation just because we're in a hurry."

"Understood." Mara looked back at the store shrinking in the side mirror, thinking of Wren's small, determined nod, the bravery of a teenager choosing to walk directly toward danger because the alternative, waiting passively for it to arrive, felt worse. "I just hope we're faster than he is. He's had forty years of practice being patient. We've had about a week."

"Then we make the week count," Caleb said, his hands steady on the wheel despite everything, the resolve of a man who had already lost two years to this exact kind of patience and had no intention of losing anything else to it without a fight.

Mara let the silence settle over the cab for a while after that, watching the orchard rows blur past the window, gold and heavy with the last of the season's fruit, ordinary and beautiful in a way that felt almost defiant against everything they now understood was moving underneath this valley's calm surface. She found herself thinking of Wren's face when Caleb had promised her this time would be different, the fragile hope of someone choosing to believe a thing because the alternative was unbearable, and hoped, fiercely, that they would manage to deserve that hope rather than simply borrow against it.

"We're going to need more than good intentions," she said finally. "We need the ledger Dev mentioned. We need a plan for Wren that doesn't depend entirely on her own nerve holding steady under pressure. And we need to figure out, soon, whether Odette's help is exactly what it looks like or something we should be watching more carefully."

"One thing at a time," Priya said from the back seat, though her voice carried the same underlying unease Mara had been sitting with all morning. "Tonight, Dev. Tomorrow, everything else."

Caleb turned into the orchard drive, the house coming into view through the trees, and for a moment none of them said anything else, each of them alone with their own private math of exactly how much time they thought they actually had left before Kell's patience ran out and forced all of their hands at once.
