# CHAPTER FIVE

Rosewood Care was the kind of facility that tried very hard to not feel like what it was, framed watercolors in the hallways, a fish tank in the lobby that a laminated sign explained was "Ernest," a smell of industrial cleaner fighting a losing battle against something underneath it that no amount of lemon-scented anything could fully cover. Mara had visited enough construction sites disguised as something friendlier, model homes built to sell a feeling rather than a floor plan, to recognize the same instinct here, and it didn't make her feel any better about walking down the hall to her grandfather's room.

Thomas Voss was sitting by the window when she came in, an oxygen line looped over his ears, a blanket over legs that had stopped fully obeying him sometime in the last year according to the notes Mara had skimmed in the car. He looked smaller than she remembered, the way people did once illness got done rearranging them, but his eyes when they found her were exactly as sharp as they'd ever been, sharper, if anything, like everything the rest of him had lost had been redirected there instead.

"You came," he said. Not a question. Not quite a statement either. Something in between, weighing the fact of her presence like it told him something.

"Of course I came." Mara pulled the second chair closer, the vinyl cushion creaking under her. "How are you feeling?"

"Don't do that." His voice was thin but not weak, a man conserving effort rather than lacking it. "You didn't drive out here to ask me how I'm feeling. You drove out here because your grandmother's dead and you found something in that house that scared you, and you want to know if I'm well enough to explain it before I'm not around to ask."

Mara felt the directness of it land somewhere just under her ribs, an old, familiar sensation, that unsettled feeling of being read accurately by someone who'd known her before she'd learned how to control what she showed people.

"I found a photograph," she said, deciding there was no version of this conversation where dancing around it would serve either of them well. "You and Grandma. On the back it says, the year everything went wrong, and somehow also right. You said something like that to me yesterday. About me looking like her, that year."

Thomas's hand, resting on the arm of his chair, tightened slightly, the knuckles going paler against skin already gone thin and spotted with age. "Nineteen eighty-six," he said, and something in his voice had changed, gone careful in a way she recognized now, the same careful she'd heard from Odette that morning, the town's shared, practiced tone for approaching something dangerous sideways instead of head-on. "Bad year. Bad in a way this town doesn't like to talk about, even now, even to family."

"What happened?"

"Not today." He said it gently, but there was no give in it, an old man drawing a line he had clearly drawn before and intended to hold. "I'm not being cruel, Mara. I'm being careful, and there's a difference, even if it doesn't always feel like one from where you're sitting." His eyes moved to the window, to the parking lot beyond it, to nothing in particular. "Some things, once you say them out loud, you can't take back the saying. I need to be sure you're ready to carry it before I hand it to you. I wasn't, the first time I learned it. It cost me things I never got back."

"Like what?"

Thomas didn't answer that either, and the silence that followed had a different texture than his usual careful evasions, heavier, older, the silence of a man revisiting a specific loss rather than simply withholding information.

"How's Caleb," he said instead, and the change of subject was so abrupt Mara nearly missed the way his eyes had sharpened on her face while he asked it, watching for something.

"He didn't know me," Mara said, before she could decide whether to say it at all. "At the funeral. Grandpa, he looked at me like a stranger."

Something crossed her grandfather's face, and Mara, who had spent a career learning to read the small, involuntary signs a structure gave before it failed, recognized it immediately as fear, quickly and deliberately suppressed, an old man forcing his features back into careful neutrality by an act of will she could see the cost of.

"You should be careful around that boy," he said finally, and his voice had gone lower, quieter, an urgency underneath the frailty that made the hair on Mara's arms rise. "Not because of him. Because of what happened to him. I don't have proof, not the kind that would hold up anywhere that matters, but I have suspicions I've been sitting on for longer than I care to admit, and I think whatever's wrong with that young man's memory isn't the kind of wrong that happens by accident."

"What do you mean?"

Thomas shook his head, a small, tired motion, and Mara watched him retreat back behind the careful wall he'd built, the effort of the last few minutes visibly draining something out of him that his body couldn't spare.

"I've said more than I meant to already," he said. "Come back next week. I'll have had time to decide how much of this I can hand you without it costing you the way it cost me." His eyes found hers again, steady despite the exhaustion evident in every other part of him. "In the meantime. Watch him. Watch this whole town. And Mara, whatever you find, whatever anyone tells you, trust the parts you can actually verify with your own hands over the parts you're just being told. That's always been your gift, long before you knew to call it that. Don't let this place talk you out of using it."

The home aide appeared in the doorway a moment later, a broad-shouldered woman with kind, tired eyes, and gave Mara the small, apologetic look of someone whose job required interrupting things that mattered.

"He needs his rest," she said, and Thomas didn't argue, which told Mara, more clearly than anything he'd said, exactly how much the conversation had cost him.

She drove back to the orchard house in the fading light with her grandfather's words circling in her mind, trust the parts you can actually verify with your own hands, and found herself, for the first time since arriving in Amity Falls, genuinely afraid of what she might eventually verify.

---

Del caught her in the parking lot before she reached her car, jogging a little to close the distance, a manila folder tucked under one arm.

"Ms. Voss." She glanced back toward the building once, checking, the way you would when a conversation might be about to be observed. "I don't usually do this. Overstep, I mean. But your grandfather's a good man, and he doesn't get many visitors who ask him real questions instead of just talking at him about the weather."

"Is he alright? I didn't mean to tire him out."

"He's fine. Or as fine as he gets these days." Del hesitated, weighing something. "He talks in his sleep sometimes. Has for the eight months I've been with him. Names, mostly. Nothing that ever made sense to me until you walked in today and he lit up like I hadn't seen him do in weeks." She held out the folder. "This isn't anything official. Just some papers he asked me to keep somewhere safe a while back, before his hands got too unsteady to trust with anything that mattered. He never told me what they were, just said if his granddaughter ever came around asking the right kind of questions, I should probably let her see them before I let anyone else."

Mara took the folder carefully, feeling the slight weight of it, paper gone soft and dense from being handled many times over many years. "Why give this to me now? I only asked him one real question."

"Because it was the right one." Del's eyes were steady, kind, tired the way people get from spending their days holding other people's fear so they didn't have to carry all of it themselves. "He's been waiting a long time for somebody to ask about nineteen eighty-six instead of just humoring an old man's confusion. I think some part of him's been afraid he'd die before anybody did."

She walked back toward the building before Mara could ask anything else, leaving her standing in the parking lot with a folder she hadn't earned yet, by her grandfather's own reckoning, but had apparently been given anyway.

She didn't open it in the car. She told herself it was because the light was fading and she wanted to read it properly, under a real lamp, with enough attention to give it, and she mostly believed that too, the same way she'd mostly believed the photograph in her bag was only sentiment.

The drive back to the orchard house took longer than it should have, not because of traffic, there wasn't any, but because some part of her kept slowing down at every turn, some old animal instinct warning her that whatever was in that folder was going to be the kind of weight that, once picked up, could not be easily set back down.

---

Back at the house, she made herself dinner she barely tasted, a can of soup heated more out of obligation to her own body than any real hunger, and set the folder on the kitchen table while she ate, looking at it the way she might look at an inspection report she suspected contained bad news, reluctant the way you get when you've learned, professionally, that delaying the reading never actually changes what's written inside.

She opened it once the dishes were done, because there was no longer a reasonable excuse left to delay it with.

Inside were newspaper clippings, yellowed and brittle at the folds, several from the local paper dated across a span of months in 1986, headlines about an unusual drought, about crop failures the county extension office called unprecedented, about a town meeting where, according to one clipping, tempers had run high enough that the sheriff had been called to keep order. None of the articles mentioned anything supernatural, of course, nothing that would have made sense to an outsider reading them cold. But underneath the clippings, in a separate, smaller stack held together with a rubber band gone brittle with age, were pages in her grandfather's handwriting, younger and steadier than the hand that had spoken to her that afternoon, dense with notes and cross-references and a timeline that took up two full pages by itself.

She didn't read all of it that night. She made herself stop after the first page, not because she wanted to, but because some careful, disciplined part of her recognized the shape of what she was looking at, understood that this was the kind of document that needed to be read properly, with a clear head and enough time to actually absorb what it was telling her, not skimmed at eleven at night by someone running on grief and adrenaline and a can of soup.

She put the folder in the same drawer where she'd found the ring, which struck her, closing it, as either an accident or the kind of decision her hands had made before her mind caught up to why, the two most significant objects in this entire investigation now sitting an inch apart in the dark, waiting for her to be ready for both of them.

She lay awake a long time that night, listening to a house that had never once, in her whole childhood, felt like it was holding its breath, and understood, somewhere around two in the morning, that it was doing exactly that now, and had probably been doing it for longer than she'd been alive to notice.
