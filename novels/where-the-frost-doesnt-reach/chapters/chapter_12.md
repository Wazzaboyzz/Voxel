# CHAPTER TWELVE

The dog showed up on a Thursday, which Mara would remember later mostly because it was the first ordinary day either of them had gotten in weeks, no folder to open, no grandfather to visit with careful questions, just a morning of sorting through the last of her grandmother's linens and an afternoon that had nothing scheduled in it at all. She'd woken that morning without the low, familiar hum of dread that had greeted her every day since the funeral, and had lain there for a full minute simply noticing its absence, the way you might notice a headache had finally lifted only once you realized you could think clearly again.

She heard the barking before she saw anything, a rangy, uncertain sound coming from somewhere near the property line, and found Caleb crouched at the edge of his own driveway with a scrawny brown mutt keeping a full car length of distance between them, ribs a little too visible under a coat the color of weak coffee, one ear standing up and the other flopped over like it had given up on the arrangement years ago.

"She's been circling the orchard for two days," Caleb said, not looking up, his voice pitched low and even, the gentleness of a man trying not to spook something that had clearly already been spooked plenty. "I put food out yesterday. She ate it after I went inside. Won't come any closer than this."

"No collar?"

"None. No chip, either, I had Priya scan her this morning when she came by." He held out a hand, palm down, patient, and the dog took one cautious step forward before thinking better of it and sitting back on her haunches at exactly the same distance as before. "I think somebody dropped her off out on the county road. Happens more than people want to admit, out here. Somebody can't afford to feed a dog anymore, they figure an orchard's as good a place as any to leave one and hope."

Mara crouched down beside him, slower than he had, feeling the dog's dark eyes track the movement with the same wary attention she imagined she'd have given anything unpredictable herself, this last miserable summer. "She's thin."

"Thin, but not sick, best I can tell. Skittish is the bigger problem." Caleb's hand stayed exactly where it was, patient, unhurried, and something about watching him do it, the same steady patience she'd watched him bring to a fence hinge or a stubborn engine, made her chest do something complicated. "I figure if I just keep being boring and predictable long enough, she'll decide I'm not worth being afraid of anymore."

"That's a very specific philosophy for a dog."

"It's a philosophy for most things, in my experience." He glanced sideways at her, something dry surfacing in his voice even now. "Works on skittish orchard managers too, or so I've been told."

Mara laughed before she could stop herself, the sound surprising her with how easy it came, and the dog's ears went up at the noise, considering it, before deciding it wasn't a threat either.

They stayed there another twenty minutes, neither of them saying much, the morning going warm and gold around them the way September mornings in this valley always seemed to, birds working through the nearest row of trees with the unhurried business of a season not yet ready to turn, until the dog finally closed the last few feet on her own terms and pressed her nose, briefly, suspiciously, against the back of Caleb's still-patient hand.

"There," he said quietly, like he'd won something considerably larger than a dog's trust. "There we go."

---

She stayed. That was the whole of the decision, in the end, no dramatic moment of choosing, just a dog who ate on the porch that evening and didn't leave afterward, curling up under the kitchen table like she'd always belonged there, and Caleb feeding her again the next morning without either of them discussing whether that made her his now or simply a fixture of the property the way the crooked apple tree was.

"She needs a name," Mara said, on the third morning, watching the dog watch Caleb move around the kitchen with the same careful, assessing patience she'd used on him the first day, still deciding, it seemed, whether trust was a permanent arrangement or one that needed re-earning daily.

"I've been thinking about that." Caleb set down two mugs of coffee, sliding one across to Mara with the ease of a habit that had settled into place faster than either of them talked about out loud. "Toll."

Mara felt the word land oddly, given everything they now knew, given the whole shape of what her grandfather had spent a hospital room's worth of breath explaining to them both. "That's a strange thing to name a dog, considering."

"I know. That's sort of the point." He crouched down, scratching behind the dog's crooked ear, and she leaned into it with her whole scrawny body, tail thumping once against the floor. "Everybody in this town's spent two years treating the word like something to whisper around me. I figure if I can say it every day, calling a dog in for dinner, it stops being able to hurt as much. Takes the teeth out of it a little."

Mara watched him for a long moment, understanding, not for the first time this week, that this man had built an entire private philosophy out of taking the things that had been used to diminish him and finding some quiet, stubborn way of making them ordinary instead. "That's either the healthiest thing I've ever heard, or slightly unsettling."

"Probably some of both." He straightened, and Toll immediately pressed her whole weight against his leg, apparently having decided, in the space of three days, exactly who she belonged to now. "You have a better name?"

"No." Mara crouched down too, and Toll's dark eyes moved to her, considering, before she allowed a single cautious sniff of Mara's offered hand. "Toll's fine. Better than fine, actually. I think I like it."

---

The days that followed fell into a rhythm neither of them named out loud but both seemed to be building toward, deliberately or not. Mara spent her mornings at her grandfather's desk, working through what remained of Eleanor's estate, the ordinary, unglamorous business of closing out a life, insurance forms and property deeds and a lawyer's careful, patient emails about probate timelines that had nothing supernatural in them at all. Caleb spent his running the orchard, the last stretch before harvest demanding attention Mara understood better now than she had at the start of the summer, having spent enough afternoons walking the rows with him to know the difference between a healthy tree and one that needed watching. She found, more than once, that she liked watching him work almost as much as she liked helping, the competence of a man moving through land he knew in his bones even when the rest of his history had been quietly taken from him.

Toll came everywhere, unhurried, entirely certain of her welcome now, and there was something in her presence, Mara thought more than once, that kept the whole valley's slow-building dread from swallowing every ordinary hour whole. A dog didn't care about tolls or bargains or forty-year-old cover-ups. A dog cared about dinner, and a scratch behind the ears, and whichever patch of porch caught the most afternoon sun, and having something that simple in the house made the rest of it, somehow, easier to carry.

"I keep waiting for this to feel strange," Mara admitted one evening, the three of them, Caleb and Toll and herself, sitting on the porch step while the light went long and gold over the orchard rows. "Being here. Doing this. Ordinary evenings with a dog and dinner and no crisis waiting in the next hour."

"Does it feel strange?"

"No." She said it slowly, testing the honesty of it as she said it. "That's the strange part. It doesn't. It feels like something I could have had eight years ago, if things had gone differently, and I don't know what to do with how easily it's fitting now, given everything else."

Caleb was quiet for a moment, his hand finding Toll's head where she'd settled between them, scratching absently. "Maybe it's not supposed to feel complicated all the time. Maybe some of it's just allowed to be simple, even with everything else going on underneath it."

"You sound very sure of that."

"I'm not sure of much these days. But I've decided that's one thing I'm willing to just believe without proof." He glanced over at her, something steady and unhurried in his expression that she was starting to recognize as entirely, uncomplicatedly his, no borrowed memory required to produce it. "You. Toll. Ordinary evenings. I'd like those to just be allowed to be good, without me needing to justify it against everything else this valley's handing us."

Mara let herself lean into that, the porch step solid under her, Caleb's shoulder warm against hers, Toll's steady breathing the only sound besides the crickets starting up in the grass beyond the fence. She thought of her grandfather, of Elias Frey, of a whole valley's worth of careful, accumulated fear, and understood that none of it had to be diminished by allowing herself, for one evening, to simply enjoy something uncomplicated. There was a kind of discipline in that too, she was learning, a discipline no different from the one she'd spent her whole career practicing on job sites, the ability to hold two true things at once without letting the heavier one crush the lighter, the danger real and the good evening equally real, neither one required to cancel the other out.

"Okay," she said finally. "Then let's let this be good. Just for tonight."

They sat there until the light went fully out, neither of them in any hurry to be anywhere else, Toll's crooked ear twitching occasionally at some sound only she could hear, and Mara found herself thinking, watching the first stars come out over the orchard rows, that whatever the valley still had waiting for them, she was glad, at least, to be facing it from somewhere that had already started to feel, unmistakably, like home.

The knock on the door came just after nine, hesitant, uncertain, and Toll's head came up sharply from where she'd been dozing against Caleb's leg, a low, uncertain whine building in her throat, some old animal instinct reading the visitor's nervousness before either of the humans had even reached the door.

Wren Castellano stood on the porch when Caleb opened it, a bicycle abandoned in the grass behind her, her hands shoved deep in her jacket pockets, and something in her face, pale and unsettled in the porch light, told Mara immediately that whatever ordinary evening they'd just allowed themselves was already, quietly, over. Toll pressed herself against Mara's leg, ears still flat, and Mara found herself, for the first time in days, doing the old, automatic arithmetic of a woman calculating exactly how much worse the next hour was about to get.
