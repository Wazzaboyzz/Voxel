# CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN

The partial win should have felt like more than it did. Mara understood that, intellectually, turning the board's decision over in her mind for the third day running while she walked the orchard rows alone in the early evening, Toll trotting a companionable distance ahead of her, the dog having apparently decided sometime in the past week that Mara's solitary walks required an escort even when Caleb wasn't the one taking them. An independent investigation. Kell recused from the very areas of council business he'd used to run his forty-year theft. It was real progress, the kind her grandfather had spent decades working toward without ever once achieving it, and some days she could feel the size of that clearly enough to be proud of it.

Other days, like this one, all she could feel was the hollowness of a victory she couldn't fully celebrate with the one person she most wanted beside her for it.

Caleb had shown up at the hearing. He'd held her hand in that hallway, however briefly, however uncertainly. But in the four days since, the distance had settled back into something close to what it had been before, not cold exactly, not a retreat from the tentative ground they'd found in that fifteen-minute recess, but careful in a way that told her he was still working through something she couldn't rush him past no matter how much she wanted to.

She found him at the fence line on the fourth evening, the same stretch of fence where they'd first really talked back in the early days of summer, and something about seeing him there, in the exact spot where all of this had started, made her chest ache with a complicated tenderness she hadn't expected.

"I keep coming back here," he said, before she could ask, not turning from where he stood looking out over the rows. "I don't know why. It's not like I remember what it meant before. But something about standing here feels like the closest thing I have to solid ground right now."

"I remember what it meant," Mara said quietly, coming to stand beside him, close but not touching, matching the careful distance he seemed to still need. "If it helps to hear it. This is where you first really talked to me again, this summer. Not the funeral, not the store, not any of the polite, careful exchanges everyone in this valley seems to specialize in. Here. You asked me an honest question and waited for an honest answer instead of the easy one."

"What did I ask?"

"Whether I actually wanted to stay, or whether I was just here because grief hadn't given me anywhere else to be yet." Mara watched the last of the light catch the top branches of the nearest trees, gold going slowly to grey. "It was the first time all week anybody had asked me something that actually mattered instead of just offering condolences shaped like small talk."

Caleb was quiet for a long moment, processing that, and when he spoke again his voice had gone rougher, more uncertain than she'd heard it since the office conversation. "I've been trying to figure out how to tell you where I've actually landed, this past week, without it sounding like I'm still making you wait for an answer you deserve to have already."

"You don't owe me a deadline on this."

"I know. That's not why it's hard." He turned to face her fully now, the careful distance between them still intact but his eyes steady on hers in a way that told her whatever came next had cost him real effort to arrive at. "I spent the hearing watching you fight for something that mattered, and I realized, sitting there three rows back like a coward, that I'd been treating my own doubt like it was the only honest thing left in me. Like doubting you was somehow more trustworthy than trusting you, just because doubt felt like the thing I'd arrived at on my own instead of something handed to me."

"That's not cowardice," Mara said. "That's just fear wearing a more respectable coat."

"Maybe. But here's what I actually figured out, standing at this fence for the better part of an hour before you found me." He reached for her hand then, finally, deliberately, no longer testing the ground the way he had in the hallway, but choosing it outright. "I don't need the old memory back to know something true about right now. I know, without anyone else's word to back it up, that watching you walk into that hearing room and fight for something bigger than either of us is the single most certain I've felt about anything since I woke up in a life I didn't recognize. That's not secondhand. That's mine. I built it myself, this summer, one ordinary day at a time, and I don't think I need forty years of history to trust something I watched myself choose with my own eyes open."

Mara felt something in her chest, held carefully guarded for eleven days now, finally begin to loosen, the plain relief of watching a structure she'd feared permanently compromised prove, instead, simply slower to settle than she'd hoped. "So where does that leave us."

"It leaves me asking if you'll have me back. Not because the fear is gone. I don't think it's ever fully going to be gone, not with two years still missing and Kell still out there able to twist it against me whenever it suits him. But because I've decided the fear doesn't get to be the only voice in the room anymore. I trust you. I trust what I've built with you these past two months, on my own terms, with my own eyes. That has to be enough, because waiting for some perfect, unshakeable certainty that never wavers isn't actually how trust works for anyone, memory intact or not."

"No," Mara agreed, her voice thick now, "it isn't. I've never once had that kind of certainty either, Caleb. Not with you eight years ago, not with anyone since. Trust isn't the absence of fear. It's choosing someone anyway, every single day, even on the days the fear gets loud." She stepped closer, finally closing the careful distance they'd both been holding, and felt him exhale, long and unsteady, as though he'd been holding that breath for the better part of eleven days. "I'd rather have this version of us. The one that had to work for it, that knows exactly what it almost lost. I think it's stronger than whatever we would have had if Kell had never found a crack to press on in the first place."

He pulled her in then, slow and certain, his forehead resting against hers the way it had that night under the crooked apple tree, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all, the orchard settling into evening around them, Toll circling once before flopping down contentedly in the grass nearby, entirely satisfied with however this reunion had resolved itself.

"I missed this," Caleb said finally, quiet, his hand coming up to rest against the side of her face. "More than I let myself admit, these past eleven days. I kept telling myself the distance was necessary. It was. I still think it was. But it was also the loneliest I've felt since I woke up not knowing who any of you were."

"I missed you too." Mara closed her eyes, letting herself simply feel the warmth of him after eleven days of careful, deliberate space. "For what it's worth, I don't think the distance was wasted. You came back to me with an answer you actually built yourself, instead of one you just borrowed to make the discomfort stop faster. That matters more than getting here quickly would have."

"Practical, even now."

"I'm an engineer. I like things built to actually hold weight, not just look sturdy in good weather." She felt him laugh, low and warm against her, the first easy sound she'd heard from him in nearly two weeks, and thought that whatever came next, whatever Kell still had left to throw at them, they were meeting it now as two people who had chosen each other on purpose, with open eyes, rather than two people simply carried along by a current neither of them had ever fully examined.

They walked back toward the house together as full dark settled over the orchard, Toll trailing behind them, unhurried, and Mara found herself thinking that this, too, was its own kind of victory, quieter than the hearing, harder won in some ways, but no less real for how little of the valley would ever know it had happened at all.
