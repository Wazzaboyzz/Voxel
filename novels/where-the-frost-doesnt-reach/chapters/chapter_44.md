# CHAPTER FORTY-FOUR

Grief and relief turned out to be closer neighbors than Mara had ever expected, sharing a fence line the way Voss land shared one with Reyes land, close enough that some days she genuinely couldn't tell which yard she was standing in.

They buried her grandfather on a Tuesday, three weeks after the harvest festival, three weeks after a hundred-year-old man had knelt on a wooden platform in front of the whole town and finally, audibly, begun to age. Thomas Voss had held on just long enough to hear the shape of it secondhand, Priya sitting at his bedside describing Kell's collapse in careful, plain sentences while his oxygen line hissed its steady rhythm beside him, and by the time Mara arrived the next morning he was smiling in a way she hadn't seen since before her grandmother's funeral, a loose, unguarded smile that had nothing left to protect.

"Tell your Caleb," he'd said, his voice thinner than it had been even a week before, "that I'm sorry I never told either of you the whole truth sooner. I thought I was being careful. Turns out I was mostly just being afraid, and calling it the same thing." He'd reached for her hand then, his grip weaker than she remembered but somehow steadier than anything he'd offered her all summer. "You did the thing I was too tired to finish. Don't let anybody tell you that's a small thing."

He died four days later, in his sleep, the home aide told her, easy, the kind of ending Mara had stopped believing people in this town were allowed to have.

The funeral was smaller than her grandmother's, quieter, the whole valley still catching its breath from everything that had happened at the harvest festival, and Mara stood at the graveside in the same black dress she'd worn twice now this year, Caleb's hand steady in hers the entire time, and understood that she was, for the first time in eight years, not standing alone in this particular grief. Odette had organized the reception without being asked, the way she seemed to organize most things this town needed done quietly and well, and Yusuf had stood up partway through to say a few plain words about a man who'd spent eleven years arguing with him across a council table and never once, in all that time, been wrong about the things that actually mattered. Even Priya's dog had come, curled patient and solemn under the folding table of casseroles, as if the whole valley understood that grief, done properly, required everyone who could show up to actually show up.

---

He remembered more every week now.

Not in any order that made sense, not in any pattern she could predict, but steadily, the fragments surfacing at odd hours the way sediment settles once water finally goes still. He remembered the sound of her laugh before he remembered her name for it. He remembered the specific weight of her head against his shoulder on a long drive before he remembered where they'd been driving to. He woke one morning in early November with the entire memory of a Tuesday four years back, an ordinary, unremarkable Tuesday, sitting on this same porch eating breakfast, arguing gently about whether the crop rotation needed adjusting, and he'd had to sit very still for a long moment afterward, turning the memory over like a stone he was afraid of dropping, before he trusted himself to tell her about it.

"It's not like it used to be," he told her one evening in late October, the two of them wrapped in the same blanket on the porch step, Toll's warm weight pressed against both their legs, the orchard gone bare and honest around them, branches stripped down to their true shape after the last of the harvest. "I used to think getting the memories back would feel like getting myself back. Like I'd wake up one day and be the man I was before, fully restored, gap closed." He turned his head to look at her, something steady and certain in his face that she'd watched grow more solid with every week that passed. "It doesn't feel like that. It feels like I'm building a second version of loving you, right alongside the first one, and some days I can't fully tell which memory belongs to which version anymore. I'm not sure I want to be able to tell the difference."

"Does that scare you?"

"It did. For a while." He was quiet a moment, watching his own breath fog faintly in the cooling air. "It doesn't anymore. I think I finally understand what you were trying to tell me at the fence line, that first week. It was never about the memory being the proof. You were the proof. The memory's just, I don't know. Paperwork. Nice to have. Not the thing that actually made any of it true."

Mara felt something in her chest that had been carefully braced for eight years, and then for one more terrifying summer on top of that, finally, fully release, a tension she'd carried so long she'd stopped recognizing it as separate from herself.

"Tell me one," she said. "One of the ones that's just yours. Not one you're not sure which version it belongs to."

He thought about it long enough that she assumed he'd come up empty, and then his mouth curved, small and private, the particular smile she'd learned meant he'd found something worth handing over carefully. "The day we brought Toll home. That one's mine, whole, no seams in it anywhere. You'd wanted a dog for months and I kept saying the orchard didn't need one more mouth to feed, and then we drove out to that shelter two counties over just to look, you said, just to see, and you sat down on the concrete floor of that kennel in your good jeans so a scared, half-grown mutt would come close enough to sniff your hand." His hand found Toll's head where she was sprawled across both their feet, scratching absently behind one ear. "I remember thinking, watching you do that, that I'd never once seen anyone decide to love something so completely before they'd even confirmed it would love them back. I didn't say any of that out loud. I just paid the adoption fee before you could talk yourself out of it."

"I don't remember you being that sentimental about it at the time."

"I wasn't, out loud. I was very committed to seeming reasonable about the whole thing." The old, dry humor surfaced, sideways, exactly the way it always had. "Turns out reasonable was never really my strong suit where you're concerned. I just used to hide it better."

Mara felt something in her chest that had been carefully braced for eight years, and then for one more terrifying summer on top of that, finally, fully release, a tension she'd carried so long she'd stopped recognizing it as separate from herself.

---

She moved the last of her things out of the Chicago apartment in the first week of November, a decision that had arrived less like a single choice and more like a slow accumulation of smaller ones, the way a wall doesn't fail all at once so much as it gives, quietly, load by load, until one day the crack you'd been ignoring is simply the whole story. Priti hadn't been surprised, not really, had simply asked when Mara wanted her resignation to take effect and whether she'd consider consulting remotely on the Halvorsen project through its close, and Mara had said yes to the second question before she'd fully processed saying yes to the first.

She kept her tools. She kept her license, current and active, because some part of her suspected this valley, once she understood the full shape of what it actually needed, might have use for someone who knew exactly how to read a structure for the places where the truth had been quietly load-bearing all along.

The town, for its part, had started treating her differently too, in the weeks since the harvest festival, the careful, sideways politeness she remembered from her first week home replaced by something warmer and considerably more direct. Odette had taken to saving her the good coffee, the kind she kept behind the counter for people she'd actually decided to trust. Yusuf, sworn in as council chair in a ceremony smaller and stranger than any Mara had ever attended, had asked her opinion twice now on infrastructure repairs the valley had been quietly neglecting for decades, structural questions dressed up as small talk that she suspected were really an invitation, offered sideways, the way this whole town seemed to offer anything that actually mattered. Even Denise had stopped relaying gossip and started simply asking, plainly, how Mara was doing, as if the whole valley had collectively decided that directness was a debt it owed her now.

She still caught herself, some mornings, waiting for the old current of dread to return, the specific bracing she'd carried for eight years against a town that had once made her feel like an outsider in her own childhood. It never quite arrived anymore. In its place was something she hadn't expected to feel here again, not after everything, a settled, unhurried certainty that this was simply where she lived now, not a place she was managing or surviving or investigating, but home, in the plain, unglamorous sense of the word, the sense that had nothing to do with grand declarations and everything to do with knowing which floorboard creaked and which neighbor to call in a storm.

Caleb helped her carry the last box up the porch steps of the orchard house, the one that had been her grandmother's and was, as of a signature two weeks ago, entirely hers now, and set it down just inside the door with the particular care of a man handling something he understood mattered more than its weight suggested.

"You're staying," he said. Not quite a question, though there was still enough uncertainty in it that she understood he needed to hear her say it plainly, the way he'd needed plain things from her since the very first week.

"I'm staying." She stepped closer, close enough to feel the last of the autumn chill radiating off his jacket, close enough to see the exact place the scar crossed his eyebrow, a mark she'd stopped thinking of as evidence of what was missing and started thinking of, simply, as part of his face. "Not because of the ring in the drawer, or the fourteen-months-ago date, or any of the evidence I built a case out of back in September. Because I want to. Because whatever this is, twice-built or once-built, it's mine, and I'm done being anywhere else."

He kissed her then, slow and unhurried, the kind of kiss that had nowhere left to rush toward, and when they finally broke apart he rested his forehead against hers, both of them breathing in the same cold, apple-sweet air.

"Stay tonight," he said, low, his hand finding hers, thumb tracing an absent line across her knuckles. "Not because anything's wrong. Just because I don't want tonight to be one more night on the list of nights we spent apart, and I don't think either of us has to keep counting those anymore."

She didn't answer with words. She followed him inside instead, past the kitchen table where a jacket that had never quite fit him properly still hung on the back of a chair, up the narrow stairs of a farmhouse that had been rebuilt more than once over its long life and had never once stopped standing because of it, and let the door close quietly behind them on a night that asked nothing further of either of them, no evidence, no verification, no old memory required to make it real.

Later, much later, with the house gone dark and quiet around them and Toll's soft breathing audible from the hallway floor outside the door, Mara lay with her head on Caleb's chest, listening to the steady, unhurried rhythm of his heart, and thought that this, finally, was the thing no structural report could have predicted and no inspection could have caught in advance. Not a flaw. Not a crack. Just two people, built and rebuilt, choosing, night after ordinary night, to keep bearing the weight of each other anyway.

"Still awake," Caleb murmured, his hand moving slow and unhurried through her hair.

"Still here," she said, which had become, somewhere over the last two months, the truest answer either of them knew how to give.

---

Wren called two days later, her voice carrying an odd, careful energy down the line, the particular tone of someone who'd found something and hadn't yet decided how much it was going to cost to say out loud.

"I found something in the Frey attic," she said. "Another box. There's a letter in it, an old one, way older than anything about Kell." A pause, papers rustling faintly on her end. "Mara, I think this might not be over. Not the way we thought it was."

"How old a letter," Mara asked, already reaching for the notepad she kept by the phone out of habit, the same notepad that had once filled up with load calculations and now, more often, filled up with things this valley needed someone to track.

"Older than the mill. Older than anything with a date I can actually read clean. Whoever wrote it used the word bargain the same way Odette does, like it's a technical term instead of just a word." Wren's voice had steadied, the careful energy settling into something closer to resolve. "I didn't read past the first page. I wanted you to be there when I finished it. Feels like the kind of thing that shouldn't be read alone in an attic."

"That's smart. Bring it by tomorrow, not tonight. Let's all have one uncomplicated evening first." Mara caught Caleb's eye through the window, and he lifted a hand in an easy wave, unaware he was being watched, unaware he was, in this moment, exactly the kind of ordinary, steady thing that made facing whatever came next feel survivable instead of merely necessary. "We'll look at it together in the morning, with coffee and good light, and nobody reading it alone."

"Okay." A pause, and then, quieter, almost shy in a way Mara hadn't heard from her since before the mill. "I'm glad you're the one I get to bring this stuff to now."

"Me too," Mara said, and meant it more than the simple words let on.

Mara looked out the kitchen window at the orchard rows gone bare and silver in the November light, at Caleb moving between them checking irrigation lines for the winter, steady and whole and hers, and felt the old, familiar hum of unfinished business settle quietly back into her chest, less frightening this time, mostly because she understood now, with total certainty, exactly who she'd be facing whatever came next beside.

"Tell me everything," she said. "But it can wait until dinner. Some things deserve to stay simple for one more evening before they get complicated again."
