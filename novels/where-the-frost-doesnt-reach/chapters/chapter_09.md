# CHAPTER NINE

"The orchard's had two frosts skip us the last two years. Everybody said we got lucky." Caleb's face had gone very still, the way it did, she was learning, right before he said something that cost him. He leaned back against the records table, arms crossing loosely over his chest, a posture that looked casual and wasn't. "I thought it was a good year. I told my aunt I thought maybe things were finally going right for once, after everything. I actually said that. Things are finally going right." He laughed, short and without humor, the sound landing wrong in the quiet office, bouncing off the metal filing cabinets with a hollowness that made Mara wince on his behalf. "Somebody's been spending two years of my memory to make sure my apple trees didn't freeze so this town's finances would hold together for one more harvest season, and I stood in my own orchard feeling grateful for it."

"You didn't know."

"I know that. It doesn't help much right now." He looked at her, something raw moving behind his eyes that hadn't been there at the funeral, hadn't been there even that afternoon at the fence line, a man discovering the exact shape of a wound he'd been walking around with for two years without knowing it existed, feeling for the first time the edges of something he'd mistaken for solid ground. "Mara. If this is true, if it's Kell, if he's been doing this for two years and did it before that in the eighties. What happens to me if they can't undo it? Do I just. Stay like this. Missing two years I apparently spent asking you to marry me again, and never get them back?"

Mara wanted to give him an answer that would fix something. She'd spent her whole professional life being the person in the room who could tell you exactly why a structure would or wouldn't hold, who could look at a stress report and say with total confidence this beam fails at this load, this one doesn't, and she had never once, in eight years, wanted a clean answer as badly as she wanted one now, for him, and she didn't have it. The absence of it sat in her chest like a physical weight, heavier for how badly she wished she could set it down somewhere useful, and she found herself doing the thing she'd trained herself never to do on a job site, offering reassurance before she'd actually verified the load calculation behind it.

She stopped herself. Made herself do this properly instead, the way she'd want someone to do it for her.

"I don't know," she said, and made herself hold his gaze while she said it, refusing the easier comfort of looking away. "I won't lie to you and pretend I do. But I know the way tolls work isn't supposed to be permanent erasure, it's redirection, which means the memories aren't destroyed, they're just being paid out somewhere they don't belong. If we can stop whoever's doing it, there might be a way to stop the debt from growing. Whether that gets you back what's already gone." She shook her head, the honesty costing her something she hadn't expected it to cost, a small grief for the clean answer she couldn't give him. "I don't know. I wish I did."

He nodded slowly, like her honesty had cost her something he recognized the shape of, some old instinct in him clearly attuned to the effort a person spends choosing truth over comfort, and for the first time since the funeral home, he looked at her the way a man looks at someone he's deciding to trust rather than someone he's deciding what to make of. The shift was small, barely visible, a fractional easing around his eyes, but Mara felt it land the way she'd feel a load finally settle correctly onto a beam built to carry it, a small, internal click of something finding its proper place.

"Okay," he said. "Then let's find out before he decides he needs somebody else's two years too."

The clerk at the front desk called out that they were closing in five minutes, her voice carrying the weary cheer of someone who'd said the same sentence at the same time every weekday for longer than she cared to think about, and Mara and Caleb gathered the folder and Mara's bag and walked out into a town that looked, from the outside, exactly as ordinary as it had looked the day she left it eight years ago. The evening light had gone the kind of gold that only ever seemed to happen in this valley, slanting long and warm across the courthouse steps, catching dust motes stirred up by their own footsteps, and Mara found herself struck by how thoroughly ordinary it all looked, how completely the town had mastered the art of appearing exactly like every other small town in the county while apparently hiding something that had already cost one man four years of his life and was, right now, quietly finishing the job on another.

"Do you think he knows we found it?" Caleb asked, once they were in the truck, the engine not yet started, both of them sitting for a moment in the settling dusk, the folder resting on the seat between them like something that might reach out and touch them if they weren't careful.

"I think a man who's kept a secret this size for forty years has probably built in ways to notice when somebody starts looking in the right direction." Mara turned the folder over in her hands, feeling the weight of it, thin as it was, a heaviness that had nothing to do with its size, like holding something dense at its core wrapped in ordinary packaging. "Whether he already knows specifically what we found tonight, I couldn't tell you. But he's had eyes on this town for four decades. I'd be surprised if he doesn't at least suspect something's moving."

"Then we should move faster than he expects."

"Or smarter." She looked at him, at the new, harder set of his jaw, the look of a man who'd spent two years being managed by everyone around him and had just found the first real thread of his own life to pull, a thread he seemed, now that he'd found it, entirely unwilling to let go of again. "We need allies. People who already know something and haven't told us the whole of it yet, because I don't think your aunt was being cruel keeping things from you, Caleb. I think she was scared, and scared people in this town seem to default to silence the way other places default to gossip."

"Odette." Caleb's hand tightened on the wheel, not yet turning the key, his knuckles going pale against the worn leather. "You think she knows more than she's said."

"I think everyone in this town knows more than they've said. I think that's actually the whole disease, not just Kell. A town that's spent forty years agreeing not to look too closely at anything, because looking too closely almost got somebody killed once, and it was easier to just. Stop looking." Mara felt the shape of an idea settling into place, a structural problem finally revealing which wall was actually load-bearing and which had just been dressed up to look important, decorative rather than functional, the kind of distinction her whole career had trained her to make instinctively. "Your aunt's been protecting you the only way she knew how. I think it's time we ask her to protect you a different way instead. By finally telling you everything she's been carrying."

Caleb started the truck, the engine turning over with a low, familiar rumble that seemed to settle something in his shoulders, some small comfort in the ordinary mechanics of a machine he understood completely in a world that had just proven itself considerably less understandable than he'd believed that morning, and pulled out of the courthouse lot onto the darkening road home, the folder resting on the seat between them like a third passenger neither of them was quite ready to acknowledge out loud.

"She's not going to like this conversation," he said, eyes on the road, the orchard rows beginning to appear on either side as they left the town proper behind.

"No," Mara agreed. "But I don't think either of us has the luxury of caring what she likes anymore. Not with a clock running that neither of us can see the face of." She watched the darkening fields slide past the window, each one indistinguishable from the last in the fading light, and thought about how many of those same fields had likely been touched, in some quiet, invisible way, by the exact thing they were now driving home to confront.

Neither of them spoke again until the orchard's mailbox came into view at the end of the lane, the familiar shape of it a strange comfort after everything the evening had handed them, and Mara found herself doing the quiet arithmetic of a woman trying to decide how much of tonight she was ready to say out loud to another person before she'd even fully said it to herself.

"When we get there," she said finally, "let me start with Odette. She trusts me less than she trusts you, which sounds backward, but I think it means she'll be more careful with me, and careful is what we need right now. Careless is how people in this town get themselves killed, if my grandfather's notes are anything to go by."

"You think she'd lie to you."

"I think she'd manage me, the way everyone in this valley seems to manage everyone else, out of what they'd call love and I'd call a slow, well-intentioned kind of harm." Mara looked over at him, at the tension still sitting in his shoulders, unresolved and clearly not going anywhere soon. "I don't want managed tonight, Caleb. I want the truth, even if it's ugly, especially if it's ugly. We don't have the luxury of a gentler version anymore."

The porch light was already on when they pulled up the drive, glowing warm and yellow against the deepening dark, and a shape was visible on the top step, unmistakably Odette, sitting with a dish towel twisted in her hands the way she did when she'd been waiting long enough to run out of other ways to occupy them.

"She already knows," Caleb said quietly, cutting the engine. "Look at her. She already knows we found something."

"Then let's go find out exactly how much of it she's known all along."
