# CHAPTER TWO

The house still had her grandmother's handwriting on things. A label on the spice rack that said CINNAMON, NOT NUTMEG, YOU KNOW WHO YOU ARE, presumably aimed at Mara's mother, dead now nine years and still catching blame for stolen nutmeg in a house she hadn't lived in for thirty. A grocery list on the fridge, half a week old, milk and stamps and something crossed out so hard the pen had torn the paper, the word underneath lost to whatever fit of irritation had driven the strike-through. A calendar by the phone, the old kind with a full month per page, three appointments still penciled in for weeks Eleanor Voss would never see, a hair appointment, a church committee, a dentist. Mara had found herself standing in front of that calendar the night before for a full five minutes, unable to decide whether crossing off the appointments felt like tidying or like erasing the last evidence that her grandmother had expected to be alive to keep them.

Mara stood in the kitchen at six in the morning with a cup of coffee she'd made wrong on purpose, weaker than she liked, watery enough that it barely qualified, because some stubborn part of her wanted the kitchen to taste unfamiliar rather than exactly the same. It hadn't worked. The coffee tasted wrong, but the room still smelled like the same dish soap Eleanor had used for thirty years, lemon and something chemical underneath it, and the smell alone was enough to put Mara back at age eleven, sitting at this same table doing homework while her grandmother hummed something tuneless and off-key at the stove.

The lawyer was coming at nine. The will, the house, the land, whatever Eleanor Voss had decided to do with sixty acres and a falling-down barn and a granddaughter who'd left and a town that hadn't. Mara had assumed, driving up from Chicago three days ago with an overnight bag and a return flight already booked, that she'd sign whatever needed signing and be back at her desk by Thursday, back in a city where nobody looked at her like she should already know things, where the worst surprise waiting for her most mornings was a client's revised timeline. She was starting to understand that assumption had been optimistic in a way she hadn't examined closely enough at the time, the kind of estimate she'd have flagged immediately if a junior engineer had brought it to her, built on hope instead of load calculations.

She found the ring in the third drawer of her grandmother's desk while looking for a stapler, of all mundane errands, the kind of task that felt almost obscene in its ordinariness given everything else happening around it. The desk was an old rolltop, cluttered with the accumulated paper of a long life, rubber bands gone brittle, a magnifying glass with a cracked handle, three decades of Christmas cards someone had apparently never been able to throw away. The drawer stuck the way old wood always did, swollen slightly with humidity, and Mara had to work it free with more force than the task should have required, which was probably why she noticed the ring at all, tucked toward the back where a smoothly opening drawer might have left it undisturbed.

It wasn't Eleanor's. Mara knew all of Eleanor's rings, three of them, a wedding band worn thin from sixty years of dishwater and gardening, a garnet from some anniversary Mara had heard the story of a dozen times, and a plain gold signet that had belonged to Eleanor's own mother, none of which looked anything like this, a plain gold band with a single small stone that had gone slightly cloudy the way old stones do when nobody's worn them in a while, when they sit forgotten in the dark long enough that even light seems to have trouble finding its way back into them. Mara turned it over once in her palm, unremarkable, the kind of ring you might find at any estate sale in the country, and almost put it back exactly where she'd found it. Then something made her turn it again, angling it toward the window, and she saw the engraving worn thin on the inside, the letters shallow enough that she had to tilt it twice before they resolved: C & M, and a date.

The date was fourteen months ago.

Mara sat down on the floor of her grandmother's study with the ring in her palm and did the arithmetic three times because the first two answers seemed wrong, seemed like the kind of error a tired mind makes at six in the morning after too little sleep and too much grief, transposing digits, misreading a year. Fourteen months ago, she had been in Chicago, at her cousin's engagement party, wearing a dress she'd bought specifically because it made her feel like someone who had her life together, telling people she was too busy for anything serious, laughing at the right moments, meaning almost none of it. Fourteen months ago, as far as she'd known until yesterday afternoon, Caleb Reyes had been somewhere she'd spent eight years training herself to stop letting herself imagine, filed away in the same mental drawer as the wedding dress she'd never worn and the house they'd never finished pricing out.

She did not cry. She noticed, distantly, almost clinically, the way she might have noted a hairline crack in a load-bearing wall, that she wanted to, and that the wanting felt like standing outside a locked door whose key she'd misplaced somewhere in the last eight years, somewhere between the packed car in the dark and the life she'd built specifically so nothing like this could ever reach her again.

* * *

Priya's cruiser was parked outside the general store when Mara walked in an hour later, ostensibly for coffee filters, though she'd counted the ones already in the cupboard before she left and knew there were plenty, actually because she'd run out of places in the house that didn't have something of her grandmother's watching her, some object with a story attached that Mara wasn't ready to hear yet. The store smelled the way it always had, sawdust and cured meat and the faint sweetness of the candy aisle, unchanged in a way that felt almost aggressive after everything else about this trip had shifted underneath her.

"You look like you slept in a car," Priya said, not unkindly, from behind the counter where she was buying gum like a civilian instead of a deputy, out of uniform for once, hair loose instead of pulled back.

"I found a ring."

Priya's hand stopped on the gum, fingers going still against the wrapper, a small tell that Mara filed away automatically, the way she'd have filed away an unexpected reading on a stress gauge.

"C and M," Mara said, keeping her voice level with the same discipline she used presenting bad structural news to a nervous client, the fact first, the reaction managed afterward. "A date fourteen months ago. Priya, I wasn't here fourteen months ago. Caleb doesn't remember me existing. So who is C and M, or is it what I think it is, and if it's what I think it is, I need you to stop protecting whatever this town is protecting and tell me."

A man at the end of the counter, thick forearms, a Carhartt jacket gone soft with wear, paused stacking feed bags long enough that Mara clocked him listening, that unmistakable stillness of a person pretending not to eavesdrop while doing exactly that. He caught her noticing and gave her a small, apologetic nod, the universal signal for I heard that and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, unlike some people in this room.

"Yusuf," Priya said, a little sharp, a warning wrapped around a single syllable, "don't."

"I'm not doing anything." He hefted another bag onto the stack, unhurried, the physical ease of a man who'd been doing this exact labor his whole life and had stopped noticing the weight of it decades ago. "I'm just saying, Priya, at some point somebody's going to have to tell this girl something, and it might as well be before she finds the whole story out of a jewelry drawer."

"This isn't council business, Yusuf."

"Everything's council business in this town, that's the problem." He set the bag down and looked at Mara directly, the first person since she'd arrived who had, the first pair of eyes in this whole town that hadn't slid sideways off her the way water slides off something waxed. "You're Thomas Voss's granddaughter. I sat across a table from your grandfather for eleven years arguing about things I'm not allowed to explain to you yet, and I'll tell you the only thing I actually can. Whatever you think is strange about Caleb Reyes, it's stranger than that, and stranger yet, some of us think the town's been lying about it in a way that's going to get somebody hurt."

"Yusuf." Priya's voice had gone flat, the tone of someone invoking rank, a warning shot fired in a voice that expected to be obeyed.

"I'm on the council too, Deputy. I get a vote same as Kell." He picked the bag back up like the conversation was over, the gesture final, though his eyes stayed on Mara a second longer than the rest of him seemed ready to leave. "Ask your grandfather. While you still can."

He left without buying anything else, the bell over the door swinging behind him with a small, tinny sound that felt louder in the silence he left in his wake, and Priya let out a breath that had clearly been sitting in her chest for longer than the last thirty seconds, her shoulders dropping an inch, the professional mask slipping just slightly.

"Your grandfather," Priya said finally, quieter now, "isn't well."

"I know. I saw him yesterday."

"Did he say anything?"

Mara thought of Thomas Voss in his chair by the window, the oxygen tube looping under his nose, the thin blanket over knees that had once carried him across sixty acres of orchard land without complaint, the way his eyes had tracked her across the room with a clarity his body no longer matched, sharp in a face that otherwise looked like it belonged to someone already halfway gone. And the one thing he'd said before his home aide came in to check his vitals, voice barely above a whisper, eyes fixed on her like the sentence cost him something to produce. You look like your grandmother did the year everything went wrong. She hadn't understood it. She still didn't, though she found she couldn't stop turning it over, the way you couldn't stop running your tongue against a chipped tooth.

"No," Mara said. "Not yet."
