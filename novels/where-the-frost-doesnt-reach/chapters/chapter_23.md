# CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE

*Seven weeks after the funeral*

By the second week after the confrontation at Kell's house, the story going around Amity Falls was that Mara Voss had come back from the city with a chip on her shoulder and a grudge against a good man, and that grief over her grandmother had apparently curdled into something that made her drag Caleb Reyes, poor Caleb, still not himself, into wild accusations against Ambrose Kell of all people. Mara could trace the story's spread almost the way she'd trace a load path through a building, one small point of contact at a time, a comment here, a raised eyebrow there, until the whole structure of it stood upright without anyone ever quite admitting who'd poured the foundation. It was, she thought, a remarkably efficient piece of engineering in its own right, built entirely out of plausible deniability and the town's well-practiced fondness for tidy explanations.

Mara heard the shape of it first from the woman at the pharmacy counter, who went quiet and overly polite the second she recognized her, ringing up a bottle of aspirin with the studied concentration of someone determined not to make eye contact for even a second longer than the transaction required, and then more directly from her own cousin Denise, who called to ask, with careful concern, whether Mara was doing okay, really okay, because people were saying things.

"What things," Mara said, though she already knew, the phone pressed to her ear while she stood in her grandmother's kitchen doing dishes she didn't strictly need to do, the repetitive motion of it the only thing keeping her hands from shaking with something closer to anger than hurt.

"That you're not, you know. Handling grandma's death well. That you're seeing conspiracies." Denise's voice dropped lower, apologetic, the low, guilty tone of someone relaying gossip she felt bad for having heard at all. "I'm not saying I believe it. I just wanted you to hear it from me instead of the diner."

Odette, when Mara stopped by the store that afternoon, confirmed the rest of it without being asked, her hands moving through the motions of restocking a shelf with a steadiness that felt deliberate, a woman who had clearly decided somewhere in the last two weeks that usefulness was the only apology she had left to offer, the same instinct Mara now understood a little better after their conversation about Thomas. "He's good," she said, restocking a shelf with more force than the cans strictly required, the metal clinking together with a sharpness that punctuated her sentence. "I watched him do this exact thing to a county inspector fifteen years ago who asked one too many questions about a drainage permit. Within a week the whole town believed the inspector had a drinking problem. He never did."

"So what do we do? We can't fight a story with just the truth if nobody wants to hear it." Mara felt the familiar frustration of a person accustomed to solving problems that responded to evidence, standing now in front of a problem that clearly didn't care about evidence at all.

"You don't fight the story." Odette set the last can down and looked at Mara directly, the store's fluorescent lights catching the tired lines around her eyes more starkly than the softer light of the porch ever had. "You get proof he can't spin. Something physical. Something that isn't a nineteen year old girl's shaky account or your grandfather's forty year old suspicions." Her eyes were steady, and something in them had shifted since her own confession the week before, a woman done apologizing and ready to be useful instead. "You're an engineer. You understand load and pressure and things that fail in predictable ways. That weather station out past the mill, the one that's supposedly reading conditions for the county extension office. Nobody's serviced it in years except Kell himself. He insisted on it personally, said it was a hobby."

Mara felt something click into place, that specific satisfaction of finding the actual crack instead of guessing at where one might be, a feeling she recognized from a hundred inspections where a stray detail suddenly explained everything else that hadn't been sitting right. "You think he's tampering with the readings."

"I think a man who's been faking weather emergencies for decades needs a way to make sure the official record backs up whatever story he's telling. I think if you got inside that station and found evidence it's been rigged to report frost warnings that don't match the actual temperature on the ground, you'd have something even Ambrose Kell can't talk his way around."

Mara thought about that for a long moment, turning the idea over the way she'd turn over a suspect weld, looking for the seam that would tell her whether it would hold. "How would I even get in without it being obvious somebody's been there."

"Carefully." Odette's mouth quirked, the first real hint of dry humor Mara had seen from her in days. "You're the one who reads buildings for a living. I imagine you'll figure out the part that involves not leaving fingerprints on a rusted access panel."

"That's not exactly professional training."

"No. But I've watched you handle worse this summer with considerably less preparation." Odette wiped her hands on her apron, something almost like pride flickering across her tired face. "Whatever you decide, be careful. And take Caleb. Not because you need protecting. Because that man's spent two years being protected from everything that actually mattered, and I think it would do him good to be trusted with something real instead."

---

She told Caleb that evening, out at the fence line where they'd first really talked, the crooked apple tree throwing long shadows in the last of the light, its branches heavier now with fruit than they'd been the last time she'd stood here, the ordinary business of the orchard continuing underneath everything else that had happened. There was something almost absurd, she thought, in how normal the world insisted on looking even now, apples ripening on schedule, the same birds calling from the same trees, as if the valley itself hadn't noticed anything was wrong.

"It's breaking and entering," he said, not arguing so much as making sure she'd thought it through, his eyes steady on hers, the look of a test she wanted to pass. "Or close enough to it that a friendly judge could call it that if Kell decides to press it."

"I know."

"And if you're wrong, if it's just an old station and nothing's been tampered with, we've handed him exactly the story he's already telling about us. Reckless. Paranoid. Willing to break the law to chase a theory."

"I know that too." Mara looked at him, the sun catching the scar through his eyebrow, and thought that a week ago this man hadn't known her name and now he was the person she trusted most to tell her honestly when a plan was bad. "I'm not asking you to come. This is mine to risk."

"No." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said most things that mattered. "You're not doing this alone. Not because I don't think you can handle it. Because I don't want you to have to."

Something in Mara's chest gave way, quietly, a small structural surrender that felt, for once, like relief instead of failure.

"Okay," she said. "Tonight. Before he thinks to check it himself."

They spent the last hour of daylight going over what they'd need, sitting on the porch steps while the orchard settled into evening around them, Caleb sketching the mill road from memory onto the back of a feed receipt, marking the rise where the station sat and the treeline that would give them cover on the approach. Mara was grateful, watching him work, for the ease that came from a man who'd spent his whole life reading land the way she read blueprints, noticing sightlines and cover and the places a truck's headlights would sweep before a driver ever consciously chose to look there.

"There's a gate about here," he said, tapping the paper, "chained but not locked properly, hasn't been in years. We can leave the truck on the old logging turnout and walk the last quarter mile, keeps the engine noise off the ridge."

"You've thought about this before tonight."

"I've thought about a lot of things involving that man's property since the night at his house." His jaw tightened briefly, the memory of Wren's shaking hands still close enough to reach for. "Never had a good enough reason before now."

"What made tonight different, then. What tipped it from thinking about it to actually drawing the map."

Caleb considered the question longer than she expected, his pencil going still against the paper. "Odette, I think. Watching her finally stop protecting everyone from the truth and start handing it over instead. Made me realize I'd been doing my own version of that, holding back from actually acting because acting meant admitting how much I already suspected. If she can stop being careful after two years, I figured I could manage one night."

Mara felt something settle in her at that, the quiet certainty of a plan built by two people who trusted each other's judgment in a crisis, a trust that had been earned in increments over five hard weeks rather than assumed. She packed a small bag while the light faded, a headlamp, a multitool, the same slim screwdriver set she used for inspection work, the tools of her actual profession pressed into service for something considerably less legal than anything she'd signed her name to before.

"Last chance to back out," she said, half joking, zipping the bag closed.

"Not happening." Caleb was already reaching for his truck keys, his voice steady, certain, the calm of a man who'd made his decision hours ago and saw no reason to revisit it now. "Let's go find out what he's hiding out there."

The drive out to the mill road took longer than either of them expected, Caleb keeping the headlights low along the last stretch, the truck's engine dropping to something closer to a murmur as he eased it onto the old logging turnout he'd marked on the receipt. Mara sat with the bag in her lap, running through the sequence of what she needed to check once they were inside the housing, load-bearing calm settling over her the same way it always did in the last minutes before a difficult inspection, the sharp focus that came from knowing exactly what she was looking for and trusting herself to recognize it when she found it.

"You're quiet," Caleb said, cutting the engine.

"I'm working," she said. "Same thing, most days."

He reached over and squeezed her hand once before either of them opened a door, no words needed for it, and they climbed out into the cooling dark together, the mill's silhouette rising black against a sky just beginning to prick with stars, and started walking the last quarter mile toward whatever truth was waiting for them at the top of the ridge.

The path Caleb had marked held up exactly as promised, the gate's chain rattling loose in his hand rather than resisting, and they slipped through the gap without either of it making more noise than the wind already covered. Mara found the walk itself almost meditative in its tension, every sense sharpened past what she was used to, the crunch of dry grass under her boots suddenly loud enough to make her wince, the distant call of some night bird registering as a small, startled jolt before her mind caught up and dismissed it as harmless. Caleb walked a half step ahead of her, unhurried but alert, occasionally raising a hand to slow her pace at a rise in the ground or a gap in the treeline that might expose them longer than either of them wanted.

"You've done this before," she whispered, once they'd crested the first low hill and the mill's outline had grown large enough to make out actual detail, the weather station's small metal housing just visible on its post near the tree line beyond.

"Not this exactly. Plenty of nights walking property lines in the dark, though, back before." He didn't finish the sentence, and she understood without asking that before meant before the two years he couldn't account for, a whole category of his own history he still spoke around rather than through. "Some things your body just remembers how to do, apparently, even when the rest of you doesn't."

They reached the station's base a few minutes later, the small metal box sitting exactly where Odette had described it, so unremarkable that Mara understood immediately why nobody had thought twice about it in years. She crouched at its base, running her fingers along the housing's seams by feel before risking the headlamp, cataloguing the shape of the thing the way she would any unfamiliar structure before deciding how to open it without leaving a mark.

"Ready," she breathed, and felt Caleb settle into position behind her, shielding the small circle of light with his own body from anyone who might be watching from the road below, the two of them working together with the same easy coordination they'd built over five short weeks that felt, standing here in the dark, like considerably longer.
