# CHAPTER THREE

Mara had walked the property line before she'd decided to, the way her feet used to find the shortcut through the Kowalski's yard to the school bus stop without her brain filing a request first. Eight years gone and her body still knew this ground. That was the kind of detail she would have flagged as an anomaly in a structural report, load-bearing muscle memory outliving the relationship that built it, and then she would have moved on to the next line item, because that was what she did with things that hurt too much to look at directly.

The orchard in late afternoon was doing the thing it always did in September, the light going thick and gold and slow, like the air itself had ripened along with everything else. Rows of trees stretched away from her in both directions, Voss land on her left, Reyes land on her right, divided by a line of fence posts that had needed replacing since before she left and clearly still hadn't been, silvered wood leaning at angles that would have failed any inspection she'd have signed off on. She counted posts out of habit. Fourteen from the gate to where she was standing. She used to know that number without counting.

She wasn't thinking about Caleb, specifically, when she stopped at the fence line. She was thinking about her grandfather's face that morning, the way his eyes had tracked her with a clarity that didn't match the rest of him, the sentence he hadn't finished. You look like your grandmother did the year everything went wrong. She'd turned that over for six hours now, in the truck, in the shower at the house that still smelled like her grandmother's soap, in the kitchen where nobody had thrown out the good china even though nobody used it. She hadn't found an angle that made it mean anything except grief talking, an old man mixing up decades, and she didn't fully believe that explanation even as she reached for it.

So she stopped at the fence, and she looked at the crooked tree, third from the post, still there, a little more crooked than she remembered, the way everything in this town seemed to be a slightly worn copy of the version she'd carried with her, and she let herself, for exactly as long as it took to breathe in and out twice, remember what had happened under it.

That was when Caleb found her.

She heard the truck before she saw him, the particular knock of an engine that needed a valve job, and then boots on dry grass, unhurried, a man walking toward something he wasn't sure he wanted to reach. She turned before he said anything. She'd have known his walk blind.

"You're on my side of the property line," he said, which might have been a joke if his voice had been lighter.

"Sorry." She didn't move. "I used to know exactly where the line was. I'm apparently a little rusty."

He stopped a few feet off, close enough that she could see the day on him: dust on his forearms, a streak of something dark, oil maybe, along the side of his jaw where he'd wiped his face with the back of his wrist. He had his father's build, she noted, the kind of frame the orchard made out of a man over years of hauling ladders and crates, but he moved like someone who hadn't fully settled into it yet, like he was still getting used to occupying this particular body in this particular place. Two years, she thought. Two years of being a stranger to your own life would do that.

"You used to spend a lot of time out here?"

It was such an ordinary question, asked by a man with no memory of the answer, that Mara had to look away toward the trees before she answered it. There was a version of this conversation where she made a joke, deflected, gave him the kind of answer a woman gives a man she's meeting for the first time at a funeral reception. She didn't have that in her right now. She was too tired, and he'd asked too plainly.

"I did," she said.

"With me?"

"Yes."

He didn't say anything for a while. Wind moved through the rows, that particular sound apples make in early fall, dry and papery, nothing like the sound they'd make once they were heavy enough to bow the branches. Mara watched him not-ask the next question for almost thirty seconds, watched him turn it over, decide against it, and then decide for it anyway, the way she remembered him deciding things: slow, and then all at once, no visible seam between the two states.

She used the silence to catalogue him the way she used to catalogue exits in unfamiliar rooms, an old habit she'd never bothered to unlearn because it had never once felt like paranoia, just diligence. He'd cut his hair shorter than he used to wear it. There was a new scar along his left forearm, thin and pale, the kind you got from an unguarded blade in a packing shed, not the kind you got from anything dramatic. He still stood with his weight slightly forward, like a man perpetually one step from being needed somewhere else, a posture she'd once found frustrating and now found unbearably familiar. Eight years should have made him a stranger. It hadn't. It had only made the strangeness sit in the wrong places, not in how he stood or how he held his shoulders, but in the two-year gap behind his eyes where something she used to be able to read simply wasn't there to read anymore.

He crouched instead, picked up a fallen branch from the base of the fence post, turned it over in his hands like it required his full attention. "I've been finding things," he said, not looking at her. "Since about two years ago. Little things, mostly. A jacket in my closet that's not my size, too broad in the shoulders, like it belonged to somebody I used to be and then wasn't anymore. A truck payment record for a truck I don't own, made out to a dealership two towns over I've never set foot in as far as I remember. A receipt from a jeweler in the next county, dated to a day I have no memory of living, for a ring I don't have anymore, because apparently I gave it to someone." He straightened, tossed the branch aside, finally looked at her. "I found that one eight months ago. I've been carrying it around since, in my wallet, like evidence. I haven't shown it to a single person in this town. I don't think I trust any of them enough."

Mara's chest did something complicated and painful, a tightness that started under her ribs and climbed. "Caleb."

"I'm not asking you to explain it." He said it fast, like he needed to get the sentence out before he changed his mind about finishing it. "I know you probably can't, or won't, or it's complicated in some way everybody in this town seems to think I can't handle. I've gotten real familiar with that look in the last two years. Everybody in this town treats me like I'm one wrong question away from breaking something. Odette gets this look, like I've asked her to hand me a loaded gun. Priya changes the subject so fast it's basically a magic trick. My own aunt cried once when I asked her a direct question about a specific Tuesday two years ago, just asked what happened that day, nothing dramatic, and then told me it was allergies. In September. In a store with the door propped open and no pollen count worth mentioning." He turned to look at her properly for the first time since he'd arrived, and something in his face had gone very still, very deliberate, a man setting something heavy down carefully instead of dropping it. "You're the first person who hasn't lied to me by omission in about forty-eight hours, and I think that's mostly because you look too tired to bother constructing anything. So I'm asking you. Not for the whole story. I don't even know if I'm ready for the whole story. Just tell me one true thing."

Mara held his gaze and thought about every version of this conversation she'd imagined having with him over eight years, furious ones, in the first two years, where she made him watch her cry and then refused to forgive him for whatever excuse he offered; quieter ones, later, where she'd imagined running into him somewhere neutral, a conference, an airport, and being cool and unbothered while he explained himself; and then, eventually, no versions at all, because she'd made herself stop imagining a conversation with a man who, as far as she'd known, had simply decided she wasn't worth keeping. None of those imagined conversations had prepared her for this: for being asked to give the explanation instead of receive one, to a man who was, functionally, meeting her for the second time in his life, and who had spent two years alone with a jacket that didn't fit and a receipt he didn't trust anyone with.

She thought about lying. Not a large lie. A small, protective one: it's complicated, I'll explain when I understand it myself, the kind of thing Odette or Priya might say, the kind of sentence that would buy her time to figure out what was actually happening before she said anything irreversible. She could see exactly how that sentence would land: another brick in the wall this town had built around him, one more person choosing the comfortable evasion.

She didn't say it.

"You asked me to marry you," she said instead. "Under this tree. That one, the crooked one, third from the fence post. You didn't have a ring yet, you asked anyway. You said you'd get around to the ring eventually and you didn't want to wait on a piece of metal to say something true."

Something moved behind Caleb's eyes, quick and involuntary, the look of a man checking a memory that wasn't there and finding, instead, an absence shaped exactly like the thing he was looking for. His jaw did the thing she remembered, the bracing, the same motion she'd watched a hundred times across a hundred small disagreements and one enormous, wonderful yes.

"And you said yes."

"I said yes."

"And then what happened to us?"

Mara looked at the crooked tree, third from the fence post, still standing, still bearing fruit nobody currently living remembered planting it for, and understood, with the same cold clarity she brought to a compromised beam, a foundation crack that told you everything about a building's future if you were willing to actually read it, that the next sentence out of her mouth was going to determine whether this man ever trusted her again. Not the town. Not the story underneath the story, the tolling, the valley, none of the architecture she didn't have words for yet. Just this. Whether he trusted her.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the truth, and I know it's not the answer you want, but it's the only one I've got that isn't a guess dressed up as a fact. I left eight years ago because I thought you changed your mind about me. No note, no call, just gone one morning like the whole thing had been a phase you outgrew. I've spent eight years believing that story about myself, that I'm the kind of person people leave, that I read the whole thing wrong from the start. And now I'm standing here finding out you don't even remember the two years where, apparently, you asked me to marry you a second time. Which means either I was wrong about everything, the whole eight-year story I built my life around, or something happened to both of us that neither of us understands yet." She made herself keep looking at him, made herself not glance away toward the tree or the truck or anywhere that would have been easier. "I don't have a clean story for you, Caleb. I have a ring in a drawer I found this morning and a grandfather who won't finish his sentences and a whole town that flinches every time your name comes up in a room. I'm trying to find out what's true. I would like it if you'd let me try to find out with you, instead of alone. I think you've been alone with this long enough."

The wind moved through the trees again, harder this time, enough to pull a few early leaves loose and send them skating low across the grass between them. Somewhere down the row, a truck engine turned over, one of his hands starting the evening watering, ordinary sound in an ordinary orchard that Mara now understood was not ordinary at all, had perhaps never been ordinary, not once, not for either of them, and she wondered how much of what she'd once mistaken for a simple life here had actually been this, something enormous, humming quietly under the surface of apple trees and property lines, waiting to be noticed.

Caleb was quiet long enough that she thought he might simply walk away, might decide that one true thing was one true thing too many for a Tuesday. Instead he looked past her, toward the house on the Voss side of the fence, toward the light in the upstairs window where her grandfather's home aide would be starting the evening routine, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, a decision made and settled into.

"Okay," Caleb said finally. "Okay. Where do we start."

"I think," Mara said, "we start with my grandfather. While we still can."

He nodded, once, like a man agreeing to a job he didn't fully understand the scope of yet but had decided to take anyway, and for a moment neither of them moved, the fence line between them and the whole unspoken architecture of two years and eight years and one crooked tree standing quiet witness to all of it, the way it always had.

Mara thought, watching him turn back toward his truck, that this was the part no one warned you about, not the grief of losing someone, which at least had a shape you could learn, a weight you could eventually carry without thinking about it every hour. This was something else. This was standing across a fence line from the person you'd built your entire understanding of yourself around, and realizing that understanding was constructed on a fault line neither of you had known was there, and that the only way through it now was to keep walking toward the parts of the story that might not be kind to either of you.

"Caleb," she said, before he could get more than a few steps away.

He stopped, half-turned. "Yeah."

"For what it's worth, I didn't come back for this. I came back to bury my grandmother and go home." She shook her head slightly, as if arguing with an earlier, simpler version of herself. "I don't know what I came back for anymore. But it isn't nothing, and I don't think it's an accident, and I think you and I are both going to have to get comfortable with not knowing which of those things scares us more."

He held her gaze a moment longer than the sentence required. "I've had two years of not knowing," he said. "I'm about as comfortable with it as I'm going to get." A pause, and something almost like the old dry humor surfaced, sideways, the way she remembered it. "Doesn't mean I like it."

"No," Mara agreed. "Neither do I."

He walked back to his truck without saying anything else, and she stood at the fence a while longer after the engine caught and the taillights swung away down the dirt track toward the Reyes farmhouse, watching the light go from gold to gray over trees that had outlived every certainty either of them had ever built on this ground.
