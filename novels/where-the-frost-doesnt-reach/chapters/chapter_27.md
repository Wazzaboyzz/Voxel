# CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN

Wren showed up at the orchard house two days after the threat text arrived, uninvited, on foot, having apparently walked the mile and a half from her mother's place rather than wait for someone to come get her, and Mara found her sitting on the porch steps when she came out with the morning's coffee, Toll already curled contentedly against the girl's side as though she'd been expected all along.

"You didn't have to walk," Mara said, sitting down beside her, handing over the second mug she'd brought out on instinct, some old habit of always making enough for one more person than strictly necessary.

"I wanted to think on the way." Wren wrapped both hands around the mug without drinking from it, staring out at the orchard rows with an expression more serious than Mara had seen from her since the silo. "I've been thinking about it since the text came in. Priya told my mom, my mom told me, everyone's being extra careful now, which I understand, but it also means everyone's circling the wagons around me again like I'm made of glass."

"You almost got hurt, Wren. Twice now."

"I know. I was there both times." There wasn't any bitterness in it, just a plain, steady insistence that made Mara set her own mug down and actually listen instead of half-listening while she planned what reassurance to offer next. "I've had two weeks to think about what almost happened at Kell's, and about the silo, and I keep landing in the same place. I don't want to be protected out of this. I want to be prepared for it instead. Those aren't the same thing, even though everyone keeps treating them like they are."

Mara felt the old, familiar instinct rise in her, the urge to soften the conversation, to tell this nineteen-year-old that she'd already given enough, that safety mattered more than usefulness, and she caught herself before she said any of it, recognizing, with some discomfort, the shape of exactly the kind of well-meaning dismissal she'd have resented at Wren's age herself.

"Talk me through what you mean by prepared," she said instead.

"I mean, if I'm going to be a target either way, because apparently I already am one whether I do anything or not, I'd rather spend my energy getting good at controlling this instead of getting good at hiding from it." Wren finally took a sip of her coffee, some of the tension in her shoulders easing slightly now that she'd said the thing out loud without being immediately talked out of it. "Dev's had years of practice and he still got taken by surprise. I've had a summer. If there's a controlled way to build up what I can actually handle before another crisis forces me to find out the hard way, I want that. I don't want the next silo to be the first time I've ever really tried."

Mara thought of every apprentice she'd ever supervised who'd asked for exactly this, more responsibility before they felt ready for it, and understood, watching Wren's face, the difference between a request born of recklessness and one born of clear-eyed preparation. This wasn't a girl chasing danger. This was someone trying to make sure the next time danger found her, it wouldn't find her defenseless.

"I can't make that decision for you," Mara said slowly, "and I don't think I should try to. But I also don't think it's fair to just say no because it's easier for the rest of us to feel like we're protecting you. If we're going to do this, it needs structure. Small steps. Someone experienced watching every single time. And the second it feels wrong, we stop, no negotiating."

"That's fair." Wren looked relieved, some of the wariness she'd carried up the porch steps finally draining out of her posture. "I'm not asking to jump into anything huge. I just don't want to keep being the person everyone talks about in the past tense, like the scary part already happened and now my only job is to recover from it quietly in the background while the rest of you do the actual work."

"You won't be." Mara meant it, and heard the certainty in her own voice with something like surprise. "I'll talk to Priya. And Caleb. If we're doing this, everyone needs to actually agree to it, not just quietly tolerate it because you're determined enough to make it awkward to say no."

Wren managed a small, genuine laugh at that, the first real one Mara had heard from her in days. "That's basically my whole strategy, yeah."

"I noticed." Mara bumped her shoulder gently against the girl's, an easy, unplanned gesture that felt, in the moment, like the beginning of something worth protecting on its own terms, separate from the case, separate from Kell, simply two people deciding to trust each other with something difficult. "For what it's worth, I think you're right. I just also think being right doesn't make it any less terrifying to watch you walk toward this on purpose."

"I know." Wren looked out at the orchard, the morning light catching gold in the leaves, ordinary and unbothered by any of it. "It's terrifying to be the one walking toward it too. I'd just rather be terrified and useful than terrified and useless. I think I've had enough of the second one to last a lifetime."

They sat with that for a while, the coffee going lukewarm in their hands, Toll shifting occasionally to keep herself in the warmest patch of sun as it moved across the porch boards. Mara found herself, unexpectedly, thinking of her own nineteen-year-old self, the one who'd left this town certain she understood exactly what she wanted and exactly how little it had to do with anyone else's caution, and wondered whether Wren's particular brand of stubbornness was simply what nineteen looked like when it collided with something too large to run from.

"Can I ask you something," Wren said eventually, turning the mug slowly between her palms.

"Go ahead."

"Does it get easier? Being scared and doing the thing anyway. Or do you just get better at pretending it doesn't show."

Mara considered the question seriously, the way it deserved. "Little of both, honestly. You get better at knowing which fear is telling you something useful and which fear is just noise. And you get better at not needing the fear to go away completely before you act, because it mostly doesn't, not really, not for the things that are actually worth being scared of." She looked at Wren, at the careful, determined set of her young face. "But no, it doesn't stop being scary. I don't think it's supposed to. I think the scary is how you know it still matters."

Wren nodded slowly, filing that away the way Mara had watched her file away everything else this summer, with the seriousness of someone determined to grow into whatever this valley was about to ask of her, whether or not she'd chosen the timeline for it.

"Talk to Priya today," Mara said, standing, offering a hand to pull Wren up from the step. "And Caleb, when he's back from the grove. If we're building this, we build it properly, together, with everyone actually on board. I meant that part."

"I will." Wren took her hand, standing, brushing porch dust from her jeans. "Thank you. For not just telling me no because it was simpler."

"Simpler isn't always right," Mara said, and found, saying it, that she believed it more fully than she had at the start of this conversation. "You taught me that a little, just now. So thank you too."
