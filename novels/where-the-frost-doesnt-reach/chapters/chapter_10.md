# CHAPTER TEN

Odette didn't stand when they climbed out of the truck, didn't call out a greeting, just sat there on the top step with the dish towel twisted so tight in her hands that Mara could see the muscles standing out in her forearms even in the porch light's dim, yellow wash. Toll lifted her head from where she'd been sprawled by the door, gave one soft thump of her tail against the boards, and settled back down, apparently deciding this particular tension in the air belonged to the humans and not to her.

"You went to the records office," Odette said. Not a question.

"We did." Caleb's voice came out harder than Mara had heard it yet, the accumulated weight of the whole evening finally finding an edge to press against. "You want to tell me why that's the first thing you say to me, and not hello, or how's your grandfather, or any of the things a person normally says."

"Because I've spent two years being careful with you, Caleb, and three weeks of watching you and this woman circle each other has convinced me careful just ran out of road." Odette's voice cracked on the last word, and she pressed the towel flat against her stomach like it could hold something in place, some old grief finally too large to keep contained behind her usual composure. "Your grandfather's file. Eighteen ninety, no, ninety one. Thomas showed it to me once, years ago, before you were born, when I was young and stupid enough to think knowing the truth would fix something."

"You knew," Mara said quietly, climbing the porch steps slowly, giving Odette time to see her coming rather than closing the distance too fast. "This whole time. You knew what happened to him and you let him think he did something to deserve it."

"I let him think a lot of things, and I hated myself for every one of them, and I'd like the record to show I disagreed with Thomas's advice at the time and disagree with it more now." Odette's chin came up, some old stubbornness surfacing under the guilt, a flash of the woman Mara remembered from years ago, sharp-tongued and unwilling to be talked down to even when she was in the wrong. "Your grandfather told me, back when he first suspected Kell, that the safest thing for Caleb was to not know he was a target, because a man who doesn't know he's being hunted doesn't panic, doesn't go asking the wrong questions to the wrong people, doesn't get himself hurt trying to solve something he has no gift to solve. I believed him. I believed him for two years while I watched my nephew lose himself in pieces and I said nothing, and you can hate me for it, I've had plenty of practice hating myself for it first."

Caleb had gone very still, the stillness Mara now recognized as the exact center of his temper, quieter and more dangerous than shouting would have been, a control that seemed to cost him more effort than raising his voice ever would have. "You could have told me."

"I could have gotten you killed."

"You don't know that."

"I don't know the opposite either, and I wasn't willing to bet your life on finding out which." Odette's eyes were wet now, unashamed of it, tracking his face with a desperation that looked, to Mara, like a woman finally setting down a weight she'd carried alone for far too long and terrified of what she'd find underneath it. "I loved your father like my own brother and I promised him, before he died, that I'd keep you safe in a town that doesn't always know how to do that for the people it decides don't need protecting. I have made mistakes trying to keep that promise. I am not going to apologize for the promise itself."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The evening had gone that particular deep gold that Mara remembered from a hundred nights on this porch, back when it had been easy, before either of them understood what the valley actually was, the light catching the dust in the air and turning it briefly, deceptively beautiful, the kind of light that made a person forget, for a moment, that anything underneath it could be wrong at all.

"Kell," Caleb said finally, changing tack, the anger banked but not gone, sitting just under the surface of his voice like a current running beneath still water. "What do you know about him. Actually know, not what Thomas suspected forty years ago."

"I know his Weathers gift has always run stronger than anyone else on the council's, which used to make him useful and now makes me wonder how long he's been quietly overusing it." Odette twisted the towel again, the fabric visibly worn thin from a lifetime of this exact nervous habit. "I know he's up for re-election as chair in six weeks and the town's economy has had two unnaturally lucky years in a row that everyone's been thanking God for instead of asking harder questions about."

She stopped, and something in her face shifted, a decision visibly settling into place, the last piece of resistance finally giving way.

"And I know," she said, quieter now, "that three days ago, before your grandmother's funeral even happened, Mara, he came into the store and asked me, very casually, whether Thomas Voss's granddaughter was a Finder, and how skilled."

Mara felt something cold settle low in her stomach, the specific chill of learning a threat had been circling long before she'd noticed it. "You told him."

"I told him I didn't know, because at the time I didn't." Odette met her eyes directly, some old fierceness surfacing through the guilt. "He didn't ask like small talk, Mara. He asked like a man doing an inventory. I've thought about that phrasing every day since, wondering if I should have lied more convincingly, wondering what he'd have done with a different answer, and I don't have a good answer for either question, and I've stopped pretending to myself that I do."

Caleb had gone quiet for a moment, some new calculation visibly running behind his eyes, and when he spoke again his voice had lost some of its earlier hardness, replaced by something more careful, more exhausted. "How long has this been going on, Aunt Odette. Not the redirect. The town. Kell. All of it. You make it sound like something everyone's just quietly agreed to live alongside, the way people live next to a fault line they've decided not to think about."

"Longer than either of us has been alive, if your grandfather's notes are anything to go by." Odette's hands finally stilled on the towel, resting in her lap as though she'd run out of nervous energy to spend on it. "This valley made a bargain a long time ago, before any of the families currently living here had names anyone remembers. Gifts, in exchange for tolls. Nobody ever wrote down exactly who agreed to it or on what terms, which I've always thought was the whole design of it, so nobody living now could ever go back and argue the fine print. We just inherited the arrangement the way you inherit an orchard, or a house, or bad knees. Nobody asks whether they consent to bad knees. They just live with them."

"That's not the same thing," Mara said quietly.

"No. It isn't. But it's the story people tell themselves, generation after generation, so the weight of it feels like inheritance instead of injustice." Odette looked out toward the dark tree line where her own house sat, invisible now in the deepening night. "I used to think Kell was simply a bad man doing a necessary job badly. I'm less sure of that now. I think he might genuinely believe he's the only thing keeping this valley from falling apart entirely, and I think that belief has let him justify things to himself that a more honest man would have refused to do decades ago."

"You're defending him."

"I'm explaining him. There's a difference, and I need you both to understand it, because a man who thinks he's the villain is easy to fight. A man who's convinced himself he's the last line of defense is going to be a great deal harder to stop, because he won't experience anything you do to him as justice. He'll experience it as betrayal by people too naive to understand what he's been protecting them from."
