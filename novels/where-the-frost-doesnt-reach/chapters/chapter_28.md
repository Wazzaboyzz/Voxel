# CHAPTER TWENTY-EIGHT

Priya traced the number within the hour, a prepaid phone, purchased in cash two counties over, the kind of purchase designed specifically to leave no trail back to anyone, and the dead end of it sat over the group like a second, quieter threat underneath the first. Mara watched her set the phone down on the kitchen table with more care than the object required, a careful gentleness that belonged to someone handling bad news she hadn't yet decided how to phrase. The morning had brought a bright, deceptively cheerful light through the kitchen windows, the kind of sunshine that made a threatening text message feel almost more absurd than frightening, until Mara reminded herself, firmly, that the absurdity was exactly the point. Threats built to look ordinary were still threats. She'd learned that much just from the last two weeks, if nothing else.

"Could be Kell," Priya said, though her voice carried the hedge of someone who'd learned this week not to assume anything simple. "Could be somebody working for him. Could be somebody entirely separate who just doesn't want this dug up, which given what your grandfather's notes suggested, might be a longer list than we think."

"Comforting," Caleb said.

"I didn't say it to comfort you."

They'd gathered at the orchard house again by early afternoon, the four of them, Mara, Caleb, Priya, and Wren, who'd shown up unannounced with a thermos of coffee and an expression that said she had no intention of being sent home this time. Toll lay sprawled across the kitchen floor in a patch of sun, entirely unconcerned with the state of anyone's nerves, and something about her ordinary, uncomplicated presence kept the room from tipping fully into panic. Outside the window, the orchard rows stretched away in their neat, endless lines, apples heavy now on branches that would need picking within the week regardless of whatever else was happening to the people who owned them, the land as indifferent as land always was to the human dramas playing out on top of it. Mara found herself staring at those rows longer than the conversation strictly allowed, the old habit of looking for a fixed point when everything else in a room felt like it was shifting under her.

Caleb had made coffee nobody was drinking. Priya kept turning the burner phone over in her hands like it might tell her something new on the fourth pass that it hadn't on the first three. Wren sat closest to the window, her own mug long gone cold, watching the rest of them with an attention that Mara was only now starting to recognize as something other than nerves.

"I want to say something," Wren said, and everyone turned to look at her. She was sitting very straight, hands wrapped around her mug like she needed the warmth to steady herself, her knuckles pale against the ceramic. "I know everyone's been treating me like the thing that almost went wrong. And I get why. But I've been thinking about it since that night, and I don't want to just be the almost-victim in this. I want to actually help, on purpose, if there's a safe way to do it."

"Wren." Mara kept her voice gentle but firm. "You don't have to prove anything."

"I know. That's not why." Wren looked at Caleb directly. "You said something that night, at Kell's house. About knowing what it costs to be the one somebody decides is expendable. I've been sitting with that. I don't want to be expendable either, but I also don't want to just be protected into uselessness while everyone else does the actual work. There has to be a middle path between reckless and useless."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Mara found herself impressed, despite the fear still sitting low in her chest, at how clearly a nineteen year old had apparently spent two weeks thinking harder about agency and risk than most adults she knew ever bothered to. She thought of every apprentice she'd ever worked with who'd wanted responsibility handed to them before they'd earned the judgment to use it well, and understood that Wren was asking for something rarer and more mature than that, permission to be trusted with a risk she'd already thought through carefully on her own. It wasn't nothing, being asked to trust someone that way. It came with its own weight.

"There might be," Caleb said slowly, looking at Mara. "You said Finders can sometimes read old objects safely if the emotional charge isn't active anymore. What if we're not asking Wren to touch anything of mine, or anything connected to an active toll. What if we're asking her to read something old. Something from the eighties case. Something with no living stake left in it."

Mara turned the idea over, feeling the shape of it settle into something workable, the same satisfaction as a load path finally rerouted somewhere safer without losing any of its structural purpose. She thought about it the way she thought about a beam under strain, looking for the place the weight could travel without breaking anything on the way down. "Elias Frey's belongings. If anything of his survived, if my grandfather kept anything besides that file. Objects that have been sitting inert for decades wouldn't carry the same live danger as touching an active toll. It's not zero risk, but it's a controlled version instead of an accidental one."

"I can ask my mother," Wren said, sitting up straighter, something like purpose replacing the shakiness from before. "The Frey family, they moved away after Elias died, but before they did, half the county bought up estate items at auction. My mom's got boxes of things from that sale in the attic, she's never been able to throw anything out. If there's something of his in there, I could try, carefully, with all of you watching, in a place Kell has no reason to be looking."

Priya was already shaking her head slightly, though not in outright refusal, more the reflexive caution of someone who'd watched her own brother nearly destroy himself for this exact kind of investigation, her arms wrapping tighter around herself. She looked, for a moment, less like the person tracing burner phones and more like a sister who hadn't slept well in days. "If we do this, it's controlled. Small object, short exposure, and the second it feels wrong, we stop, no arguments."

"Agreed," Wren said immediately.

"I mean it, Wren." Priya's voice had gone quieter, rougher at the edges. "Dev didn't think he was doing anything reckless either, at first. It crept up on him one small decision at a time. I'm not saying that to scare you. I'm saying it because I watched it happen and I don't want to watch it happen twice."

"I hear you," Wren said, and something in the way she said it, steady, unhurried, made it clear she meant it as more than a placation.

Caleb caught Mara's eye across the kitchen table, something unspoken passing between them, the shared recognition of two people who understood exactly what it meant to let someone else take on a risk they couldn't fully protect them from, and neither of them said anything else, because there wasn't anything left to say that wouldn't have sounded like trying to talk a determined young woman out of the one thing she'd asked to do on her own terms. They made plans instead, practical ones. Priya would go with Wren to her mother's attic that evening, with Caleb on call if anything felt wrong, and Mara would stay behind to keep working through what remained of the old case file, looking for any name that might tell them who else besides Kell had reason to want the past left buried.

---

That evening, after Priya and Wren had left to search the attic, Caleb found Mara out at the crooked apple tree, the third one from the fence post, standing with her hand flat against the bark the way she used to when she was working up to saying something difficult. The last light was fading fast, the sky gone that deep, bruised purple that came just before true dark, and the orchard around them had gone quiet the way it did every evening, birds settling, the day's heat finally releasing its grip on the ground. Mara had come out here without quite deciding to, the way her feet kept finding this exact tree whenever her thoughts got too loud to sit still with indoors. It was the same tree she used to climb as a girl, the same crook in the trunk that had scraped her knee more times than she could count, and there was something almost funny about the fact that her body still remembered it as a place to go when everything else felt unsteady.

He didn't say anything at first. He just came and stood beside her, close enough that his shoulder was against hers, and this time neither of them pretended the distance was an accident.

"I keep thinking about what she said," he said. "About not wanting to just be protected into uselessness." His shoulder was against Mara's now, solid and warm, and neither of them moved to correct the distance. "I think I understand that better than I want to. Everyone in this town spent two years being gentle with me because they thought gentle was the same thing as safe. It wasn't. It just meant I spent two years not knowing what was actually happening to my own life."

"I don't want to be gentle with you that way," Mara said quietly. "I want to be honest with you, even when honest is harder."

"I know. That's why I trust you." He turned his head, close enough now that she could feel his breath, warm in the cooling evening air. "Mara. I need to say something, and I need you to let me say all of it before you respond, because if I stop halfway I don't think I'll finish it."

She nodded, not trusting her voice, her heart suddenly loud enough in her own ears that she was certain he must be able to hear it too. Somewhere behind them, back at the house, a porch light clicked on, the only sign of the ordinary world still going about its evening a few dozen yards away.

"I don't remember loving you. I know that should be the whole problem, and some days it feels like it is. But I've spent this week watching you be exactly the kind of person worth loving, stubborn and honest and terrified and doing the hard thing anyway, and I don't need the old memory to know that what I'm feeling right now is real. It's not a photograph of somebody else's life anymore. It's mine. I don't know what to call falling for someone twice, but I think that's what this is, and I didn't want to keep not saying it just because the timeline is complicated."

Mara's chest ached with something that was equal parts joy and grief, an unbearable sweetness in finally hearing the thing she'd wanted to hear for eight years arrive in a shape she'd never imagined it would take. She thought of all the versions of this moment she'd pictured once, years ago, before she'd let herself stop picturing it at all, and how none of them had looked like this, two people standing in the near dark with a threatening text message and an attic search hanging over their heads, and how none of that seemed to matter half as much as it should have.

"Caleb."

"You don't have to say it back tonight." His voice had gone rough. "I just needed you to know it wasn't only happening on your side."

She reached up, finally, and touched the scar through his eyebrow, the one mark on him that told its own story without needing any memory to explain it, and felt him go very still under her hand, his breath catching audibly in the quiet dark between them. She let her thumb rest there a moment longer than she needed to, feeling the small raised line of it under her skin, a piece of him that had survived even when so much else hadn't.

"It was never only on my side," she said. "Not even eight years ago. I just spent a long time telling myself a story where it was easier to believe you'd stopped."

Neither of them moved for a long moment, the space between them finally, fully closed, foreheads nearly touching, and somewhere in the dark past the orchard rows, a car engine started, far enough away to be nothing, close enough that neither of them quite let themselves relax into the moment they both wanted. Mara felt Caleb's hand find hers in the dark, steady, and for a few seconds neither of them said anything at all, just stood there together under the crooked tree, letting the last of the light go without either of them reaching to turn on a porch lamp or break the quiet first.
