# CHAPTER THIRTY-THREE

The town, for its part, did not receive the news of the formal inquiry the way Mara had hoped. She'd expected relief, or at least a grudging curiosity, the natural response of ordinary people learning that a county board had finally agreed to look hard at a man they'd trusted for forty years. What she got instead, over the following week, was something closer to a slow, deliberate closing of ranks, and it was Odette who finally laid the whole shape of it out for her, over coffee that had long since gone cold on the store counter.

"They're saying you two orchestrated the whole thing," Odette told them grimly, her voice pitched low even though the store was empty, an old habit that told Mara how thoroughly this town had trained its people into caution. "That you planted the evidence to frame him because Mara's still bitter about leaving eight years ago, and Caleb's, well." She hesitated, her fingers tightening around her own mug.

"Say it," Caleb said, his jaw set.

"That Caleb's not thinking straight because of everything that happened to him, and that you talked him into this because you needed him on your side to make the story believable." Odette's mouth twisted with distaste at having to repeat it. "It's ugly, and it's working on more people than I'd like to admit, mostly because Kell's spent forty years being the person this town trusts to handle things they don't want to think about too hard."

Mara felt something cold settle in her chest, that vertigo of watching a lie become load-bearing in a structure she couldn't get inside to fix, no matter how many facts she stacked against it. "He's turning Caleb's memory loss into a weapon against us."

"He's turning everything into a weapon against you, dear. That's what he does." Odette reached across the counter and squeezed Mara's hand, an unexpected gesture from a woman who'd spent most of the last two months holding herself carefully apart. "For what it's worth, I don't believe a word of it, and neither does anyone who's actually watched the two of you this summer instead of listening to gossip at the diner. But I won't lie to you and tell you it isn't costing you both something real in this town."

Mara asked the question she'd been circling for days without quite letting herself land on it directly. "How bad. Give me actual numbers, if you have them. I'd rather know."

Odette considered that, weighing honesty against gentleness the way Mara had watched her weigh it more than once this summer, and chose honesty, the way she usually did once pressed. "Bad enough that two of the households on your canvassing list called me afterward to say they were reconsidering their statements. Bad enough that somebody left a note on Yusuf's store door this morning, nothing threatening exactly, just a single word. Traitor." She said it flatly, without drama, letting the word sit in the quiet store the way it had apparently sat on that door all morning before Yusuf had scrubbed it off. "He knows how to make people afraid of being on the wrong side of something before they've even decided what the right side is."

"Then we keep building the case louder than he can whisper against it," Mara said, though even as she said it she could hear the effort it cost her to sound as certain as the words demanded.

She left the store that afternoon walking slower than usual, turning the whole conversation over in her mind the way she'd once turn over a failed inspection report, looking for the single point where the damage had actually started rather than simply cataloguing every place it now showed. It wasn't Kell's cruelty that unsettled her most, she realized somewhere along the walk back to her truck. It was how efficiently ordinary fear did his work for him once he'd planted it, how little effort it actually took a town to protect itself by quietly abandoning the people trying to protect it from something larger. She thought of her grandfather, forty years documenting a pattern he'd never had the leverage to expose, and wondered, not for the first time, how many nights he'd sat with exactly this same cold, isolating math, and decided the safer thing was silence.

That evening, Caleb was quieter than Mara had seen him since the funeral, sitting on the porch step with Toll's head heavy in his lap, staring out at the orchard rows without really seeing them, the dog's steady breathing the only sound for a long while. Mara had come to recognize this quiet in him over the past weeks, not sullenness exactly, but the sound of a man working something over privately before he trusted himself to say it out loud, the same stillness she'd seen in him the night he'd finally asked about her grandfather's file. She'd learned, too, not to rush it, that pressing him before he was ready only pushed the thought further underground, so she simply sat down beside him and waited, letting the ordinary sounds of the evening, crickets starting up somewhere past the fence line, the last birds settling, fill the space until he was ready to use it.

"You okay?" she asked, sitting beside him, close enough to feel the tension radiating off him, the evening air carrying the last warmth of the day and, underneath it, the first cool edge that meant autumn wasn't as far off as the calendar still claimed.

"I keep thinking about what Odette said. About Kell twisting my memory loss into a weapon." His jaw worked, a muscle jumping there that she'd learned, over the weeks, meant he was holding something back with real effort. "Two years of my life, gone, and now this town's using the empty space where those years should be as a reason to doubt my own judgment about the woman I love. Like I'm not capable of knowing my own mind just because part of it's missing."

The word love sat between them, unplanned, unrehearsed, and for a moment neither of them acknowledged it directly, though Mara felt it land somewhere deep and permanent, a foundation stone she hadn't realized was still being poured, laid quietly into place in the middle of a sentence about something else entirely, which somehow made it feel more true than if he'd said it on purpose.

"You are capable," she said quietly. "You've been more clear-eyed about all of this than half this town with both their memories fully intact. Don't let him make you doubt that."

Caleb was quiet for a long moment, his hand moving absently through Toll's fur, the dog leaning into the touch without waking. "I know that, when I'm thinking straight. But there's a part of me, small and quiet and persistent, that keeps asking the same question at three in the morning. What if I only feel this certain about you because something's missing that would have told me to be more careful? What if the gap in my memory isn't just where the truth used to be, but where the doubt used to be too?"

Mara felt the question land like a splinter, small and sharp and impossible to fully remove once it was in, the kind of thing that would keep catching on everything it touched from now on. She wanted, badly, to give him some tidy reassurance that would dissolve it entirely, the way she'd once have offered a client a fix for a crack she hadn't fully diagnosed yet, and made herself resist the impulse, because she'd learned this summer, slowly and at real cost, that the fixes that lasted were the ones built on an honest accounting of the actual problem.

"I don't have an answer that fixes that for you," she said honestly. "I wish I did."

"I know." He looked at her, something raw and uncertain in his eyes that she hadn't seen since that first day at the fence line. "I just needed you to know the question exists. Before it gets bigger than either of us can manage."

They sat with it a while longer, the porch light drawing moths in slow, patient circles, Toll's breathing the only steady thing between them, and Mara found herself thinking of every structure she'd ever assessed where the client had wanted her to say the crack was nothing, and every time she'd had to tell them instead that a crack you named honestly was safer, in the long run, than one you painted over and hoped would hold. She didn't say that out loud. It felt too much like turning him into a problem to be solved rather than a man she loved, sitting beside her with a fear he'd trusted her enough to voice instead of swallow.

"For what it's worth," she said finally, "I'd rather have this version of you, the one honest enough to tell me the question exists, than a version who pretended certainty he didn't feel just to spare me one uncomfortable evening. I've had eight years of a man who kept things from me because he thought it was kinder. I don't want that again, even dressed up as reassurance."

Something in Caleb's shoulders eased, fractionally, at that, the release of a man who'd expected to be managed and found himself, instead, simply believed. "I don't remember much about who I used to be with you," he said. "But I don't think that version of me kept things from you to be kind. I think he probably just ran out of time to say them."

"Maybe." Mara looked out at the dark orchard rows, apples hanging heavy and unseen in the night, the whole valley holding its ordinary shape around a fight most of it still didn't fully understand it was part of. "I used to think the worst thing that ever happened to me was you not calling. Now I think the worst thing that could happen is either of us deciding silence is safer than the truth, even when the truth is uncomfortable. I'd rather sit through three in the morning with you than lose you to a question you were too afraid to ask out loud."

He reached for her hand then, not the tentative almost-touch of the fence line or the porch weeks ago, but something steadier, deliberate, his fingers lacing through hers with the certainty of a decision already made rather than one still being weighed. "Then we keep doing that," he said. "Saying the three in the morning thing out loud instead of letting it sit there alone in the dark."

Inside, the phone rang, sharp and sudden in the quiet evening, and Mara felt Caleb go still beside her before either of them had even moved to answer it, some old instinct in both of them now permanently tuned to expect bad news in any sound that arrived without warning. She rose to get it, Toll lifting her head to watch her go, and found Priya's voice on the other end, tight and urgent in a way that told her, before a single word of substance had been spoken, that whatever fragile calm they'd just built on the porch step was about to be tested again.

"Yusuf just called me," Priya said. "Somebody slashed two of his tires tonight, in his own driveway. He's fine, he wasn't in the truck. But Mara, this isn't whispering anymore. This is Kell showing us exactly how much he's willing to escalate before this inquiry ever reaches a hearing."

Mara looked back through the screen door at Caleb, still sitting on the step in the porch light, Toll's head once more settled in his lap, and understood, with a cold, steady clarity that had nothing left in it of doubt, that whatever came next, they would be facing it together, three in the morning questions and all.

She came back out to the porch and sat down beside him again, her hand finding his without needing to be asked, and told him plainly what Priya had said, the way she'd promised herself she would from now on, no softening, no managing him around the hard parts.

"He's escalating," Caleb repeated, quiet, absorbing it, his hand tightening around hers. "Tires tonight. What's next, worse next week. He's not going to stop just because we asked him to nicely through the proper channels."

"No. He's not." Mara looked out at the dark orchard, the same rows that had felt, twenty minutes ago, like the only steady thing in a town that had started quietly turning its back on them. "But neither are we. I think that's the part he's miscalculated. He's used to people backing off once the cost gets personal enough. He doesn't know yet that some of us have already paid the worse version of that cost, and it didn't make us quieter. It made us harder to scare."

Caleb was quiet for a moment, and when he spoke again, something in his voice had shifted, the raw uncertainty from earlier settling into something more resolved, not gone, but folded now into a steadier resolve sitting alongside it. "We should call Yusuf. Make sure he's actually okay, not just saying he is because that's what he thinks we need to hear."

"Agreed." Mara stood, pulling him up with her, Toll rising too, stretching and shaking herself before trotting toward the door as though she, too, understood the evening's quiet was officially over. "And then I think we stop reacting to whatever he does next and start deciding, ourselves, what happens next instead. We've spent this whole summer responding to him. I'm tired of playing defense."

Caleb caught her hand at the door, stopping her for one more moment before the night's real business pulled them both back into motion. "For what it's worth," he said, "I meant what I said earlier. About wanting to keep saying the three in the morning things out loud. Whatever else happens tonight, I need you to know that part wasn't just talk."

"I know," Mara said, and found, standing there in the doorway with tires slashed and threats mounting and a whole valley's worth of fear pressing in from every direction, that she believed him completely, the one certainty in a night that had just handed her several new reasons to doubt everything else.
