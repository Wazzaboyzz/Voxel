# CHAPTER FOURTEEN

Priya didn't sleep. Mara could tell the moment she opened the door the next morning, a gray exhaustion in her face that came from spending the whole night running the same four years over in her head, looking for the moment she should have pushed harder instead of accepting silence as an answer. The kitchen behind her looked like it belonged to someone who had given up pretending to eat breakfast properly, a single mug gone cold on the counter, a chair pulled out and never sat in, papers already spread across the table in the disorder of a person working through the night one document at a time.

"You look like you haven't stopped since we left the barn," Mara said, stepping inside, Caleb close behind her with Toll trotting in ahead of them both, sniffing at the corners of an unfamiliar kitchen with mild professional interest.

"I couldn't. Every time I closed my eyes I kept seeing his face at nineteen instead of wherever he actually is now, and I couldn't stand not knowing which version of him I should even be picturing." Priya rubbed at her eyes, the gesture doing nothing to hide how raw they already were. "I pulled everything," she said, setting a folder down on the kitchen table, her hands not quite steady. "Every record I have access to on Dev. Last known address, four years old. Last phone bill, three years old, cancelled for nonpayment. No employer filings anywhere in the state past that eighteen-month call. It's not that he's hard to find. It's that somebody made sure there was nothing left to find."

Caleb pulled out the chair across from her, quiet in the way Mara had learned meant he was giving someone room instead of failing to have something to say. "You don't have to do this alone."

"I've been doing it alone for four years. I got good at it." Priya's laugh had no humor in it. "I told myself so many versions of a story where he just needed space that I stopped noticing I'd stopped actually looking. That's the part I can't forgive myself for yet. Not that he's gone. That I let myself believe gone was the same thing as fine."

"You were a kid when he left, weren't you," Caleb said, gently.

"Twenty. Old enough to know better, young enough to want to believe the easy version." Priya's hands tightened around her own mug. "He called me once, eighteen months in, and I could hear something in his voice I didn't have a name for at the time. Now I think I do. It sounded like a man who'd already decided something terrible about his own future and was calling to say goodbye without using the word."

The admission sat heavy in the room, and Mara felt her chest ache on Priya's behalf, the grief of realizing, years too late, that a warning had been offered and missed.

Mara sat down beside her, not touching the folder yet, giving Priya the choice of when to open it further. "What was he like. Before."

Something in Priya's face softened, just slightly, a tenderness that came from being allowed to remember someone as a person instead of only as a case. "Careful. Too careful, honestly. He was the kind of Finder who apologized to objects before he touched them, like he thought they might mind." She almost smiled. "He used to say the gift was a conversation, not a tool. That you had to ask permission from whatever you were reading, or you'd take more than the object was willing to give."

"That doesn't sound like someone who'd disappear without telling you why."

"No. It doesn't." Priya opened the folder at last, sliding a single photograph across the table, a young man with Priya's same steady eyes, caught mid-laugh at what looked like a summer barbecue, ordinary and unguarded in a way that made the four years since feel suddenly, physically heavier in the room. "Which is exactly why I think somebody found a way to make him do something his own conscience would never have agreed to on its own. And I think whoever that was is still walking around this town letting everyone assume my brother just got tired of us."

Mara picked up the photograph carefully, studying the easy grin, the arm slung around what looked like a much younger Priya's shoulders, both of them caught mid-laugh at something outside the frame. It was such an ordinary picture, the kind that sat in a hundred family albums across this valley, and Mara felt an ache comparing this ordinary photograph against the extraordinary theory it now had to support.

"How old was he here?"

"Twenty-two. Right before things started going strange." Priya's voice had gone quieter, more careful, a woman handling a memory the way you'd handle something fragile enough to break under too much direct attention. "He used to fix my bike chain every summer without being asked. Just showed up with a tool kit like it was his job. I didn't understand until years later that he probably knew things about that bike chain no ordinary mechanic would have known, that he was probably reading the whole history of it every single time, and never once made me feel strange for needing his help."

Caleb looked at the photograph for a long moment, something working behind his eyes. "The barn. Wren said she felt something happening to me there, more than once. If it's the same person doing this to both of us, that's not a coincidence. That's a method. Somebody who's found more than one person in this valley willing to bend, or scared enough to be bent."

"Kell." Priya said the name flatly, no longer bothering to soften it with a maybe. "I've thought it for years and never had anything to hang the thought on besides how careful he's always been never to actually deny anything directly when people ask him hard questions."

"We don't have proof yet," Mara said, though she felt the shape of the same suspicion settling into her own chest, a theory heavy enough that it explained too much to be comfortable. "We have a pattern. A missing Finder. A stolen fiance. A weather system in this valley that's behaved strangely for longer than anyone's willing to say out loud. That's not proof. But it's enough to start asking better questions than we have been."

Priya wiped at her eyes once, quick and businesslike, the deputy reasserting herself over the sister with visible effort. "Then let's start asking them. Carefully. I already lost four years assuming things would sort themselves out if I just waited long enough. I'm not doing that again."

Outside, the wind had picked up without warning, rattling the kitchen window in its frame hard enough that all three of them glanced toward it at once, an old habit none of them had needed a week ago and all of them now shared without discussion.

"That's not normal weather," Caleb said quietly.

"No," Mara agreed. "I don't think anything in this valley has been normal weather for longer than any of us realized."

Priya gathered the photograph back into the folder with careful hands, the earlier rawness folding itself back down into something more controlled, more usable, the deputy's composure a coat she was visibly putting back on one sleeve at a time. "I want to start with the last place anyone actually saw him. Not the last phone call. The last time a real person in this town looked at my brother's face and didn't think anything of it because nothing seemed wrong yet."

"Do you know where that was?"

"The feed store. Yusuf's place, actually, before Yusuf took it over from his father. Dev used to help unload deliveries some weekends for extra cash." Priya's jaw set. "I never asked Yusuf directly if he remembered anything from that last summer. I think some part of me was afraid of what asking might turn up, if I let myself actually look instead of just filing a report and waiting."

"Then we ask him today," Mara said. "Together. No more waiting on a version of this that turns out to be easier."

Caleb had been quiet through most of this, working something over, and now he spoke slowly, choosing each word with the same care Mara had learned meant he'd thought it through twice before letting it out. "I keep thinking about the two years I lost. Nobody in this town warned me either, not really. Odette hedged. My aunt cried and blamed allergies. Everyone knew enough to be careful around me and not enough of them thought I deserved to know why." His jaw tightened. "If Dev found something out that scared people enough to help hide him, that's the same pattern. This whole valley's better at managing its own fear than it is at protecting the people that fear is actually about."

"That's not comforting," Priya said.

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to tell us we're not chasing something new. We're chasing the same thing that's been running underneath this whole town for longer than any of us have been alive to notice it." Caleb's hand found Toll's head as the dog settled against his leg, an absent, grounding gesture. "Which means whoever's been managing it has had a lot of practice making sure nobody connects the dots. We need to be smarter about connecting them than they're used to people being."

Mara turned that over, feeling old inspection instincts sharpen underneath the fear, the same clarity that came from treating a mystery like a structural problem instead of an emotional one. "Then we treat this like any investigation. We don't chase feelings. We chase what's documented, and where the documentation goes conveniently quiet, that's where we look hardest."

"Careful," Priya said, something almost like a smile ghosting across her tired face. "You're starting to sound like a deputy."

"I've spent a career finding the load-bearing lie in a structure everyone else trusted because it hadn't fallen down yet. This isn't so different." Mara meant it as reassurance, and heard, saying it, how much steadier it made her feel too, the comfort of a trusted skill applied to a fear she didn't yet know how to hold any other way.

Priya nodded, closing the folder, and for a moment the kitchen held only the sound of the wind outside and Toll's steady breathing from her spot by the door, three people bracing themselves, in their different ways, for a morning that was about to make an old grief considerably harder to keep filed away as simple absence.

She stood, gathering her keys from the counter, and paused halfway to the door, looking back at the two of them with something unguarded crossing her face for just a moment. "Thank you. Both of you. I know this isn't your fight the way it's mine. You could have kept your distance from Dev's part of this and only chased down what's happened to Caleb directly."

"It's the same fight," Caleb said simply. "It was always going to be the same fight the moment we understood how this town actually works."

"Still. Thank you." Priya's voice had gone rough. "Four years is a long time to carry something alone. I didn't realize how heavy it had gotten until I watched the two of you agree to help carry it without me even having to ask properly."

Mara stood too, resting a hand briefly on Priya's shoulder, the same steadying gesture she'd offered Wren days before, the language this summer had taught all of them for saying the things that didn't fit neatly into words. "You don't have to carry any of it alone anymore. Not the finding, and not whatever we find once we get there."
