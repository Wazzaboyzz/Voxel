# CHAPTER THIRTY-FIVE

Mara threw herself into work the way she'd learned to do years ago in Chicago, back when a bad breakup or a worse client meeting needed somewhere to go besides sitting quietly in her own chest. She spent the first two days after the office conversation cataloguing every scrap of her grandfather's file a second time, cross-referencing dates and names with a thoroughness that had less to do with the case and more to do with keeping her hands too busy to reach for her phone, and told herself the distinction didn't matter as long as the work got done.

It was Priya who finally called her on it, showing up at the house on the third evening with a bag of takeout and an expression that said she wasn't leaving until Mara ate something that hadn't come out of a vending machine.

"You've read that file eleven times," Priya said, setting the containers down on the kitchen table without ceremony. "I've been counting. It's not getting any more honest on the twelfth pass."

"I'm being thorough."

"You're avoiding your own porch." Priya pulled out a chair and sat, unbothered by Mara's silence, the particular patience of someone who'd known her long enough to wait out a stubborn mood rather than fight it head on. "How are you actually doing. Not the version you'd give the county board."

Mara set down the folder she'd been holding, feeling, for the first time in three days, the actual weight of the question rather than the busy work she'd built specifically to avoid it. "I keep replaying the conversation. Looking for the moment I could have said something different and changed how it ended. There isn't one. I've checked from every angle I can think of."

"Because there isn't one," Priya said gently. "Some things don't have a better version, Mara. They just have the version that happened, and the job afterward is living through it honestly instead of hunting for the edit that would have made it hurt less."

"That's very wise for someone eating potstickers out of the container."

"I contain multitudes." Priya slid the container closer, an unmistakable instruction, and Mara found herself, despite everything, managing something close to a real laugh, small and short but real, the first one in three days. "Eat. Then tell me honestly whether you think he's coming back, or whether you're just hoping hard enough that you haven't let yourself actually consider the alternative."

Mara turned the question over, chopsticks forgotten in her hand. "I think he will. Not because I need him to, though God knows I do. Because I watched him choose honesty over comfort in that office, even though it cost him everything easier to just tell me what I wanted to hear. A man who does that when it's hardest isn't a man who disappears. He's a man working something out the only way he knows how, carefully, so he doesn't hand me a version of certainty he can't actually stand behind."

"That's either very healthy or you've read an alarming number of self-help books since you moved back," Priya said, though her voice had gone warmer, something like relief moving through it. "For what it's worth, I think you're right. I've known that man his whole life, memory or no memory. He doesn't run from hard things. He goes quiet and works them out alone first, and it drives everyone who loves him a little crazy, but he always comes back with an answer he actually means."

"I keep thinking about what he said, though," Mara admitted, finally picking up her chopsticks, more out of obligation to Priya's patience than actual hunger. "About not knowing what's actually his and what's been handed to him. I understand it, intellectually. I even respect it. But some small, unreasonable part of me keeps wanting to argue that trust doesn't have to come from memory at all. That it can just as easily come from choosing someone, day after day, regardless of what you remember choosing before."

"You could tell him that."

"I did, essentially. It didn't fix anything, because it wasn't really an argument he needed to win. It was a fear he needed to sit with until it stopped being loud enough to drown everything else out." Mara finally ate a bite, and found, to her mild surprise, that it actually helped, the ordinary act of eating something warm doing more for her than three days of careful, controlled busywork had managed. "I just have to trust that he'll get there without me pushing him toward it. Which is, it turns out, one of the harder things I've ever had to do."

"Harder than finding out your dead grandfather suspected a hundred-year-old conspiracy?"

"Different kind of hard." Mara managed a real smile this time. "That one at least had evidence I could chase. This one just has waiting."

---

Caleb, for his part, was not handling the distance nearly as gracefully as Mara imagined he was from across town. He worked the orchard from before sunrise until well after the crew had gone home, filling every daylight hour with tasks that needed doing and several that didn't, and found that none of it quieted the argument running on a loop somewhere behind his ribs, the same three in the morning question circling itself no matter how many crates he stacked or hinges he oiled.

He found himself, on the second evening, back in his father's old workshop, the letter still sitting where he'd left it weeks ago, and read it again slowly, deliberately, the way he might reread a contract before signing something that mattered.

*Don't be as slow as I was. Your mother waited eleven years for me to say something simple. Don't make Mara wait that long for something you already know is true.*

He'd believed that letter, once, enough to build a proposal out of it in the crooked apple tree's shadow. He believed it still, and that was, he was slowly coming to understand, exactly the part that frightened him most. It would have been easier, in a strange way, if Kell's lie had simply been false and nothing else, if he could set it down cleanly and walk back to Mara without this lingering unease. What actually frightened him wasn't the possibility that his feelings had been manufactured. It was the possibility that they hadn't been, that they were entirely, uncomplicatedly real, and that he had built an entire understanding of himself on top of a version of trust he'd never actually earned firsthand, only inherited secondhand from people telling him who he used to be.

"You're going to wear a groove in that floor," Odette said from the workshop doorway, startling him, though her voice was gentle rather than sharp. She'd let herself in the way she sometimes did, an old habit from decades of being close enough to this family to skip the formality of knocking. "Your mother used to pace exactly like that when your father was being stubborn about something. It's apparently hereditary."

"I don't remember her doing that."

"No. You wouldn't." Odette said it without cruelty, simple fact, and came to sit on the same overturned crate he'd sat on weeks ago reading his father's letter. "But I do. I've got two years of your life stored up that you don't, Caleb, same as I've got forty years of your father's and your grandmother's before that. That's the thing about this valley nobody tells you when you're young enough to resent it. Memory doesn't have to live in just one person to be real. Sometimes it lives in everybody who loved you enough to hold onto it for you."

Caleb sat with that a long moment, the workshop's dusty quiet settling around them both. "Then tell me something. Something true about me and Mara, from before. Not the big things. Something small enough that nobody would bother lying about it."

Odette considered the request seriously, the way she considered most things Caleb had learned to trust her with over the past two months. "You used to steal apples off her grandfather's trees on purpose, even though you had a whole orchard of your own two rows over, just because she thought it was funny watching you get caught by Thomas every single time. You never once got better at not getting caught. I always assumed that was deliberate."

Something in Caleb's chest loosened, unexpectedly, at the sheer, unglamorous smallness of it, a detail too specific and too silly to have been invented for his benefit. "That does sound like something I'd do."

"It's exactly something you'd do. Some things don't change just because two years went missing in the middle." Odette stood, brushing dust from her knees, and paused at the doorway on her way out. "For what it's worth, I don't think you're broken, Caleb. I think you're a man being extraordinarily careful with something he's terrified of losing twice. That's not the same thing as being unable to trust it. Give yourself the same patience you'd give anybody else working through something this hard."

She left him alone with the quiet after that, the workshop settling back into its usual dusty stillness, and Caleb found himself thinking, for the first time since the office conversation, not about what he didn't have, but about what he did. Two months of choosing Mara, every single day, without a single memory forcing his hand. Two months of watching her be stubborn and honest and frightened and doing the hard thing anyway, exactly as he'd told her in the barn, and finding that watching her be that person had built something in him that felt, the longer he sat with it, less like a borrowed conclusion and more like his own hard-won one.

He thought about calling her that night, and didn't, not because the doubt had returned but because he understood, with a clarity that surprised him, that the version of trust worth having wasn't the version he rushed back to the moment the fear got uncomfortable. It was the version he built slowly, on his own timeline, so that when he did finally reach for her again, it would be because he'd actually arrived somewhere solid, not because the silence had simply become too hard to sit with any longer.

---

They didn't speak for five days, the longest stretch of silence between them since the funeral, and Mara marked the time the way she marked everything difficult, methodically, precisely, refusing to let herself pretend the days weren't passing even as she refused to let herself chase him for a resolution he wasn't ready to offer. On the sixth morning, she found a single ripe apple sitting on her porch step, no note attached, no explanation needed, and understood exactly what it meant without a single word required to say it.

She stood there a long moment, the apple warm from the morning sun in her palm, and thought that some conversations didn't need words at all, only the patient, deliberate choice to show up again in whatever small, honest way a person had left.
