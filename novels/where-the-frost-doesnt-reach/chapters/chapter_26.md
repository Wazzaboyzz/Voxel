# CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX

They left the store a little past nine, the complaint filed, the evidence logged, the machinery of something official finally, finally set in motion, the morning sun warm on the sidewalk, almost like an apology from a town that had spent so long refusing to look directly at any of this, and Mara allowed herself, for the length of the walk back to the truck, to feel something that had been entirely absent from this whole terrible week.

Something close to hope.

It was a strange, unfamiliar sensation after seven weeks of grief and suspicion and careful, exhausting vigilance, and she was almost distrustful of it, the way you'd distrust a beam that suddenly stopped groaning after months of warning creaks, some old instinct insisting that quiet after strain usually meant something had simply shifted its load somewhere you couldn't yet see. She pushed the thought down. She was allowed, she told herself, to have one good morning before whatever came next.

"You're smiling," Caleb said, glancing over at her as they walked, something lighter in his own face than she'd seen there in days.

"I'm allowed to be, for about five more minutes."

"Take it. God knows we've earned five minutes." He bumped her shoulder gently with his, an easy, unthinking gesture that had become, over the past weeks, entirely natural between them, a small physical shorthand neither of them commented on anymore. "Yusuf's got the complaint filed. Dev's got a second technician lined up. For the first time since I found that ring in a drawer, it feels like we're the ones setting the pace instead of just reacting to whatever Kell does next."

Mara let herself enjoy it, the ordinary Tuesday-morning feeling of the town around them, a delivery truck idling outside the hardware store, someone's dog barking twice at nothing in particular, the ordinary hum of a place that had no idea how close it had come, this summer, to losing far more than it understood. She thought of her grandfather, of the note he'd left half-finished, and allowed herself to hope, just for these five minutes, that he might live to see at least the beginning of an ending to what he'd spent decades chasing.

They stopped at the corner where the diner's window faced the square, and for a moment Mara let herself imagine an entirely different version of this morning, one where she and Caleb were simply two people who'd found their way back to each other after eight years apart, sitting down for coffee with nothing more urgent to discuss than what to plant in the spring. She let the image sit for exactly as long as it took to cross the street, and then let it go, gently, the way she'd learned to let go of a lot of things this summer that would have to wait for a version of her life that wasn't currently under active threat.

"What are you thinking about," Caleb asked, catching the shift in her expression.

"Nothing important. Just imagining a version of this morning where the biggest decision either of us had to make was what to order for breakfast." She smiled, a little wistful. "I'll take a rain check on that version. Preferably once we're not actively worried about who's watching us cross the street."

"I'll hold you to that," he said, and something in the easy certainty of it, a man making a small ordinary promise about a small ordinary future, warmed her more than she expected it to.

"I mean it," he added, more seriously. "Once this is over, whenever that actually is. Breakfast. Nothing on fire, nobody watching the street. I want that morning with you, the boring one, and I intend to collect on it."

"I'll hold you to that too," she said, and for one more block the two of them simply walked, unhurried, like any other couple in any other town on any other ordinary Tuesday.

It lasted until her phone buzzed with a text from an unknown number, four words long, sent at 9:14 that morning.

*Careful what you started.*

Mara stopped walking. Caleb noticed immediately, the ease dropping out of his posture the instant he saw her face change, and he was at her shoulder before she'd fully processed what she was looking at, reading the message over her arm.

"Same number as before?" he asked, his voice gone flat and careful.

"I don't know. I never saved the last one." She stared at the screen, the four words sitting there with a calm, almost casual menace that felt, somehow, more frightening than an outright threat would have. "It's not signed. Could be him. Could be somebody working for him. Could be somebody we haven't even considered yet."

"Show Priya." Caleb was already reaching for his own phone. "The second we get back to the truck. If it's tied to the same burner as before, that at least confirms it's connected, even if it doesn't tell us who's actually holding it."

Mara nodded, sliding the phone into her pocket with hands that had gone, without her quite noticing when, faintly unsteady again, the five minutes of hope from a moment ago already feeling like something that had happened to a different, more naive version of herself standing on this exact sidewalk.

"He knows already," she said quietly, as they started walking again, faster now, toward the truck. "The complaint's barely an hour old and he already knows."

"Or he's had someone watching the store since we walked in." Caleb's jaw was tight, his eyes scanning the street in a way she hadn't seen from him since the night at the mill parking lot, an old instinct clearly resurfacing despite two years of not remembering where it had come from. "Either way. However he found out this fast, it means we're not moving as quietly as we thought we were."

They reached the truck in silence, Mara's earlier lightness gone entirely now, replaced by the familiar, cold clarity she'd learned to rely on this summer, a sharp focus that let her set fear aside long enough to actually think. She looked at the message one more time before pocketing the phone, four words that told her almost nothing and everything at once, proof, if she'd needed any, that filing that complaint had done exactly what they'd hoped it would.

It had gotten his attention.

Whether that turned out to be a victory or a mistake was, she understood, watching Caleb pull out onto the road with more speed than the empty street strictly required, no longer entirely up to them to decide.

They found Priya at the station twenty minutes later, and she took the phone from Mara without a word, scrolling through the message twice before setting it down on her desk with the same careful precision she'd used on the first threat, a woman clearly building a private catalogue of exactly how this man operated.

"Same carrier pattern as the last one," she said finally. "Prepaid, purchased out of county, no name attached that traces anywhere useful. I'll put in a request to the carrier for tower data, but don't expect much. Whoever's doing this either knows exactly how to stay untraceable, or they've had a lot of practice."

"Forty years of practice, if it's him," Caleb said.

"If." Priya looked up, something careful in her expression. "I want it to be him too. But I've learned this summer not to assume the obvious answer just because it's the one that lets me stop looking. Could be Kell himself. Could be somebody he's asked to do his dirty work so his own hands stay clean if this ever actually reaches a courtroom. Either way, I want both of you being more careful than you have been. No more solo trips out to weather stations at night. No more walking into that man's house without me or someone else knowing exactly where you are and when to expect you back."

Mara felt the reasonableness of it even through her own restlessness, a sharp frustration at being told to slow down at the exact moment she finally felt like they were gaining ground. "We hear you."

"I mean it, Mara." Priya's voice softened slightly, the deputy giving way, briefly, to the friend underneath it. "I already almost lost my brother to this man's version of caution running out. I'd rather annoy you both with extra precautions than have either of you be the next person this town quietly decides not to talk about."

"What would that look like," Caleb asked, "if he decided to actually escalate instead of just sending texts."

Priya considered the question with the same seriousness she'd give an active case file, no attempt to soften it for their sake. "Honestly? Something that looks like an accident. A car that fails to stop in time, a fall somewhere isolated, nothing anyone could ever pin on him directly. He's had forty years to learn exactly how to make an inconvenient person disappear without ever once looking guilty of it. That's not me trying to scare you worse than you already are. That's me telling you the actual shape of the risk, because I think you'd rather know it than have me soften it."

"I would," Mara said. "Thank you for not softening it."

Caleb's hand found Mara's, steady, and she let herself lean into it for just a moment, the morning's brief hope now tempered into something more careful, more clear-eyed, a resolve that had room in it, finally, for real fear as well as real determination.

"Okay," Mara said. "We'll be careful. But we're not stopping."

"I never said stop." Priya managed a tired, thin smile. "Just don't make me explain to your grandfather's ghost why I let you get yourself hurt three weeks after his funeral."

They left the station a while later with a short list of Priya's rules taped, quite literally, to the inside of the truck's visor, a piece of notebook paper in her neat, deliberate handwriting: check in every two hours after dark, never approach Kell's property alone, keep phones charged and location sharing on at all times. Mara found the list almost funny in its ordinariness, the ridiculous mundanity of fighting a century-old conspiracy with the same precautions a worried mother might tape to a teenager's car dashboard.

"She's not wrong to make us do this," Caleb said, catching her half-smile as he pulled out of the station lot.

"No. She's not." Mara folded the list once more and tucked it into the glove box rather than leaving it visible, an old habit of not advertising exactly how careful they were being. "It's just strange, having somebody worry about us this specifically. I spent eight years in a city where nobody knew my schedule well enough to notice if I went quiet for a day."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was. I just didn't let myself call it that at the time." She looked out at the town sliding past the window, familiar storefronts she'd walked past a hundred times as a teenager, and felt the strange doubled sensation again, danger and homecoming occupying the same ordinary Tuesday morning without either canceling the other out. "I think that's part of why coming back here scares me as much as it does. Not just Kell. The fact that people here actually notice when something's wrong. I'd forgotten what that felt like."

Caleb reached over and found her hand at a stop sign, brief but certain. "Get used to it. I don't plan on stopping noticing, whatever happens with any of this."

Mara let the words settle into her, warm despite the morning's fear still sitting close under her ribs, and thought that whatever Kell had meant by his four careful, threatening words, he'd underestimated exactly how much this valley, for all its buried secrets, had already started giving her back.
