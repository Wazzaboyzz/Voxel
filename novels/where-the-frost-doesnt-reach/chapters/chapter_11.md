# CHAPTER ELEVEN

Odette left not long after that, walking back across the dark field toward her own house with the particular stiff-backed posture of a woman who had said more than she meant to and needed distance before she could look either of them in the eye again, and Mara watched her go, feeling the strange, unfinished weight of a confession that had answered some questions and opened up several new ones in their place.

"She's not lying," Mara said, once the shape of Odette had disappeared into the tree line. "About any of it. I'd know if she were. I've spent a career learning to tell when somebody's smoothing over a flaw versus actually showing me one."

"I know she's not lying." Caleb sat down heavily on the porch step, elbows on his knees, staring out at the dark orchard rows the way a man stares at something he's trying to make sense of by sheer force of attention. "That's almost the worst part. If she were lying, I could be angry at her cleanly. Instead I just feel like everyone I trust has been quietly managing me for two years, and she had the best reasons in the world for doing it, and I still don't know how to just, be okay with that."

"You don't have to be okay with it tonight." Mara sat down next to him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, the old porch boards creaking familiarly under her weight in a way that felt, despite everything, like coming home to a version of herself she'd left behind eight years ago. "You're allowed to be angry at people for the right reasons doing the wrong thing to you. Those aren't mutually exclusive, whatever this town seems to have decided about it."

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh, though nothing about his face suggested actual humor. "You sound like you've had practice being angry at people for the right reasons."

"I left this town angry at you for eight years, convinced you'd simply stopped wanting me, and it turns out the actual truth is stranger and sadder than anything I imagined, and I still haven't fully worked out how to feel about that yet either." She looked out at the same dark rows he'd been staring at, the trees standing in their ordered lines like sentries, patient and unknowing of everything that had happened underneath their branches over the last two years. "Maybe that's just what this valley does to people. Gives everyone good enough reasons to hurt each other quietly that nobody ever has to feel like the villain."

Toll shifted against Caleb's boot, resettling with a low, contented groan, entirely unbothered by the weight of the conversation happening above her, and something in the simple, animal ordinariness of it eased a fraction of the tension sitting in Mara's chest.

"I keep waiting to feel something specific," Caleb said, looking out at the dark rows of trees instead of at her, his voice dropping into something quieter, more exposed than the anger from a few minutes ago. "Everybody keeps telling me what I used to feel. About this place. About you. And I believe them, I have no reason not to, but it's like being handed a photograph of somebody else's life and being told, this was yours, you should recognize it." He turned his head slightly, and in the dim porch light Mara could see something raw and unguarded in his expression that hadn't been there even an hour ago. "Except with you it's different. I don't recognize the photograph. But something about standing this close to you feels like coming inside after being cold longer than I noticed."

Mara didn't trust herself to look at him directly, so she looked at her own hands instead, folded in her lap, steady in a way the rest of her didn't currently feel. "That's a very specific thing to say to someone you met two days ago."

"I've had time to think about the wording." A small, unexpected echo of Odette's own line from the funeral, and Mara wondered if that was just how people talked in this town, or if some habits ran deeper in a family than memory did, passed down through blood the way a jaw shape or a laugh might be, immune to whatever a toll could take. "I'm not asking you for anything, Mara. I don't know what's fair to ask for, given everything. I just wanted to say it before I lost my nerve, because apparently losing things is something I'm unusually good at right now."

She let out a breath that was almost a laugh, more relief than humor, the tension in her chest easing another fraction. "That's a terrible joke."

"I've been told my sense of humor survived better than most things." His hand, resting on the step between them, moved a half inch closer to hers. Didn't touch. Just closed the distance enough that she could feel the warmth of it radiating against her skin. "For what it's worth. Whatever this is. I'd like to find out what it turns into, instead of somebody else deciding that for us again."

Mara thought about the ring in her grandmother's desk drawer, fourteen months old, engraved with initials that belonged to two people standing on this exact porch and yet somehow not, a version of Caleb she'd never gotten to meet asking a version of her he barely remembered a second time, under circumstances neither of them had chosen and neither of them could fully undo. There was something almost unbearably strange about sitting here now, at the actual beginning of whatever this was turning into a second time, knowing an entire unwitnessed chapter of it had already happened once and vanished, leaving behind only a ring and a scar she couldn't see and questions that might never fully resolve.

"I keep thinking about the version of you I never got to meet," she said quietly. "The one who bought that ring. Who asked me again, under some tree I haven't found yet, and meant it enough to go looking for a ring in the first place." She looked at him now, letting herself actually study his face in the dim light, the small scar through his eyebrow that hadn't been there eight years ago, the new lines at the corners of his eyes that came from two years of squinting into a life that hadn't quite made sense to him. "I don't know if it's strange or comforting that I'll never get to meet him. Some days it feels like grief. Tonight it just feels like you're already familiar in a way that has nothing to do with him at all."

"Maybe that's the part that matters," Caleb said, his voice low. "Not whether I'm the same man who did whatever he did during those two years. Whether whoever I am right now is somebody worth choosing anyway."

Mara looked at his hand, close enough to hers that the space between them felt deliberate, charged with a significance neither of them was naming out loud, and thought about eight years of believing this man had simply stopped wanting her, and the ring in her grandmother's drawer, and the two years he'd lost without ever getting to choose what he lost them to, and the particular, fragile courage it must have taken him to say any of this tonight, on a porch, after everything they'd both just learned.

She moved her hand the last half inch and let it rest against his.

Neither of them said anything else. The moths kept circling the porch light, and somewhere out past the orchard rows, in a house Mara had not yet learned to think of as dangerous, Ambrose Kell was, at that exact hour, sitting at his own kitchen table with a ledger that had nothing to do with money in it, doing an inventory of his own, his pen moving steadily across a page neither Mara nor Caleb would see for another two weeks, by which point the numbers written there would already have changed the shape of everything they thought they understood about how much time was left.
