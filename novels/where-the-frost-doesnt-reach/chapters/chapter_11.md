# CHAPTER ELEVEN

Odette left not long after that, walking back across the dark field toward her own house with a stiff-backed, hurried posture, a woman who had said more than she meant to and needed distance before she could look either of them in the eye again, and Mara watched her go, feeling the strange, unfinished weight of a confession that had answered some questions and opened up several new ones in their place.

"She's not lying," Mara said, once the shape of Odette had disappeared into the tree line. "About any of it. I'd know if she were. I've spent a career learning to tell when somebody's smoothing over a flaw versus actually showing me one."

"I know she's not lying." Caleb sat down heavily on the porch step, elbows on his knees, staring out at the dark orchard rows the way a man stares at something he's trying to make sense of by sheer force of attention. "That's almost the worst part. If she were lying, I could be angry at her cleanly. Instead I just feel like everyone I trust has been quietly managing me for two years, and she had the best reasons in the world for doing it, and I still don't know how to just, be okay with that."

"You don't have to be okay with it tonight." Mara sat down next to him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, the old porch boards creaking familiarly under her weight, the sound itself, despite everything, like coming home to a version of herself she'd left behind eight years ago. "You're allowed to be angry at people for the right reasons doing the wrong thing to you. Those aren't mutually exclusive, whatever this town seems to have decided about it."

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh, though nothing about his face suggested actual humor. "You sound like you've had practice being angry at people for the right reasons."

"I left this town angry at you for eight years, convinced you'd simply stopped wanting me, and it turns out the actual truth is stranger and sadder than anything I imagined, and I still haven't fully worked out how to feel about that yet either." She looked out at the same dark rows he'd been staring at, the trees standing in their ordered lines like sentries, patient and unknowing of everything that had happened underneath their branches over the last two years. "Maybe that's just what this valley does to people. Gives everyone good enough reasons to hurt each other quietly that nobody ever has to feel like the villain."

Toll shifted against Caleb's boot, resettling with a low, contented groan, entirely unbothered by the weight of the conversation happening above her, and the simple, animal ordinariness of it eased a fraction of the tension sitting in Mara's chest.

"I keep waiting to feel something specific," Caleb said, looking out at the dark rows of trees instead of at her, his voice dropping into something quieter, more exposed than the anger from a few minutes ago. "Everybody keeps telling me what I used to feel. About this place. About you. And I believe them, I have no reason not to, but it's like being handed a photograph of somebody else's life and being told, this was yours, you should recognize it." He turned his head slightly, and in the dim porch light Mara could see raw, unguarded feeling written plainly across his face, something that hadn't been there even an hour ago. "Except with you it's different. I don't recognize the photograph. But standing this close to you feels like coming inside after being cold longer than I noticed."

Mara didn't trust herself to look at him directly, so she looked at her own hands instead, folded in her lap, steady in a way the rest of her didn't currently feel. "That's a very specific thing to say to someone you met two days ago."

"I've had time to think about the wording." A small, unexpected echo of Odette's own line from the funeral, and Mara wondered if that was just how people talked in this town, or if some habits ran deeper in a family than memory did, passed down through blood the way a jaw shape or a laugh might be, immune to whatever a toll could take. "I'm not asking you for anything, Mara. I don't know what's fair to ask for, given everything. I just wanted to say it before I lost my nerve, because apparently losing things is something I'm unusually good at right now."

She let out a breath that was almost a laugh, more relief than humor, the tension in her chest easing another fraction. "That's a terrible joke."

"I've been told my sense of humor survived better than most things." His hand, resting on the step between them, moved a half inch closer to hers. Didn't touch. Just closed the distance enough that she could feel the warmth of it radiating against her skin. "For what it's worth. Whatever this is. I'd like to find out what it turns into, instead of somebody else deciding that for us again."

Mara thought about the ring in her grandmother's desk drawer, fourteen months old, engraved with initials that belonged to two people standing on this exact porch and yet somehow not, a version of Caleb she'd never gotten to meet asking a version of her he barely remembered a second time, under circumstances neither of them had chosen and neither of them could fully undo. There was something almost unbearably strange about sitting here now, at the actual beginning of whatever this was turning into a second time, knowing an entire unwitnessed chapter of it had already happened once and vanished, leaving behind only a ring and a scar she couldn't see and questions that might never fully resolve.

"I keep thinking about the version of you I never got to meet," she said quietly. "The one who bought that ring. Who asked me again, under some tree I haven't found yet, and meant it enough to go looking for a ring in the first place." She looked at him now, letting herself actually study his face in the dim light, the small scar through his eyebrow that hadn't been there eight years ago, the new lines at the corners of his eyes that came from two years of squinting into a life that hadn't quite made sense to him. "I don't know if it's strange or comforting that I'll never get to meet him. Some days it feels like grief. Tonight it just feels like you're already familiar on your own terms, nothing to do with him at all."

"Maybe that's the part that matters," Caleb said, his voice low. "Not whether I'm the same man who did whatever he did during those two years. Whether whoever I am right now is somebody worth choosing anyway."

"I think you are," Mara said, before she could talk herself out of the directness of it. "I've spent two days measuring you against a memory I'm not even sure was ever entirely accurate to begin with, and I keep coming up with the same answer regardless. It isn't about matching some version of you I lost. It's that the man sitting on this step right now, the one who just watched his aunt confess to two years of careful lying and still found room in himself to forgive her before the porch light finished flickering, that man is somebody I'd choose even if I'd never met him before this summer at all."

Caleb went very still at that, something moving behind his eyes that looked almost like disbelief, the look of a man being handed something he hadn't let himself hope for. "You don't have to say that just because of everything that happened tonight."

"I'm not saying it because of tonight. Tonight just finally gave me the nerve to say it out loud." Mara felt her own pulse loud in her ears, the raw vulnerability of handing someone an honest thing with no guarantee of how they'd receive it. "I've been circling this since the fence line, Caleb. Pretending it was just grief, or nostalgia, or the strange gravity of being back somewhere familiar. It isn't. It's you. Whoever you are now, memory or no memory, ring or no ring."

He looked at his own hand, still a half inch from hers on the worn wood of the step, and closed the distance the rest of the way himself this time, his fingers finding hers with a certainty that hadn't been there a minute ago. "I don't know how to be careful about this anymore," he admitted. "I've spent three months being careful about everything, my own name, my own history, what I'm allowed to trust about myself. I don't want to be careful about this too."

"Then don't be." Mara turned her hand over, lacing her fingers properly through his, the porch boards creaking softly as she shifted closer. "I've had eight years of careful. I'm tired of it. I'd rather have this, whatever shape it ends up taking, than spend one more summer being cautious with something that might actually matter."

They sat like that a while, neither of them in any hurry to fill the quiet with more words, and Mara found the silence itself doing a kind of work the conversation couldn't, settling something between them that felt sturdier than anything either of them had managed to say out loud. Toll shifted again at their feet, a low, contented sigh escaping her, and somewhere out past the treeline an owl called once, unhurried, indifferent to the two people finally admitting something to each other on a porch that had witnessed a version of this exact moment once before, unremembered now by at least one of them.

"Can I ask you something," Caleb said eventually, his thumb tracing an absent line across her knuckles. "And you don't have to answer if it's too much."

"Ask."

"Do you think he loved you. The version of me you knew before." His voice had gone careful again, though not the guarded caution from earlier, something gentler, more curious than defensive. "I know it's a strange thing to ask about a man neither of us can actually produce evidence for anymore. I just find myself wanting to know if I'm living up to something, or starting from somewhere close to scratch."

Mara considered the question seriously, turning it over the way she might turn over an old structural drawing, looking for what still held true and what time had simply worn away. "I think he did. I think he loved me in the ordinary, unglamorous way people love each other when they've stopped performing it for anyone, when it's just Tuesday breakfasts and arguments about crop rotation and a ring bought without ceremony because the ceremony wasn't really the point." She looked at him, steady now, no longer needing to look away. "But I don't think that's a standard you're being measured against, Caleb. I think it's just history. What you're building right now, tonight, on this step, that's not an echo of him. It's its own thing entirely, and I'd rather have it than the memory of anything that came before."

"That's a generous way to let me off the hook."

"It's not generosity. It's just true." She squeezed his hand once, gently. "I spent eight years grieving a version of you I thought had rejected me. I'm not interested in spending the next eight comparing you to a version I never actually said goodbye to. I'd rather just be here, with you, as you actually are."

Neither of them said anything else for a long while. The moths kept circling the porch light, drawn in slow, dizzy loops around the yellow glow, and somewhere out past the orchard rows, in a house Mara had not yet learned to think of as dangerous, Ambrose Kell was, at that exact hour, sitting at his own kitchen table with a ledger that had nothing to do with money in it, doing an inventory of his own, his pen moving steadily across a page neither Mara nor Caleb would see for another two weeks, by which point the numbers written there would already have changed the shape of everything they thought they understood about how much time was left.

For now, on this porch, none of that had reached them yet. There was only the warmth of his hand in hers, the dog's steady breathing at their feet, and the unhurried quiet that settles over two people who have just told each other something true and are still learning how to sit inside it together.
