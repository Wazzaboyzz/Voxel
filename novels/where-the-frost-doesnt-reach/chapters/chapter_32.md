# CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO

The idea came from Odette, three days into the inquiry's slow crawl through preliminary review, delivered over the store counter with the particular briskness she used when she'd already decided something and was only informing them of it rather than asking permission.

"A paragraph in a county minutes book doesn't move anybody," she said, ringing up a bag of feed for a customer with one hand while gesturing at Mara with the other, entirely unbothered by the multitasking. "What moves people is somebody they trust standing on their porch telling them the truth to their face. You want this town to stop believing Kell's version, you don't wait for the board. You go door to door and you give people the chance to hear it from somebody who isn't a stranger."

"That's a lot of doors," Priya said, skeptical, though Mara could already see her turning the idea over the way she turned over every plan, looking for where it might break.

"Then we'd better start walking." Odette closed the register with a decisive click. "I'll make you a list. Households who've known your grandfather forty years, households who've had their own quiet run-ins with Kell they never talked about publicly, households who just deserve better than gossip deciding what they believe about their neighbors."

They started the next morning, the five of them splitting into pairs, Mara and Caleb taking the west side of town along the river road, Priya and Yusuf covering the houses nearer the council offices where Kell's reputation ran deepest and the conversations would be hardest, Dev and Odette handling the farms further out where most families had known both the Voss and Frey names for generations and might listen, if nothing else, out of old loyalty.

The first house on Mara's list belonged to a retired schoolteacher named Constance Aldridge, who answered the door in a cardigan despite the warm morning and looked at Mara for a long moment before saying, "Thomas Voss's granddaughter. I wondered when you'd come around to see me properly instead of just at the funeral."

"I'm sorry it took this long," Mara said, and meant it, standing on a porch that smelled faintly of lavender and old wood polish.

"Don't apologize. Grief takes what it takes." Constance held the door open wider, an invitation. "Come in. Tell me what you actually came to tell me, and don't dress it up for my comfort. I taught fifth grade in this town for thirty-one years. I've heard worse than whatever you're about to say."

What followed was, Mara would think later, one of the stranger and more moving hours of the whole summer, sitting in a retired teacher's front room with photographs of forty years of former students on every wall, laying out the whole case, Whitlock and the tolls and the amplifier and her grandfather's quiet, careful suspicion, while Constance listened without once interrupting, her hands folded in her lap, her face giving away nothing until Mara finally finished.

"I always wondered about my brother," Constance said quietly, once the silence had stretched long enough that Mara wasn't sure she meant to say anything at all. "He died young. Fifty-one. The doctors called it his heart, and maybe it was, but he'd been Ambrose Kell's closest friend for years before that, always doing him one favor or another, always looking older than his age for reasons nobody could quite explain." She looked at Mara steadily. "I'll sign whatever statement you need. It's long past time somebody asked the right questions about this town's quiet, convenient deaths."

They left Constance Aldridge's house with a signed statement and, more valuable than that, Mara thought, the particular fortification of having been believed by someone with no obligation to believe them at all.

The rest of the day went less smoothly. Two households turned them away outright, one man simply closing the door before Caleb finished his second sentence, another woman listening politely and then telling them, with real regret in her voice, that she couldn't afford to be on the wrong side of Ambrose Kell if this all came to nothing, not with her husband's business depending on a county contract Kell's office still oversaw. Mara understood the fear even as it frustrated her, recognized in it the exact calculation her grandfather must have run a hundred times over forty years, weighing the cost of speaking against the cost of staying silent, and coming up, again and again, on the side that kept his family fed.

"It's not cowardice," she said to Caleb, once they were back in the truck, the afternoon light slanting gold through the windshield. "I keep wanting to be angry at people for not signing. But it's not cowardice. It's just math most of them have been doing their whole lives without ever being allowed to see the real numbers."

"Doesn't make it less frustrating," Caleb said, though his voice held more understanding than complaint. "But you're right. We're not the ones who've had to live under this particular roof for forty years wondering what happens if we say the wrong thing to the wrong person."

By the fourth day, word had clearly started to travel ahead of them, some houses greeting them with wary curiosity, having already heard from a neighbor what this was about, others meeting them with a door already half closed before Mara had said a single word, Kell's version of events evidently still carrying real weight in some corners of the valley. But the signatures accumulated anyway, one household at a time, statements and old suspicions and quiet, decades-old grievances finally finding somewhere to land, a slow, patient counterweight building against forty years of careful, cultivated goodwill.

That evening, gathered back at the orchard house comparing notes, Wren showed up uninvited again, this time with a stack of her mother's old church bulletins under one arm, insisting she'd found something worth adding to the pile.

"My mom kept every single one going back to when she was a girl," Wren said, spreading them across the kitchen table with the particular pride of someone who'd done real, independent work and wanted it recognized. "There's a section every year, prayers for the sick, and I started noticing a pattern once I actually looked for it. Certain names show up asking for prayer, get better fast, disappear from the list within a month or two, and it's always right around the same time somebody young and healthy in this town dies of something nobody quite explains."

Mara looked at the bulletins spread across the table, decades of small, ordinary church announcements that had apparently been quietly documenting Kell's pattern all along, hidden in plain sight in exactly the kind of paper nobody ever thought to treat as evidence.

"That's good work," Priya said, and Mara watched something in Wren visibly straighten at the praise, a girl who had spent most of this summer being protected finally getting to be, unambiguously, useful. "That's really good work, Wren."

"I told you I wanted to help on purpose," Wren said, and there was no bitterness in it, only a quiet, satisfied pride, the sound of someone finally getting to prove something to herself as much as to anyone else in the room.

They worked past midnight cataloguing what they had, the kitchen table gradually disappearing under statements and bulletins and printouts, Toll asleep under it all, entirely unbothered by the late hour or the weight of what was slowly accumulating above her, and Mara found herself, somewhere near one in the morning, looking around at all of them, Caleb across the table sorting papers, Priya on the phone confirming another household for tomorrow, Dev double-checking dates against county records, Wren carefully labeling each bulletin with the year, and thought that whatever else this summer had cost her, it had also given her something she hadn't had in eight years away from this valley.

People who would still be standing beside her in the morning.
