# CHAPTER FIFTEEN

Yusuf remembered more than Priya expected, and less than either of them wanted.

"Last delivery day of that August," he said, standing in the same back room where he'd eventually file the amplifier complaint months later, though none of them knew that yet, arms crossed against shelves of feed bags that had been there, in some form, since before his father ran the place. "Dev helped me unload two trucks that morning. Then Ambrose Kell came by around noon, which wasn't unusual, he used to stop in most weeks to talk council business with my father. What was unusual was that Dev went pale the second Kell walked in, and found a reason to be in the back for the rest of the visit."

"Pale how?" Priya asked, her voice carefully level, the deputy's neutrality doing its best to hold against something Mara could see costing her more with every question.

"Like a man who'd seen something he didn't want seen seeing him." Yusuf's jaw tightened. "I didn't think much of it at the time. Kell made a lot of people nervous. Still does. I chalked it up to Dev being young and easily rattled by anyone with that much county influence." He looked at Priya directly, something apologetic in his weathered face. "I should have asked. I've thought about that morning more than once over the years, wondering if I missed something that mattered."

They left the feed store with little more than a confirmed unease, and Priya drove them out past the old family property afterward, a modest house on the edge of town that had sat empty since their mother passed, Dev's room preserved almost exactly as he'd left it, the particular museum stillness of a space nobody had the heart to change and nobody quite dared to disturb.

"I haven't been in here in two years," Priya admitted, standing in the doorway like the threshold itself required permission. "Every time I thought about clearing it out, it felt like admitting he wasn't coming back to need it."

Mara stayed near the door, giving Priya the room to move through her own memory at whatever pace it required, while Caleb crouched by an old bookshelf, running a careful finger along spines gone soft with dust. The room smelled faintly of cedar and old paper, the particular scent of a space that had been closed up carefully rather than abandoned carelessly, and Mara found herself moved, more than she expected, by the evidence everywhere of a sister who had refused to let this room become simply empty even while refusing to properly enter it herself.

"He liked mysteries," Caleb said, tapping a spine gently. "Every single one of these is a detective novel. Local library sale stickers on half of them."

"He used to say Finders and detectives had the same basic job. Notice what everyone else walked past." Priya managed a small, genuine smile at the memory. "I used to tell him that made me the detective in the family since I actually became one, and he'd say I only got the badge, he already had the instinct built in for free."

"He kept notebooks," Priya said, opening a desk drawer that stuck on its track, the particular reluctant give of wood that had swollen and dried a dozen summers without anyone oiling it. "Field notes on his gift, I always assumed, though he never let me read them. Said some things a Finder learns aren't meant for people who don't carry the gift themselves, that it wasn't secrecy so much as translation, that some of it just didn't survive being explained to someone who couldn't feel what he felt."

She pulled out a slim, water-stained notebook, flipping through pages of cramped, careful handwriting, diagrams of objects with notes beside them, dates, brief descriptions Mara couldn't fully parse from across the room.

"Here." Priya's voice had gone tight. "Last entry. Dated the week before that final phone call."

She read it aloud, slow, like she was translating a language she'd once known better than she did now. *Kell asked me to look at something today. Told me it was a family matter, personal, nothing official. It wasn't personal. I don't know what I touched, but it felt like putting my hand into something that's been open and bleeding for a hundred years, and he watched my face the whole time like he already knew exactly what I'd find and was checking to see how much I'd figured out on my own.*

"That's not the entry of a man planning to disappear because he needs space," Caleb said quietly.

"No." Priya closed the notebook with careful hands, holding it against her chest for a moment before setting it down on the desk, unwilling, Mara understood, to fully let go of it yet. "That's the entry of a man who'd just found out something dangerous and was trying to decide what to do about it before anyone could stop him from doing the right thing."

Mara crossed the room and sat on the edge of the narrow bed, feeling the particular reverence a space like this demanded, a life paused mid-sentence, waiting for an ending nobody had written yet. She noticed, now that she was looking properly, the small details that told her more about the person who'd lived here than any notebook could, a shelf of paperback mysteries with cracked spines, a corkboard still pinned with photographs gone slightly curled at the edges, most of them featuring a much younger Priya in various states of exasperation or delight, one clearly taken at a county fair, both siblings holding up matching ribbons with matching grins.

"You two were close," Mara said, not quite a question.

"We were everything to each other for a while. Our parents worked long hours at the mill before it closed, and it was mostly just us two most afternoons, making our own dinner, doing our own homework, figuring out how to be people together before either of us really knew how." Priya's fingers traced the edge of the corkboard without quite touching the photographs. "He taught me to drive on the old logging roads before I was technically supposed to be driving anywhere. I taught him how to throw a punch properly after some kid at school gave him trouble about being a Finder, called him a freak in front of half the cafeteria. We had that kind of relationship. Whatever the other one was missing, we just filled in without being asked."

"Do you think that's part of why this hurts more than an ordinary missing person case would," Mara asked, gently.

"I think it's the only reason it hurts at all in the specific way it does. I've worked missing person cases as a deputy. I know the shape of that grief professionally, from the outside." Priya's voice cracked, just slightly, before she steadied it again. "This is different. This is the shape of a hole cut directly out of my own life, one I've spent four years pretending had scarred over instead of admitting it never actually closed."

"Do you think he blamed himself," Mara asked, gently. "For whatever Kell showed him."

"I think that's exactly the kind of thing my idiot, wonderful brother would do." Something fractured in Priya's voice, grief and pride tangled too tightly to separate. "He always thought he could fix things quietly, on his own, without dragging anyone else into danger alongside him. I used to tease him about it. Big brother complex, even with a little sister who could out-shoot him at the county fair every single year." She wiped at her eyes, fast, businesslike. "I hope, wherever he is, that instinct didn't get him killed for it."

"We don't know that it did," Mara said, gently but firmly, refusing to let the fear calcify into an assumption before they had cause for one.

"No. We don't." Priya squared her shoulders, tucking the notebook carefully into her jacket, the deputy reasserting herself over the sister once more, though Mara could see now how thin that separation had always been, how much effort it cost to maintain. "But I think this notebook just told us where to look next. He mentioned the old grain silo twice in earlier entries, said it was a good quiet place to work through a difficult reading without interruption. If he went somewhere to think through what Kell showed him, somewhere private enough that nobody would find him accidentally, that's exactly the kind of place he'd choose."

Caleb was already moving toward the door, Toll rising from her spot on the rug to follow. "Then that's where we go next."

Priya paused at the threshold of her brother's room one more time before following, looking back at the careful, undisturbed order of it, four years of grief finally given a direction instead of just an ache.

"Dev," she said quietly, to no one, or to the room itself, or to a brother who might, after everything, still be somewhere within reach of hearing it. "Hold on a little longer. I'm finally coming to find you properly."

Outside, Caleb waited by the cruiser, giving the two of them the last private moment in that doorway without making a show of it, and Mara found herself lingering too, watching Priya lock the front door with the particular care of someone sealing a chapter rather than simply securing a house.

"You don't have to explain any of that back there," Mara said, once they were walking down the front path together. "The room. The notebook. Any of it."

"I know. I think I needed to say some of it out loud anyway. It's been four years since I let myself talk about him like a person instead of a case file." Priya glanced back once at the house, small and quiet behind them. "Thank you for sitting on that bed instead of hovering by the door. It made it feel less like being watched and more like being accompanied."

"That's what I was going for." Mara fell into step beside her. "For what it's worth, he sounds like he was a good brother. The kind who taught you to drive on logging roads and never once acted like your gift was something to hide from him."

"He was." Priya's voice steadied, something almost like resolve settling into it now that the grief had been given its moment and folded carefully back down. "Which is exactly why I refuse to let whatever Kell did to him be the last chapter of his story. Whatever we find at that silo, good or bad, he deserves better than four more years of this town pretending he simply wandered off."

They reached the cruiser, and Caleb held the door for both of them without comment, something steady and unhurried in the gesture that told Mara he understood exactly how much this morning had cost Priya to walk through, and exactly how much it would keep costing her until they found an actual answer waiting at the end of it.

"Silo," Priya said, starting the engine, her voice fully composed again, the deputy back in full command of herself. "Let's go see if my brother left us anything else worth finding."
