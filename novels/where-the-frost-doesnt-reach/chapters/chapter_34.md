# CHAPTER THIRTY-FOUR

Kell gave his first public interview in eleven years the following week, sitting for a feature in the county paper that painted him as a beleaguered public servant facing a smear campaign from a grieving, unstable woman and the fiancé she'd manipulated into doubting his own mind, the photograph accompanying it showing him in a cardigan on his own porch, silver-haired and patient, exactly the image forty years of careful cultivation had been built to produce. Mara had known, distantly, that something like this was coming, Odette had warned them Kell was too practiced a survivor to simply absorb an inquiry without striking back, but knowing a blow was coming and standing under it when it finally landed turned out to be two entirely different experiences.

She read it standing in Odette's store, and by the third paragraph her hands had gone cold around the newspaper, the print blurring slightly at the edges of her vision before she forced herself to focus.

*"Ms. Voss possesses what our community calls a Finder's gift," Kell was quoted as saying, "an ability, among other things, to sense and in rare cases subtly influence a person's emotional attachments to people and places. I don't say this to disparage her. I say it because Mr. Reyes, in his current vulnerable state, deserves to understand exactly what kind of influence he may be under before this town allows itself to be torn apart by accusations built on his compromised judgment."*

It was a lie, a deliberate, surgical lie, Finders had never in any account Mara knew of been able to influence emotion, only locate and read, and Kell had to know that, which meant he'd said it anyway because he understood precisely how much damage a lie could do once it found a crack already forming, precisely where to press to make an old fear feel like new evidence. Mara stood there rereading the paragraph a second time, then a third, some part of her still hoping the words might rearrange themselves into something less calculated on the fourth pass, and they didn't, and eventually Odette gently took the paper out of her hands and set it face down on the counter, as if the physical act of hiding it might undo some of what it had already done.

"He's not just attacking you," Odette said quietly. "He's attacking the one thing you two have that he can't touch with evidence or lawyers. He's attacking whether you're allowed to trust each other at all."

"Has anyone said anything to you," Mara asked, "about believing it? Really believing it, not just repeating it because it's easier than thinking too hard."

Odette hesitated, and that hesitation told Mara more than any answer could have. "A few. Not most. But Ambrose has spent forty years making himself the reasonable voice in this town, and reasonable voices are hard to unlearn trusting all at once, even when they're lying to your face." She reached over and squeezed Mara's hand once more, briefer this time. "Give it time. The people who matter already know better. The rest will catch up once the inquiry actually produces something they can't argue with."

"Time's the one thing I'm not sure we have," Mara said, folding the paper back up, the silver-haired photograph staring up at her one last time before she tucked it out of sight. "Every day this runs unanswered, it settles a little deeper into what people already believe. I've watched that happen with structural rumors on job sites. Once a story takes hold, correcting it costs ten times what preventing it would have."

"Then don't let it sit unanswered," Odette said. "Talk to Caleb before he reads it alone and starts believing the worst version of himself Kell's trying to hand him."

Mara thought that was probably good advice, and found, walking out of the store with the paper still folded under her arm, that she wished, briefly and uselessly, that good advice came with some guarantee it would actually work.

She found Caleb in the orchard office that evening, the newspaper folded under her arm, and something in his face when he saw it told her he'd already read it, the color gone from his cheeks in a way that made her stomach drop before either of them said a word.

"It's not true," she said, before he could say anything at all, the words coming out faster than she'd planned. "Caleb, I need you to know that. Finders locate things. Objects, people, sometimes fragments of memory if we're skilled and careful. We don't influence feelings. It's not in the gift, it's not in the lineage, it's not something my grandfather could do or Dev can do or Wren can do. Kell made that up because he needed a weapon and he knew exactly where your fear already lived."

"I know that. Logically, I know that." Caleb's voice was tight, controlled in a way that told her the control was costing him something, his hands flat on the desk in front of him as though bracing against a surface that might tilt. "But logic isn't really the problem, Mara. The problem is I've spent three months building an entire relationship, an entire understanding of who I am now, on the word of people who I have no independent way of verifying, because the two years where I might have built my own trust with any of you got stolen from me. You could tell me the sky is green and I'd have to decide whether to believe you based on nothing but how you say it, because I don't have my own memory to check it against."

"You're saying you don't trust me."

"I'm saying I don't know how to fully trust anyone right now, and you're the person it costs the most to admit that about." His hands were shaking slightly, and he pressed them flat against the desk like he could hold something steady through sheer physical pressure, the wood creaking faintly under the effort. "Kell's a liar, I believe that, I do. But the fact that a lie like that could even land, the fact that some small ugly part of me heard it and felt a flicker of doubt before I could talk myself out of it, that's not about you. That's about how broken I actually am underneath everything we've built this summer, and I don't think it's fair to you to keep pretending that isn't true."

Mara felt something in her chest crack, quiet and total, that grief of watching someone she loved turn his own wound against the thing that had been healing it, a grief made worse by understanding, completely, why he was doing it. She thought of the porch step just days ago, his hand finding hers, the two of them agreeing to say the three in the morning things out loud instead of letting them fester in silence, and understood, with a bitter kind of clarity, that this was exactly that promise being kept, however much it hurt to watch it play out.

"So what are you saying," she said, careful, bracing, her voice barely above a whisper.

"I'm saying I need some space to figure out what's actually mine in all this, and what's just been handed to me by people I have no way to verify, you included, even though it costs me more than I know how to say to put you in that category." His eyes were wet now, and he didn't look away from her, which somehow made it worse, the honesty of it a kind of cruelty neither of them had chosen. "I'm not saying I don't love you. I think I do, more than I've felt anything since I woke up two years into a life I don't remember building. I'm saying I don't fully trust that feeling right now, and pretending otherwise isn't fair to either of us."

Mara stood very still, the newspaper still folded under her arm, the office suddenly feeling too small and too quiet, and thought of eight years spent believing this man had simply stopped wanting her, and understood, with a bitter, precise clarity, that she was now watching a version of that exact fear come true again, except this time she couldn't even be angry at him for it, because the wound underneath it was real, and Kell had known exactly where to press. She wanted, badly, to argue him out of it, to lay out every piece of evidence for why his instincts about her were trustworthy, and made herself stop, because she recognized, even through the pain of it, that arguing him out of needing space would only prove his fear right, would only confirm that she was the kind of person who pushed past a boundary the moment it became inconvenient to her.

She thought, standing there, of every difficult conversation she'd ever had with a client who wanted a structural problem to simply be argued away rather than actually addressed, the temptation to smooth over a real concern with confident reassurance because reassurance was so much easier to deliver than patience. She had never once respected an engineer who did that, who told a client what they wanted to hear instead of what the building actually needed, and she was not going to become, in the middle of the worst conversation of her adult life, exactly the kind of person she'd built a career refusing to be.

"Okay," she said finally, her voice steadier than she felt, drawing on some reserve she hadn't known she still had. "Take the space. I'm not going to fight you on wanting to know what's actually yours. I just need you to know that whatever you decide, once you've had the time to sort it out, I'm not going anywhere in the meantime. I'm not leaving this town, and I'm not leaving this fight against Kell, and I'm not going to pretend I don't love you just because you need room to figure out if you're allowed to trust it."

Caleb's jaw worked, some fresh wave of feeling crossing his face that he didn't try to name out loud, and for a moment Mara thought he might reach for her anyway, might close the distance the way he had at the fence line, at the barn, on the porch step, and instead he stayed exactly where he was, hands still flat on the desk, the space between them deliberate now instead of accidental. She watched him choose that distance, watched the effort it cost him to hold it, and understood, even through her own grief, that this was not indifference dressed up as caution. It was a man trying, clumsily and honestly, to do the one right thing he could think of in a moment that offered him no good options at all.

"Thank you," he said quietly, "for not making this harder than it already is."

"I'm not going to pretend it isn't hard," Mara said. "For either of us. I just don't see the point in making it uglier than it has to be on top of that."

She left him standing in the office, and it was only once she was back in her car, hands gripping the wheel hard enough to whiten her knuckles, that she let herself cry, finally, for the first time since her grandmother's funeral, eight weeks of held breath finally breaking loose all at once, the sound of it filling the small space of the car until there was nothing else left in her to give it. She sat there a long while, the engine off, the orchard dark and quiet around her, and thought, somewhere in the middle of it, of her grandfather's careful handwriting, the years he'd spent documenting a wrong he could never fully fix alone, and understood that grief this summer had apparently decided to arrive in every possible shape it owned, one after another, without ever quite waiting for the last one to finish.

Eventually she started the engine and drove home slowly, the roads empty, the dark valley rolling past her windows in shapes she'd once known by heart and was now, for the first time in weeks, seeing again as unfamiliar, a town she'd have to learn to navigate carefully now that the one steady thing she'd been orienting herself by had, without either of them wanting it, become uncertain ground. She thought of her grandmother, gone barely three months now, and of the strange loneliness of grieving one loss while another one opened up right alongside it, the way the ground sometimes gave in two directions at once and you simply had to choose, in the moment, which collapse to brace against first.

Her phone buzzed once, near midnight, and she almost didn't look at it, braced by now for bad news in any unexpected sound. It was Priya.

*You okay? Odette told me about the article. Whatever you need, I'm here.*

Mara typed back with hands still faintly unsteady. *Not okay. Will be. Thank you.*

She sat with the phone in her lap a while longer, watching the dark orchard rows through the windshield, and thought that this, too, was its own kind of structural lesson, the ones she'd learned on job sites years ago and was only now learning to apply to herself. A crack named honestly didn't mean the building was finished. It meant the real work of repair could finally start, once everyone stopped pretending the wall was fine. She didn't know, sitting there in the dark, whether Caleb would find his way back to her once the space he needed had done its work. She only knew, with the same stubborn certainty that had carried her through eight years and back across half a country, that she intended to still be standing here, in this valley, in this fight, whenever he did.
